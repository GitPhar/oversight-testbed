# Audit of ng-p3-16: does 3 November's result come back when the circuit runs alone in its own job?

## In plain English

The result stands. Running the 3 November circuit alone in its own job read 0.306, and the same circuit sharing a job a minute earlier read 0.319. That is nowhere near 3 November's 0.502 or the 0.40 bar. I regenerated every number in the write-up from the archived raw files, and the analysis rewrote its own JSON exactly. The fail does not depend on ties, floating point, the confidence-interval method or drift within a job. The claim that running alone made "no difference larger than 0.05" holds under three interval methods, although its lower edge (−0.047) is close to −0.05. Nothing has to be cut. I recommend one optional wording change: a frozen sentence says "This evening", but the jobs ran at 05:17 in the morning, and the write-up already admits this in a footnote. What could still overturn the reading is a run at 3 November's time of day (about 16:00 Denver), or evidence that IBM's servers handled a one-circuit job differently on 3 November. This run can test neither.

## The case that this result is false

The headline is negative: being alone in its job does not bring back 3 November's 0.50, and alone against shared makes no difference larger than 0.05. Here is the strongest case against it.

First, "alone" may not have been the manipulation that mattered. On 3 November the circuit ran in the afternoon (job dbl423dgg49c73dk0db0, 23:06 UTC, which is 16:06 Denver), while A ran at 05:18 in the morning, with IBM's calibration data from the day before. If the 3 November effect needs the one-PUB packaging *and* some state of the chip or server, a morning run cannot show it, and the null on "alone" is a null on "alone, this morning". The sentence "The chip on that afternoon remains the main candidate" then picks one of several untested rivals: time of day, IBM's server software and defaults between 3 and 5 November, the calibration cycle.

Second, the equivalence claim is near its edge. A − CB's 90% CI is −0.047 to +0.021. A slightly different interval, or shots correlated within a job (which would make the binomial SE too small), could push the lower end past −0.05. The "within ±0.05" sentence would then not apply. Job B's shots show a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.08.

Third, the readout could hide ties. `readout.quantum_readout` gives half a "yes" to a shot with magnetisation exactly 0, compared in floating point. That is the founding-week tie artefact class.

Fourth, A may not be "3 November's packaging" at all. The circuit was rebuilt from an archived gate list, not resubmitted as it was, and the submission path could have changed since 3 November.

Fifth, the lone barrier job behaved differently in one observable way that the write-up does not discuss. A's execution span lasted 4.6 s for 1,000 shots. B's lasted 2.2 s, and C's 5.3 s for 2,000 shots. So the server did *something* different with A, which weakens the parenthesis "assuming IBM's servers handled a one-circuit job the same way".

Most of this case fails on checking, as the checklist shows. Ties are impossible: nine equal weights give no zero magnetisation. The interval survives Newcombe and a block bootstrap. The submitted circuits match the 3 November gate list exactly, plus the one barrier, and `run_hardware` has been unchanged since the 3 November job. What survives is the time-of-day and server-state limitation. §6 of the pre-registration and the write-up's own hedges ("not a demonstrated cause"; "assuming IBM's servers …") already state it. The span-length oddity is a lead, not a hole: it does not touch any sentence's truth.

But the mechanism it names, a passing state of the chip on 3 November, is never observed. This test shows only that one-PUB packaging is not the cause on this morning. ## What would break it next

The fail itself is robust: A would need about 94 more "yes" shots in 1,000. What could still overturn the *reading* (that being alone in its job is not what made 3 November different) is that the run differed from 3 November in clock time and server state as well as packaging.

1. **Time-of-day repeat, about 7 QPU s, $0 API (candidate bet).** Run jobs A and C again, unchanged, at 3 November's clock time (about 16:00–16:10 America/Denver; the 3 November job finished 16:06). The scored rule and bars stay as they are, with CB still the in-run control. If A returns ≥ 0.40 while CB stays near 0.30, "alone" matters at that time of day, and today's sentence would need its "this morning" scope made explicit. If both return, the afternoon state is the variable, not the packaging.
2. **Free lead from this archive:** A's lone-job execution span lasted 4.63 s for 1,000 shots. B's lasted 2.19 s, and C's 5.29 s for 2,000 shots. The server handled the two lone jobs differently in time, but P(yes) did not differ (A − B +0.009). A $0 check would be whether 3 November's job metrics (if IBM still serves them, a free read) show a span length like A's or like B's. That would bear on the parenthesis "assuming IBM's servers handled a one-circuit job the same way".
3. **Free, already mapped (P3-17):** 3 November's per-qubit P(1) differs from every later run on several qubits at once (qubit 125: 0.30 against 0.64–0.70 now; qubits 117, 124, 136 and 142 near 0.5). A readout-only refit of the 3 November counts against today's would show whether the 0.502 looks like a readout-assignment shift or a gate-level change. That splits "the chip on that afternoon" into testable parts.
