## Cluster summary

**What is settled.** In typically developing children, the explicit self question ("before you looked, what did you think was in the box?") and the other question ("what will X think is in the box?") follow nearly the same developmental curve, from below chance at 3 years to above chance by 5. Wellman, Cross & Watson (2001) compared 117 self conditions against 118 matched other conditions and found no significant main effect (p = .18). Within a child, the two answers are highly intercorrelated: ToM batteries that mix self and other items reach Cronbach's α = .85 (Bernstein et al. 2007). Three groups (infants, apes and adults) also show that self-experience is projected onto others. Twelve- and 18-month-olds' gaze following flips depending on whether they themselves could see through a blindfold (Meltzoff & Brooks 2008). Great apes' anticipatory looking in a false-belief video depends on whether they themselves found a novel barrier see-through (Kano et al. 2019). Adults' altercentric intrusion in the dot-perspective task appears only when they have themselves found the avatar's goggles transparent (Furlanetto et al. 2016). One's own current knowledge contaminates judgments of a naive other and of one's own naive past self. The two contaminations go together (hindsight bias predicts ToM after controlling for age, language and inhibitory control; Bernstein et al. 2007) and persist into adulthood, though the adult effect is smaller than first reported (Birch & Bloom 2007; Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt 2014: d = 0.20, not 0.47). In autism, self- and other-attribution deficits co-occur, and the review literature reads this as one impaired mechanism (Williams 2010, after Frith & Happé 1999).

**What is contested, and which designs transfer cheaply.** Three findings complicate "one mechanism." (1) Sobel (2023) reanalyses an updated literature (244 conditions, plus 1,231 children from one lab) and finds a small but reproducible age × question interaction. Three-year-olds do better on their own representational change, and four- to five-year-olds do better on the other's false belief. He reads the late self deficit as hindsight-style memory reconstruction, not privileged access. (2) Williams & Happé (2009) show that the self question can be passed by remembering what one *said* rather than what one *thought* (the "memory-for-statement" route). With that route removed (the Plasters task, where the false belief is shown by an action and never voiced), autistic children do worse on self than on other (51.9% vs 69.2%, McNemar p = .02). Even typically developing children drop on self without the statement (McNemar p = .02 against the Smarties self question). This is the closest human analogue to program 1's finding that a model's self-consistency is memory of its own transcript. (3) The implicit and automatic belief-tracking literature (Onishi & Baillargeon 2005; Kovács et al. 2010; Southgate et al. 2007) replicates poorly: Phillips et al. 2015 trace Kovács to attention-check timing; Kulke et al. 2018 find 1 of 4 paradigms replicates and none intercorrelate; ManyBabies 2 toddlers (n = 521) do not separate knowledge from ignorance. Heyes (2014) argues it is "submentalizing." Ape false-belief evidence (Krupenye et al. 2016; Kano et al. 2019) rests on modest samples, and no ape study measures a self-report to compare with. Three designs transfer at almost no cost to a clonable, transcript-editable model:
- **Smarties versus Plasters on matched items.** The model's earlier statement is shown, redacted or planted, and the item asks about its own prior belief or about a naive agent's belief.
- **Birch & Bloom's graded container ratings, crossed with a hindsight self version.** The ratings are already a 0–100 instrument.
- **Self-informed belief induction (the goggles design).** The model's own planted tool experience with a novel occluder decides what it should attribute to an agent behind that occluder, with identical test items across conditions.

The literature's main lesson for us is method, not theory. The self/other differences that matter are small: Wellman's age × question interaction was f ≈ 0.12 by Sobel's calculation, and Sobel's own Monte Carlo detects the question effect under half the time even at N = 200 per age group. Measures differ in whether they can be passed by reading back one's own words. Implicit measures fail on confounds of timing and position. All three problems are cheap to control at API scale.

## Records

### 1. Wimmer & Perner 1983: the original false-belief task
- **Paper:** Wimmer, H., & Perner, J. (1983). Beliefs about beliefs: Representation and constraining function of wrong beliefs in young children's understanding of deception. *Cognition*, 13(1), 103–128. DOI 10.1016/0010-0277(83)90004-5.
- **Read depth:** abstract/secondary only. No open copy found; scilit lists no abstract. Described from Wellman et al. 2001 and Phillips et al. 2015.
- **What was tested:** The "Maxi" change-of-location story. Maxi puts chocolate in cupboard A and leaves; his mother moves it to drawer B; where will Maxi look? There were also deception variants (where would Maxi direct a rival).
- **On whom:** Children aged roughly 3–9 (secondary descriptions). I did not see exact Ns.
- **Result:** Younger children (3–4) answer with the real location; success emerges around 4–6 (secondary). No per-age percentages were visible to me, so none are given.
- **Limits:** Other-only. The paradigm asks about another agent, never about the child's own past belief. Later critics (Bloom & German 2000, cited in Williams & Happé 2009) argue that it conflates belief understanding with reality-suppression demands.
- **Borrow:** The canonical change-of-location item structure, with a control question ("where is it really?") and a memory question ("where did Maxi put it?"). Wellman et al. used the pattern of excluding conditions where fewer than 60% pass controls.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* not visible to me. *For us:* The self analogue of Maxi is a transcript in which the *model* "put" the object (it made a planted tool call placing it in A), after which the environment moved it without the model's observation. Ask (a) "where did you think it was before the update?" (self, past) and (b) "where will Maxi look?" (other), both as 0–100 ratings that the belief is "in A." Transcript editing can make the model's observation of the move present, absent or planted. The judge × author design applies: model B reads model A's transcript and rates A's belief, compared with A rating itself.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers** (as item scaffolding). The structure is trivially renderable as text and pairs with a self version, but the original paper carries no self/other data.

### 2. Gopnik & Astington 1988: representational change versus false belief
- **Paper:** Gopnik, A., & Astington, J. W. (1988). Children's understanding of representational change and its relation to the understanding of false belief and the appearance-reality distinction. *Child Development*, 59(1), 26–37. (DOI not verified.)
- **Read depth:** abstract/secondary. I read the ERIC record (paraphrased) and the descriptions in Sobel 2023, Wellman et al. 2001 and Bernstein et al. 2007. No open full text was found.
- **What was tested:** Children were shown deceptive objects (e.g., a candy box containing pencils, or a sponge that looks like a rock). They were then asked three question types: representational change ("when you first saw this, what did you think it was?"), false belief about another person, and appearance-reality.
- **On whom:** Day-care children aged 3, 4 and 5, in two experiments (ERIC).
- **Result:** Performance on the three question types was intercorrelated, and children "begin to consider alternative representations of the same object at about age four" (ERIC abstract). On direction there is a conflict. Sobel 2023 (footnote 1) reports that Gopnik & Astington found children did *better on the other question than on the self question*, the opposite of Perner, Leekam & Wimmer 1987. Wellman et al. 2001 cite the same paper as showing a "lack of differences between false-belief judgments for self and others," with small samples (16–20). The brief's description ("fail own past false belief at the same age they fail other's") matches the correlation and the Wellman reading. If anything, the original data show self ≤ other. I did not see the percentages.
- **Limits:** *Authors (via secondary):* small samples. *Ours:* In this task the child voices its belief before the reveal, so the self question can be answered by recalling the statement (Williams & Happé 2009; see record 18). The self advantage or parity may partly measure memory for one's own words.
- **Borrow:** The three-question battery on one deceptive object (self-past, other-naive, appearance-reality) gives a within-item self/other contrast. The appearance-reality question is a third, perspective-free control.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors (via Gopnik 1993):* first-person and third-person belief knowledge rest on the same theory, so they should develop together. *For us:* This is the core matched-item design. For each deceptive object, render (i) the model's planted or elicited initial answer, (ii) the reveal, and then (iii) the self-past question, the other-naive question and a reality control, each on 0–100 and in all six formats. Program 1's prediction is self parity with other when the initial answer is shown, and a self collapse when it is redacted. If self stays accurate with the answer redacted, the model is reconstructing from the item, not reading its transcript. Planting a wrong initial answer ("I thought it was candles") tests whether the self report follows the plant at the program-1 own-uptake rates (0.71 / 0.44 / 0.21 at 10 / 30 / 60 points). A plant credited to the other agent ("Sam said candles") tests the other side.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** It is the cleanest matched self/other item in the literature, and transcript editing can remove its known confound.

### 3. Gopnik 1993: "How we know our minds"
- **Paper:** Gopnik, A. (1993). How we know our minds: The illusion of first-person knowledge of intentionality. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 16(1), 1–14 (with commentaries). DOI 10.1017/S0140525X00028636 (from the sciencegate record; not independently verified).
- **Read depth:** abstract/secondary. No open copy was found. I used the abstract as reported in search results and the summaries in Sobel 2023, Williams & Happé 2009 and Wellman et al. 2001.
- **What was tested:** This is a theoretical target article built on developmental data. Its claim is that first-person knowledge of intentional states feels direct but is theory-mediated, in the same way as third-person attribution.
- **On whom:** It reviews studies of 3–5-year-olds.
- **Result:** Per the abstract, many 3-year-olds are consistently wrong in reporting some of their own immediately past psychological states, and they show similar difficulties reporting others'. Gopnik concludes that the felt directness of self-knowledge is an illusion produced by expertise.
- **Limits:** *Critics (BBS commentary, via search summaries):* the data are consistent with direct access plus a memory or reporting failure (Harris's line; see Wellman et al. 2001 and Sobel 2023). *Ours:* the self items reviewed are *past*-state reports. They cannot separate "no access" from "access to the present state contaminating reconstruction of the past" (hindsight).
- **Borrow:** The "illusion of directness" framing, which is almost exactly program 1's reading of model self-report. The prediction that self and other share errors item by item.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Author:* look for matched first- and third-person error patterns. *For us:* Test whether the model's errors on its own past belief correlate *item by item* with its errors on a naive agent's belief. Use the held-out-format prediction score and the one-latent versus format-specific likelihood test, applied jointly to self and other items. If one latent fits both, that is Gopnik's single theory. The judge × author interaction speaks most directly to "illusion of first-person knowledge": program 1's −0.055 interaction says a different model reads Haiku's self-relevant facts at least as well as Haiku does.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers** (as hypothesis). The claim is directly testable with our instruments, and program 1 already supports it for self-report.

### 4. Wellman, Cross & Watson 2001: meta-analysis
- **Paper:** Wellman, H. M., Cross, D., & Watson, J. (2001). Meta-analysis of theory-of-mind development: The truth about false belief. *Child Development*, 72(3), 655–684. DOI 10.1111/1467-8624.00304.
- **Read depth:** partial. I obtained the full text and read the abstract, coding scheme, Table 2, the self-versus-other analysis (with the Figure 5 caption), the full Discussion and the Conclusions. Other results sections were skimmed.
- **What was tested:** It pooled false-belief conditions coded for age, country, task type, protagonist, target object, real presence, motive (deception), participation, salience, temporal marker and self versus other.
- **On whom:** 77 reports, 178 studies and 591 conditions (479 "primary" conditions), all typically developing children. Data run to January 1998.
- **Result:** A logit model with age, country and four task factors gives R = .74 and R² = .55. Age effect: odds ratio 2.94 per year. **Self versus other:** All self tasks are content or identity tasks about the child, with an initial demonstration, so the authors compared the 117 self conditions with 118 matched other conditions (person protagonist, content or identity task, initial demonstration or participation). There was no main effect, F(1, 230) = 1.77, p = .18, and the age interaction was F(1, 229) = 3.10, p = .07. In the authors' words, "Young children … are just as incorrect at attributing a false belief to themselves as they are at attributing it to others," and the trajectories are "virtually identical at all ages." Deception motive, participation, real presence and salience raise performance without changing the age slope. Consistency across trials is .84 (52 conditions).
- **Limits:** *Authors:* Simulation theory could argue that self failures are memory failures (Harris 1992); one needs tasks beyond false belief. *Sobel 2023:* the analysis did not weight by sample size and passed over the p = .07 interaction (f ≈ 0.12). *Ours:* "self" here always means *own past* belief after a reveal. Present-tense own beliefs were never compared.
- **Borrow:** The matched-subset logic: compare self only with others on the same task type and demonstration structure. Logit-transformed proportions as the dependent variable. The control-question exclusion rules (fewer than 60% passing controls, or 40% or more dropped).
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* move away from narrow false-belief studies towards "a theoretically well-chosen package of comparison tasks"; examine individual-level patterns across trials, not group means. *For us:* Run the matched self/other subset on the model with thousands of items per cell. The analogue of age is model strength (Haiku → Sonnet → Opus). The test of interest is the question type × model-strength interaction, which is exactly where Wellman saw a trend. Report individual-item consistency (repeat agreement, already an instrument) separately for self and other items, because program 1 showed that repeat consistency is memory.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** The comparison and its statistic map one to one; we can power the interaction that the human literature could not.

### 5. Sobel 2023: the self/other difference re-examined
- **Paper:** Sobel, D. M. (2023). Growing out of your own mind: Reexamining the development of the self-other difference in the unexpected contents task. *Cognition*, 235, 105403. DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105403. Data at https://osf.io/624av. *(Added; not on the starting list.)*
- **Read depth:** full (NSF public-access copy).
- **What was tested:** Self (representational change) versus other (false belief) questions on the unexpected-contents task, by age, in three analyses. (1) A meta-analysis of all conditions asking either question. (2) A within-child subset. (3) A quasi-mega-analysis of one lab's raw data.
- **On whom:** (1) 91 papers or datasets, 244 age groups or conditions, 9,201 data points. (2) 37 papers, 70 conditions, 2,466 data points. (3) 1,231 children aged 36–60 months (mean 50.90 months; 339 three-year-olds and 892 four-year-olds), tested with a crayon box containing birthday candles, 2001–2012. Ages 36–60 months throughout; neurotypical samples; Sobel's own papers were excluded from (1) and (2).
- **Result:**
  - **Analysis 1 (logit):** Age plus question type gives R² = .28. Adding the interaction raises R² by .01, F(1, 240) = 4.07, p = .04. There is a question-type main effect (self above other overall, |B| = 2.42, p = .02) and an age × question interaction (B = 0.04, p = .04). The Hedges' g analysis gives the same pattern.
  - **Analysis 2 (within-child GEE):** Self is better overall (logit B = 2.91, OR = 18.36, p = .001), with an age × question interaction (OR = 0.94, p = .001). Younger children do better on self; by about 5 years, other overtakes self.
  - **Analysis 3:** Other is 55% correct and self 52%. Question type OR = 5.11, p = .02; the interaction gives OR = 0.96, p = .009. In a logistic regression predicting other from self and age, only age predicts; dropping the interaction makes self a significant predictor (OR = 2.72 [2.13, 3.48]).
  - **Monte Carlo:** With N = 200 per age group, the question-type effect is significant in 4,319 of 10,000 samples. The mean N in Analysis 2 was about 35.
  - **Order:** Analysis 3 used a fixed order (other first). Children who answered the first question correctly switched about as often as those who did not (36% vs 34%, χ²(1) = 0.45, p = .50). The 37 of 70 Analysis-2 conditions with self first show the same pattern.
- **Limits:** *Author:* The effects are small. The interpretation is speculative: early privileged access to one's own knowledge, then hindsight-style memory errors on self, plus a developing "altercentric" salience of others (Southgate 2020). He does not expect the interaction to extend to adults. *Ours:* the analysis covers one task family, and both questions involve the child's own initial demonstration.
- **Borrow:** The within-subject GEE with question type × age. The **Monte Carlo power simulation** as a standard step before claiming a self/other null. The warning against compositing self and other items: 110 papers composited them, and Sobel says this practice must be justified.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Author:* revise theories so that self and other questions have "different, but potentially related developmental trajectories"; test the hindsight-bias account; integrate with adult self-other overlap work; stop compositing. *For us:* (a) The analogue of age is model strength. Does the self/other gap change sign from Haiku to Opus? The program-1 rise in format agreement (W 0.54 / 0.71 / 0.86) suggests that stronger models may be more "theory-like." (b) Test Sobel's hindsight mechanism directly: a later outcome planted *after* the model's initial answer should hurt self more than other, if self-past is reconstructed from current knowledge. (c) Run his order check: put the self question before or after the other question, with the first answer shown or redacted (program 1's order-effect instrument).
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** It gives the exact contrast, the exact statistic and a power lesson, and its open data could seed item difficulty.

### 6. Onishi & Baillargeon 2005: violation of expectation in 15-month-olds
- **Paper:** Onishi, K. H., & Baillargeon, R. (2005). Do 15-month-old infants understand false beliefs? *Science*, 308(5719), 255–258. DOI 10.1126/science.1107621.
- **Read depth:** full (published PDF hosted at McGill; supplement not read).
- **What was tested:** Whether infants look longer when an actor searches somewhere inconsistent with her belief (true or false) about a toy's location. The design was 2 belief locations × true/false × 2 search locations, all between subjects.
- **On whom:** 56 infants (mean 15 months 7 days), 7 per group; 14 more were excluded.
- **Result:** The predicted belief × action interaction was F(1, 48) = 31.24, p < .0001. It held within true-belief conditions, F(1, 24) = 14.49, and within false-belief conditions, F(1, 24) = 16.69. Each of the four conditions individually gave F > 5.34, p < .05. The authors argue that infants have a rudimentary representational theory of mind. They concede an alternative in which infants track "where she last saw it," distinguishing their own perceptions from the actor's.
- **Limits:** *Authors:* a lower-level rule (search where last seen) remains possible. *Field:* violation-of-expectation and anticipatory-looking false-belief findings replicate unevenly (records 9–11). *Ours:* the measure is other-only, and the infant's own belief is never queried.
- **Borrow:** The full 2 × 2 × 2 structure, in which actual location, belief content and belief truth are crossed so that no single cue predicts the expected act. True-belief controls inside the same design.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* adapt nonverbal false-belief tasks to autism and to animals. *For us:* The crossing is the useful part. For any self/other belief item, cross what the model has seen × what the agent has seen × the truth, so that "rate by reality," "rate by own view" and "rate by agent view" make different predictions on the 0–100 scale. That supports egocentric-versus-altercentric intrusion scoring (record 17). An implicit-style measure for a model would be the rating of an *action* ("how likely is she to open box A?") rather than the belief, matching the six-format idea that different questions may need different credences.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Partly.** The fully crossed design transfers; the looking-time logic has no model analogue.

### 7. Kovács, Téglás & Endress 2010: "The social sense"
- **Paper:** Kovács, Á. M., Téglás, E., & Endress, A. D. (2010). The social sense: Susceptibility to others' beliefs in human infants and adults. *Science*, 330(6012), 1830–1834. DOI 10.1126/science.1190792.
- **Read depth:** abstract/secondary. No open copy (the CEU PDF link redirects). Described from Phillips et al. 2015 (which reproduces the original t-tests) and Heyes 2014.
- **What was tested:** Participants press a key when a ball is revealed behind an occluder. Their own belief (P) and an irrelevant agent's belief (A) about the ball's presence are crossed (P±A±). The agent matters only for an attention check (press when the agent leaves). A looking-time version was run with 7-month-olds.
- **On whom:** Adults (N = 24 in Experiment 1, per Phillips) and 7-month-old infants (N not seen).
- **Result:** Adults detected the ball faster when only the agent believed it was there than when no one did. Per Phillips' Table 1, (P−A−) − (P−A+) gave t(23) = 2.42, p = .02, d = 0.49. Per the abstract, others' beliefs affected participants "in ways similar to their own beliefs." This is the "social sense."
- **Limits:** See record 8. The adult effect is explained by the timing of the attention check. *Ours:* the infant arm has not been addressed by that critique.
- **Borrow:** The **P × A crossing** with an *own* response that the other's belief should not affect. That is exactly the own-versus-other planting structure from program 1, mirrored.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* belief computation may be automatic. *For us:* Run a P × A crossing on graded ratings. Measure whether the model's *own* 0–100 credence in "the ball is behind the screen" moves when an agent in the transcript believes it is (altercentric pull), with the model's own evidence fixed. Program 1 found other-credited *answers* ignored (≈ 0.02). The Kovács question is whether an agent's *belief state* (not an assertion directed at the model) intrudes. Phillips' lesson applies: hold constant the position in context of the agent's last event, because recency in context is the LLM analogue of attention-check timing.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Partly.** The P × A crossing transfers; the reaction-time dependent measure does not, and the original result is an artifact.

### 8. Phillips et al. 2015: "A second look at automatic theory of mind"
- **Paper:** Phillips, J., Ong, D. C., Surtees, A. D. R., Xin, Y., Williams, S., Saxe, R., & Frank, M. C. (2015). A second look at automatic theory of mind: Reconsidering Kovács, Téglás, and Endress (2010). *Psychological Science*, 26(9), 1353–1367. DOI 10.1177/0956797614558717. Code and data at github.com/langcog/KTE. *(Added.)*
- **Read depth:** partial: abstract, introduction, methods, Experiments 1–4 results (main parts), Experiments 5–8 in full, and the start of the General Discussion.
- **What was tested:** 13 experiments replicating Kovács' adult paradigm, then manipulating the response target, a permanent occluder, the presence and timing of the attention check, and an agent-free lightbulb check.
- **On whom:** Online (MTurk) and lab adults. For example: Experiment 1a N = 60 (6 excluded), 1b N = 80 (8 excluded), 1c N = 24 in the lab; Experiment 3 N = 100.
- **Result:** The key Kovács comparison replicated: (P−A−) − (P−A+) gave d = 0.59 / 0.47 / 0.42 in Experiments 1a, 1b and 1c. But every experiment showed an unreported crossover interaction (175 ms [97, 253], 121 ms [65, 176] and 66 ms [18, 114]) that the automatic-ToM account does not predict. Removing the attention check flattened the pattern (22 ms [−47, 91], p = .53), as did fixing its time (12 ms [−35, 59], p = .62). A lightbulb check at the original times with *no agent at all* reproduced the crossover (86 ms [32, 140], p = .002). Reaction time rose linearly as the attention check moved closer to the response (9.7 and 12.1 ms/s).
- **Limits:** *Authors:* this does not show adults *lack* automatic ToM, only that this paradigm gives no evidence for it. *Ours:* it bears only on the adult arm.
- **Borrow:** The **agent-free control**, which keeps every stimulus timing and removes the mind. The double dissociation logic: vary the confound without the belief, then the belief without the confound. Maximal mixed-effects models on the crossover, not pairwise t-tests.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* more research before any positive claim of automaticity. *For us:* Every self/other contrast we run needs an "agent-free" twin. Use the same transcript with the agent replaced by an inanimate process (a sensor log, a thermostat) holding the identical information. If the "other's belief" effect survives with no agent, it is a context-position or salience effect, not attribution. This is the LLM version of Heyes' inanimate controls, and it costs one extra transcript per item.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers** (as a control design). Position and recency confounds in transcripts are the direct analogue of the timing confound.

### 9. Kulke, von Duhn, Schneider & Rakoczy 2018: implicit ToM replication
- **Paper:** Kulke, L., von Duhn, B., Schneider, D., & Rakoczy, H. (2018). Is implicit theory of mind a real and robust phenomenon? Results from a systematic replication study. *Psychological Science*, 29(6), 888–900. DOI 10.1177/0956797617747090.
- **Read depth:** partial: the full introduction, the Studies 1–2 method and results, and the Study 3 method opening. The Study 3 results and General Discussion were taken from the abstract.
- **What was tested:** Preregistered replications, with original stimuli, of four anticipatory-looking false-belief paradigms: Schneider et al. 2012; Surian & Geraci 2012; Low & Watts 2013; Southgate et al. 2007 / Senju et al. 2009. Convergent validity across them was also tested.
- **On whom:** Study 1: 119 adults, of whom 43 (36%) met all paradigms' original inclusion criteria. Study 2: 91 adults (9 excluded). Studies 3a and 3b: 36 and 19 adults.
- **Result:**
  - **Schneider:** only partly replicated. A belief × location interaction appeared, but not for the critical reason.
  - **Surian & Geraci:** the positive first-saccade finding did not replicate.
  - **Low & Watts:** fully replicated (DLS M = .35, d = 0.65), but per the abstract it no longer replicated once two stimulus confounds were removed. Those confounds were the familiarization side matching the correct side, and the actress turning towards the belief-congruent side.
  - **Southgate/Senju:** the overall DLS was not different from zero (M = .16, p = .083). FB1 was positive (M = .53) and FB2 *negative* (M = −.26).
  - **Convergent validity:** none of the paradigms intercorrelated.
- **Limits:** *Authors:* implicit ToM findings are less reliable and valid than assumed. *Ours:* this is the adult side; the infant side is ManyBabies 2 (record 11).
- **Borrow:** **Convergent validity as a requirement.** If "belief attribution" is one construct, different item families should correlate across items or models. Also the habit of pairing a replication with a confound-removal study.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* treat implicit-task conclusions with caution and find out whether the effects are true negatives or real but hard to detect (Kulke et al. 2019). *For us:* For self versus other, compute the correlation of a model's item-level self accuracy with its item-level other accuracy *across different paradigms* (Smarties, Maxi, curse of knowledge, goggles). "Shared mechanism" predicts a positive manifold across paradigms and across self/other; the human implicit tasks show zero. Our one-latent likelihood test across six formats is the same idea.

### 13. Birch & Bloom 2007: the curse of knowledge in false-belief reasoning
- **Paper:** Birch, S. A. J., & Bloom, P. (2007). The curse of knowledge in reasoning about false beliefs. *Psychological Science*, 18(5), 382–386. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01909.x.
- **Read depth:** abstract/secondary. No open copy was found. The design and the original means come from Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt 2014 (record 14), which tabulates them.
- **What was tested:** Vicki puts her violin in the blue container and leaves; her sister moves it. Participants give the *probability* that Vicki will look first in each of four containers (red, green, purple, blue), with ratings summing to 100. In Ignorance, it is moved "to another container." In Knowledge-Plausible, it goes to red, which now sits where blue was. In Knowledge-Implausible, it goes to purple.
- **On whom:** Undergraduates (Yale, per secondary sources).
- **Result (as reported by Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt):** Red-container ratings were Ignorance 23 (SD 22, n = 56), Implausible 19 (SD 21, n = 43) and Plausible 34 (SD 25, n = 51). Blue was 71 in Ignorance and 59 in Plausible. Plausible versus Ignorance on red gave t(105) = −2.42, p < .05. Knowing the true location raised adults' probability that the naive agent would act on it, but only when that location was plausible. Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt compute d = .469 for knowledge and d = .645 for plausibility. They note that the original knowledge effect was only slightly smaller than analogous child effects (d ≈ 0.59 and 0.55).
- **Limits:** The effect is much smaller on replication (record 14). The red and blue ratings are not independent. There is a floor on purple.
- **Borrow:** **The response format.** Probabilities across exhaustive options summing to 100 are a native 0–100 instrument, so the curse of knowledge reads as a graded shift in mass towards the true location, not a pass or fail. The plausibility moderator is a built-in dose variable.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors (per abstract):* plausibility mediates egocentric bias in adults. *For us:* This is the single best-matched design. (a) **Other:** model rates the agent's search probabilities in the Ignorance / Implausible / Plausible conditions; the curse of knowledge is the red-rating shift. (b) **Self (hindsight):** the model first gives its own probabilities when naive, then sees the outcome, then is asked "what probabilities did you give before you knew?" with its earlier answer shown, redacted or planted. That is Bernstein's memory design (record 15) on the same items. (c) Compare the knowledge-induced shift for self-past against other; the shared-mechanism prediction is equal slopes. (d) Own-versus-other planting: plant "the agent thinks red is 60" as said by the agent versus as the model's own earlier estimate, at 10 / 30 / 60 points. (e) Six formats: the same item as a bet, as a frequency out of 100, and so on. (f) Judge × author: model B estimates model A's naive probabilities from A's transcript with A's naive answer redacted, versus A doing it for itself.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** It is already graded 0–100, and its self (hindsight) twin is well defined.

### 14. Ryskin & Brown-Schmidt 2014: true effect size of the curse of knowledge
- **Paper:** Ryskin, R. A., & Brown-Schmidt, S. (2014). Do adults show a curse of knowledge in false-belief reasoning? A robust estimate of the true effect size. *PLoS ONE*, 9(3), e92406. *(Added.)*
- **Read depth:** partial. The PMC full text was processed through a fetch summarizer, which returned all tables, means, tests and power numbers. I did not read the prose line by line.
- **What was tested:** Seven replications of Birch & Bloom 2007, plus priming (Experiment 1), a control condition (Experiment 2) and Latin-square container order (Experiment 7).

- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* very large samples, and a re-examination of what the paradigm measures. *For us:* 394 items or samples per cell is trivial at our cost (about $0.05–0.10 per 1,000 Haiku calls). Always include the Experiment-2-style control, and counterbalance option order (program 1 already shows that question order shifts ratings by a median of 25 points, which would swamp a d = 0.2 effect).
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers** (as calibration). It tells us the effect size to expect and the controls to include.

- **Paper:** Bernstein, D. M., Atance, C., Meltzoff, A. N., & Loftus, G. R. (2007). Hindsight bias and developing theories of mind. *Child Development*, 78(4), 1374–1394. (DOI not verified.) *(Added.)*

- **What was tested:** A preschool hindsight-bias battery and its relation to ToM. Experiment 1 used the hypothetical design: after learning an object's identity, when will naive Ernie identify a degrading image? Experiment 2 used the memory design: recall *your own* earlier answer after learning the correct one, with discrete and continuous items and feedback-before versus feedback-after controls for regression to the mean. ToM tasks had self and other questions (Unexpected Contents, Occluded Pictures, Appearance-Reality) plus change of location. Inhibitory control, working memory and the PPVT were also measured.

- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* study "horizontal ties" between hindsight and ToM, and test fluency as a link. *For us:* The certainty moderator is directly testable with graded ratings. Planted prior answers at 10 / 30 / 60 points from the model's view already vary distance; add the prior's stated *confidence* (a planted "I'm 55% sure" versus "I'm 95% sure"). If self-past is reconstructed from current knowledge, low-confidence priors should be overwritten more, for self and for a naive other alike. This is a clean self/other test on the same items.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** It is the self-side complement to Birch & Bloom, with graded scoring and a built-in control.

### 18. Williams & Happé 2009: "What did I say?" versus "What did I think?"
- **Paper:** Williams, D. M., & Happé, F. (2009). 'What did I say?' versus 'What did I think?': Attributing false beliefs to self amongst children with and without autism. *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, 39(6), 865–873. DOI 10.1007/s10803-009-0695-6. *(Added.)*
- **Read depth:** full (Kent repository accepted manuscript; the tables themselves were not in the extracted text, but all contingencies are reported in prose).
- **What was tested:** Whether the Smarties self question can be passed by remembering one's earlier *statement* instead of one's *belief*. In the new "Plasters" task, the experimenter feigns a cut and asks for a plaster; the child reaches for the plasters box, opens it and finds candles. The child never voices the belief; it is shown by an action. Then come the self question, the other question and a reality control. A subsample also did the standard Smarties task, where the child says "Smarties" before the reveal.
- **On whom:** 52 autistic children, 25 children with developmental disability (DD) and 41 typically developing (TD) children. 19 ASD and all 41 TD children also did Smarties.
- **Result:**
  - **Plasters, ASD:** self 27/52 (51.9%) vs other 36/52 (69.2%). 11 failed self but passed other and 2 showed the reverse (McNemar p = .02), so **self is harder than other**.
  - **Plasters, DD and TD:** parity (McNemar p = .63 and .18).
  - **Smarties:** parity in ASD (p > .99) and TD (p = .45).
  - **TD, Plasters self vs Smarties self:** 9/41 TD children passed Smarties self but failed Plasters self, against 1/41 the other way (McNemar p = .02). Voicing the belief scaffolds even typical children's self reports.
  - **Wording:** the paper also reviews earlier studies (Perner et al. 1989; Leslie & Thaiss 1992) that asked "what did you *say*" and "what will she *say*." Those studies found autistic children *better* on self than other, the reverse of the "think" wording.
- **Limits:** *Authors:* the ASD Smarties subsample was small and mostly at floor (10/19 failed both self questions). The Other question was matched across tasks, but the Self questions differ in more than verbalization (an action versus an answer to a question).
- **Borrow:** **The memory-for-statement confound and its removal.** This is the most transferable single idea in the cluster. Two items cross a *say* versus *think* question wording with a *voiced* versus *acted* prior belief.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* use Plasters-type tasks for self/other false belief in future work; study how autistic people represent their own states. *For us:* This is the human analogue of program 1's finding that repeat consistency is memory (86% agreement falls to 36% when the first answer is hidden). The design maps exactly onto transcript editing:
  - **Smarties:** the model's earlier answer ("crayons") is in the transcript.
  - **Plasters:** the model's earlier *action* is in the transcript (a tool call such as `open_box("crayon box")` made to fetch crayons) but no stated belief.
  - **Redacted:** neither.
  - **Planted:** a contrary statement.

  Ask both "what did you think was inside" and "what did you say was inside," and in parallel "what will Sam think" (Sam is a naive agent), all on 0–100. If the model's self-report is a transcript reading, self accuracy should track the presence of the *statement* (high in Smarties, lower in Plasters, at the "think" ≈ "say" level), while the other question is unaffected, which would be an ASD-like self < other profile when the statement is absent. If the model infers its prior belief from its action as well as typical children do, Plasters self ≈ Smarties self. Judge × author: model B, given A's Plasters transcript, may infer A's prior belief from the action *better* than A does. That would replicate program 1's "no privileged self-knowledge."
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** It is the most direct human analogue of program 1's core result, and transcript editing implements it cleanly.

- **Limits:** *Authors:* three interpretations remain possible (mentalism, psychological affordance, spatial-causal "seeing triangle"). Third-person observation might also suffice, and is untested.
- **Borrow:** **Bidirectional self-experience training with a trick object.** First-person experience contradicts surface appearance, so any transfer to the other must come from the self-experience.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* test the scope of generalization (this blindfold, all blindfolds, all barriers; people versus robots), and test whether *observing* another's behaviour with the blindfold (fumbling versus skilled action) substitutes for first-person experience. *For us:* That second test is exactly own-versus-other planting. In a transcript, the "trick occluder" fact is learned either (a) first-person, from the model's own tool call returning readable or unreadable text through a filter, (b) third-person, by observing another agent's transcript using it, or (c) by stated rule ("the frosted filter is see-through"). Then the model rates what an agent behind that filter believes. Program 1 found other-credited *answers* ignored. Does other-credited *experience* transfer to attribution as well as own experience does? Graded 0–100 ratings and six formats apply unchanged.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** Transcript editing makes the trick-object manipulation trivially clean, and the authors' own open question is our own-versus-other planting.

### 23. Kano, Krupenye, Hirata, Tomonaga & Call 2019: great apes use self-experience
- **Paper:** Kano, F., Krupenye, C., Hirata, S., Tomonaga, M., & Call, J. (2019). Great apes use self-experience to anticipate an agent's action in a false-belief test. *PNAS*, 116(42), 20904–20909. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1910095116.
- **Read depth:** full main text (PDF copy; SI not read).
- **What was tested:** A goggles test inside an anticipatory-looking false-belief video, between subjects. Apes first experienced, in real life, a novel white barrier as either see-through or opaque; the two look identical from afar. Then all watched the same video: the actor sees an object hidden, goes behind the barrier, and the object is moved and then removed. The measures were first looks and the differential looking score (DLS) during the actor's ambiguous reach. The design follows the FB2 structure of Krupenye et al. 2016.
- **On whom:** 47 great apes (29 chimpanzees, 14 bonobos, 4 orangutans) from Kumamoto, Inuyama and Leipzig; 23 opaque and 24 see-through.
- **Result:** First looks did not differ (Fisher p = .46). DLS was more target-biased with the opaque barrier than with the see-through one (Wilcoxon Z = 2.13, n = 30, p = .033, r = .39). It was above chance for opaque (Z = 2.35, n = 13, p = .019) and at chance for see-through (Z = 0.80, n = 17, p = .42). There was no species difference. Excluding the apes previously tested in FB2 left the DLS above chance (0.52 ± 0.66, n = 11, p = .032). The authors conclude that apes attributed visual access from their own past perceptual experience. Looking at the barrier and at the displacement did not differ between conditions, which answers Heyes' distraction critique of Senju et al. 2011.
- **Limits:** *Authors:* the effect appears in DLS but not first looks; it does not rule out a learned "line of sight" affordance; identity-based false belief is needed to separate beliefs from registrations (Apperly & Butterfill). *Ours:* about 13–17 apes contributed DLS per condition, and only 30 of 47 looked at either box. There is no self-report measure, so ape *self*-attribution cannot be compared.
- **Borrow:** **Identical test events, with only the subject's own experience differing.** Process measures (attention to each event) rule out the alternative that the barrier simply distracted the subject.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* identity-based false-belief tests; further non-mentalistic controls. *For us:* This is the same transfer as record 22, adding (i) a false-belief rather than gaze test, and (ii) the process-measure control. In our version, the "process measure" is verifying that the model *read* the planted experience: a probe question in a cloned branch ("could you read the text through the filter?"), asked without contaminating the main branch. Cloning makes this check free, which apes cannot have.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers.** Self-experience-then-attribute is directly implementable, and the clone makes manipulation checks non-invasive.

### 24. Krupenye, Kano, Hirata, Call & Tomasello 2016: apes anticipate false beliefs
- **Paper:** Krupenye, C., Kano, F., Hirata, S., Call, J., & Tomasello, M. (2016). Great apes anticipate that other individuals will act according to false beliefs. *Science*, 354(6308), 110–114. DOI 10.1126/science.aaf8110.
- **Read depth:** full main text (St Andrews accepted manuscript; SI not read).
- **What was tested:** Eye-tracked anticipatory looking in two scenarios (an ape-costume "King Kong" character and a human actor; a haystack hiding scene; an object in boxes). The agent watches a hiding, then the object is moved and removed while he is present (FB1) or absent (FB2).
- **On whom:** Experiment 1: 40 apes (19 chimpanzees, 14 bonobos, 7 orangutans). Experiment 2: 30 apes (29 from Experiment 1 plus 1 bonobo).
- **Result:** Experiment 1: 20 of 30 lookers looked first at the target (p = .098). Experiment 2: 17 of 22 (p = .016). In a combined analysis of the 29 apes in both experiments, there were more first looks to the target (Wilcoxon Z = 3.25, p = .001), in FB1 (p = .046) and FB2 (p = .031), with no species difference. The authors conclude that apes anticipate action from false belief, at least implicitly. In explicit choice tasks, apes have failed.
- **Limits:** *Authors:* all change-of-location tasks are open in principle to the "search where last seen" rule; the understanding is implicit. *Ours:* this is the same paradigm family that failed to replicate in humans (Kulke et al. 2018; FB2 was negative there). I found no published direct ape replication beyond Kano 2019's opaque condition.
- **Borrow:** The FB1/FB2 contrast: the belief is false because of a move witnessed and then unwitnessed, versus a move never witnessed.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Authors:* reconcile implicit success with explicit failure. *For us:* The implicit/explicit split in apes and infants mirrors a model question. Does the model's *action prediction* ("which box will she open?") agree with its *belief report* ("what does she believe?") on the same item? Six formats and the held-out-format prediction score test whether these share one latent. If they do for others but not for self (or the reverse), that is a dissociation worth reporting.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Partly.** The FB1/FB2 items transfer; the implicit measure does not.

### 25. Call & Tomasello 2008: "Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind? 30 years later"
- **Paper:** Call, J., & Tomasello, M. (2008). Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind? 30 years later. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 12(5), 187–192. DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2008.02.010.
- **Read depth:** abstract/secondary (no open copy; abstract and journal summary via search; also described by Kano et al. 2019).
- **What was tested:** A review of chimpanzee social cognition.
- **Result:** Chimpanzees understand others' goals, intentions, perception and knowledge, but at the time there was no evidence that they understand false beliefs. The authors propose a "perception-goal psychology" rather than a belief-desire psychology. Later implicit results (Krupenye 2016; Kano 2019) are framed as challenging the false-belief part.
- **Limits:** The review predates the eye-tracking studies.
- **Borrow:** The **knowledge-versus-belief split**: an agent could track ignorance (did not see it) without tracking false belief (believes it is in A).
- **Next steps it suggests:** *For us:* Stage the items as ManyBabies 2 does (record 11). Knowledge/ignorance items come first, then false-belief items, for self and other. A model might show parity on knowledge but a gap on false belief, or the reverse.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Partly** (as a taxonomy).

### 26. Heyes 2014: "Submentalizing"
- **Paper:** Heyes, C. (2014). Submentalizing: I am not really reading your mind. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 9(2), 131–143. DOI 10.1177/1745691613518076.
- **Read depth:** partial (Oxford author copy): the abstract, the Kovács and Southgate–Senju sections (scanned), and the Conclusions and Future Directions in full.
- **What was tested:** A critique arguing that the strongest adult implicit-mentalizing effects can arise from domain-general processes. The dot task becomes involuntary attentional orienting; the social Simon task, spatial response coding; the Kovács "Smurf" task, retroactive interference; and the Southgate–Senju task, distraction.
- **Result:** None of the leading procedures yet demonstrates implicit mentalizing. Heyes recommends closely matched *inanimate* controls (e.g., arrows; Santiesteban et al. 2014) and **"self-informed belief induction."** Her concrete proposal is a dot-perspective task in which participants first try on a translucent and an opaque visor of different colours. Furlanetto et al. 2016 later ran exactly this and found a positive result (record 17).
- **Limits:** *Ours:* submentalizing explains automatic effects; it does not address explicit self/other parity.
- **Borrow:** **Inanimate-control** and **self-informed induction** as two standard controls for any claim that a model attributes a state to an agent.
- **Next steps it suggests:** *Author:* inanimate controls, attentional measures, self-informed belief induction in several modalities (vision, hearing, touch). *For us:* The submentalizing worry has a precise LLM form. An "other-belief" effect may be lexical or positional (the agent's name sits near a location word, so the location gains probability mass) rather than attributional. Controls: (i) replace the agent with an inanimate (a camera that "recorded" location A); (ii) match token distance and recency; (iii) use self-informed induction, where the only thing that differs is the model's own planted experience. If (iii) moves ratings and (i) does not, that is attribution, not submentalizing.
- **Transfer verdict:** **Transfers** (as a control framework).

## Designs ranked by how cheaply they test "does the model's account of another's belief behave like its account of its own?"

1. **Smarties versus Plasters with transcript editing** (records 2, 18, 4, 5). Matched deceptive-container items, with self-past, other-naive and reality questions on 0–100. The model's prior statement is shown, redacted, replaced by an action, or planted (own or other-credited, at 10 / 30 / 60 points). The question wording is "think" or "say." Prediction if self-report is transcript reading: self ≈ other when the statement is present; self < other when only the action remains; self follows the plant at program-1 uptake rates. Judge × author: model B infers A's prior belief from A's transcript, against A itself. Cost: a few thousand calls.
2. **Birch & Bloom ratings with a Bernstein hindsight twin** (records 13, 14, 15). The other version gives a naive agent's search probabilities under Ignorance / Implausible / Plausible knowledge, plus the Ryskin control condition. The self version gives the model's own naive probabilities, then the outcome, then recall of its prior with that prior shown, redacted or planted, plus a feedback-after control. Compare the knowledge-induced shift for self against other, and the moderation by prior confidence. About 400 items per cell are needed for d ≈ 0.2, which is cheap.
3. **Self-informed belief induction (goggles)** (records 17, 22, 23, 26). Plant the model's own first-hand tool experience with a novel filter (see-through or opaque), against the same fact observed in another agent's transcript, against a stated rule. Test the agent's belief on identical items, and test whether the model's *own* rating shows altercentric intrusion only when the agent can "see." Add an inanimate control (record 8). This is the cleanest test of whether own experience is privileged over other-credited experience in attribution.

Secondary designs: (4) a P × A crossing of the model's own credence against an irrelevant agent's belief (records 7, 16), with position and recency matched; (5) signature-limit families (location against identity items; record 12); (6) staged knowledge/ignorance before false belief, including whether the model marks its own ignorance before others' (records 11, 25); (7) a probe-and-ablate "lesion" test of co-impairment (record 21), on the open-weight model.

## Strongest evidence for and against a shared self/other mechanism

- **For (humans):** Wellman et al. 2001 found self and other parity across 235 matched conditions, with near-identical age curves. The within-child battery coherence is α = .85 (Bernstein et al. 2007). Autism co-impairs own and others' false belief and intention once the "say" route is removed (Williams 2010; Williams & Happé 2010).
- **For (animals and infants):** First-person experience of a novel occluder changes attribution to others in 12- to 18-month-olds (Meltzoff & Brooks 2008), great apes (Kano et al. 2019) and adults (Furlanetto et al. 2016). Self-experience feeds other-attribution, though none of these measures the subject's self-*report*.
- **Against (humans):** Self and other questions have reproducibly different, crossing age trajectories (Sobel 2023; small effect). Autistic children are worse on their own belief than on others' when they cannot replay their statement (Williams & Happé 2009). Hindsight bias about one's own past persists into adulthood while other false belief is mastered (Bernstein et al. 2007), so the two dissociate developmentally despite correlating.
- **Against (animals):** There is no direct counter-evidence. The gap is that no animal study measures self-attribution, so the comparative literature cannot address parity at all.
