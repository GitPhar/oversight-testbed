# What the human adds

*Plain English, written 2026-11-05 by a Claude Fable 5.1 session that did not write any of the steers it describes. Every claim names the file that shows it. Where this page and a file disagree, the file wins. The method behind the labels is `operators/steers.md`; the labels are `ledger/steer-values.jsonl`, summed in `ledger/steers.md`.*

## The question

This project is a machine for doing research with an AI, and one person, Sean, steers it. The machine writes down its odds before every test and gets scored on them. The person was supposed to be scored too. On 5 November a review written as an Anthropic panel would read it (`paper/review-anthropic.md`, Part 1) found that the person's side of the record was nearly empty: twenty logged decisions, almost all marked "pending", and an odds column with a handful of bets in it. Sean's answer was that the record was missing the main thing he did: the direction, the rules and the design are his as much as the engine's.

This page says, in plain words, what his decisions changed, which of the engine's own mistakes a person caught, which of his decisions cost money or went nowhere, and what the small odds column does and does not say.

## What his steers changed

A **steer** is one decision by Sean: a question to start on, a rule, a stop, a budget call, a change to how the system works, or a batch of odds. There are 28 of them (`ledger/steers.jsonl`, st-01 to st-28). A scoring session has now valued each one by reading what it changed in the record, what the engine would have done without it, and any number that shows the difference, and given it a label: helped, neutral or hurt. The count is **helped 18 of 28, neutral 10, hurt 0** (`ledger/steers.md`). What follows is the substance behind the count.

**The rules the engine runs under are his.** The rule never to lower a pre-registered bar after seeing data (st-02; `CONTEXT.md` section 3 rule 6) was his words on the first day, and it is the rule that killed his own two near-wins: a quantum "near-miss" that turned out to be two rating scales pooled the wrong way, and a Sonnet edge that a classical model matched exactly (`programs/entangled-mind/results/exp5-run-2.md`; `proposal.md`, "Why this matters for safety", item 1). The rule to read the literature in full before designing anything (st-04; `ARCHITECTURE.md` invariant 5) came from one evening when a planned experiment would have used a control the API does not offer (`programs/entangled-mind/DEVIATIONS.md` D17). The rule that the engine never asks him anything in a terminal, so that every number he gives is blind (st-15; `CONTEXT.md` rule 11), is why all 26 of his odds lines say he had not seen the engine's number (`ledger/human-odds.jsonl`). The rule that chip budgets under the hard stop are the engine's call (st-16; `CONTEXT.md` rule 7) took him out of a chore and left six documented rulings, all capped in code (`ledger/spend.jsonl`).

**The frame is his.** The decision that this was not a pipeline demonstration but a scored system with a human hand (st-07; `proposal.md`, `ARCHITECTURE.md`) is what produced the ledger, the five hypotheses and every published number about the engine. The panel review's praise, "the strongest single artefact a candidate has sent us this cycle about research judgment", and its sharpest complaint, the empty human column, are both things only that frame could produce.

**Four of the seven programs start from his questions.** Program 2 asks whether a model's account of another mind is like its account of its own (st-09). Program 3 grades the engine with a quantum chip that shares none of its habits (st-10). Program 5 asks whether a model grades its own work more kindly (st-17). Program 7 asks whether a model that says it is unsure acts unsure (st-20). Program 1 and its gold map were his push too (st-01, st-03): three of the ledger's seven primary hits sit under that one question (`ledger/brier.md`).

**Four changes to the machine in the last day are his, and each one fixed something the engine was getting wrong.** They are the next section.

## Which of the engine's own mistakes a person caught

Four of the engine's habits were wrong in ways that only show from outside the loop. In each case the record shows a diagnosis, Sean's decision, and the rule that followed. An honest note first: in three of the four, the diagnosis was written by a Claude session (a review, a literature check, a file explaining the gap); what the record shows as Sean's is the decision to make it a rule, with the time it took effect. In the fourth, the cockpit, the complaint and the fix were his own.

**1. The engine did not know what was already in print.** Program 5 asked whether a model grades its own work more kindly. The scout read a March 2026 paper that tests almost exactly that, three times, and the engine bet anyway; its one "pass" (an exaggeration punished less when it sits in the model's own turn) is a published effect (`paper/novelty.md`, items 1 and 2; `paper/informed-engine.md`). Across the whole record, 6 of 10 findings were already known, 3 partly new, none new and replicated (`paper/novelty.md`). Sean's decision (st-27): the engine must know what prior work predicts before it bets, and must check whether a result is already known before calling it a finding. That became rule 13: a "Prior work" line on every direction, a "Novelty" line on every landed result, a design-review check and an audit check (`CONTEXT.md` rule 13; `operators/design-review.md` D12). He also parked the two test-awareness cards he had given 95 and 90 percent, because the literature says Anthropic already runs those tests before every release (st-22, st-23; `paper/novelty.md` item 9). Those two are the import rule applied against his own highest odds, and each gets a shadow check later to say whether it cost a hit (`ledger/shadows.jsonl` sh-002, sh-003).

**2. The only-Haiku habit.** Program 1's belief findings were mostly on the smallest Claude model, program 2 ran on one model, program 4 on one model; the review lists this as a weakness of the whole record (`paper/review-anthropic.md`, Part 3, "One-model findings"). Sean's decision (st-25): every bet on three models unless the pre-registration says why not. That became rule 12 (`CONTEXT.md` rule 12). Since then, three of the nine results have split by model: the own-turn blunting shows on Haiku and Opus and not on Sonnet (`programs/self-grading/results/sg-b3-flaw.md`), the sandbagging test was untestable on Haiku and null on the other two (`programs/test-awareness/results/ta-b3-results.md`), and "are you sure?" moves Haiku but almost never Sonnet or Opus (`programs/said-vs-did/results/sv-b1-pushback.md`). A one-model design would have reported each as a clean finding or a clean null. The price is that the largest model costs about a hundred times the smallest per call, paid in fewer items.

**3. Landed bets going nowhere.** Programs 2, 3 and 4 each ran one round on 4 November and stopped; nothing turned the audits' "what would break it next" into new directions (`CONTEXT.md` section 6, step 6a). Sean's decision (st-26): a landed bet must open the next paths. That became the remap operator and an invariant, no map may end at a landed bet, with a script that checks it (`operators/remap.md`; `ledger/check_remap.py`). The follow-ups it produced have been scored: counting one bet per run, 2 of 7 came back hits, against 3 of 20 first-layer bets (`ledger/bets.jsonl` with the parent links in `map/*.json`). Follow-ups chase results that already landed, so they are expected to hit more; the point is that the engine was not taking that advantage before.

**4. The cockpit nobody could use.** The cockpit is the only place Sean gives odds. From 14:52 on 4 November to 04:45 on 5 November it had forms, a next-to-run strip, a human column, filters, veto and add-question forms, and produced no saved odds at all (`site/README.md`, cockpit rows; `ledger/human-odds.jsonl`, first line 05:11 on 5 November). His site walk cut it to twelve plain cards and one save button (st-28; `site/README.md`, the site-night row), and his further decision was that each card must carry what our own results already show. Twenty-six minutes after the plain rebuild he saved twelve odds lines; an hour later, fourteen more. Whether the second batch came from a page that already carried the context cards depends on the deploy, which the record does not show, so that part is not yet separable from the rebuild.

## Which steers hurt or cost money

No steer is labelled hurt. Several cost money or time and bought less than hoped, and the labels say so:

- **Program 2** (st-09): $5.94 of API, seven scored bets, no hit; one partly-new lead (`ledger/bets.jsonl`; `paper/novelty.md` item 6). Its chief product is a pattern the engine learned about its own overconfidence (`ledger/lessons.jsonl`, P1).
- **Program 5** (st-17): $3.63 on a question the literature had already answered, which the engine did not say until afterwards (`paper/novelty.md` items 1 and 2).
- **Program 7** (st-20): $2.75, two clean misses and one untestable bet; a useful null (the stated number predicts behaviour no better than knowing the question type) but no hit (`programs/said-vs-did/results/sv-b2-decline.md`).
- **The chip program** (st-10): 89 of the 420 chip seconds; its calibration column looks best of the three referees, but the third audit round found two of its three early misses could not have passed, so most of that number was bought by the design rather than the world (`ledger/audits/SUMMARY-3.md`, hole 2).
- **The window** (st-06): an afternoon of session work on a display he then retired; one note from it still seeded a test.
- **P3-9** (st-24): about 42 chip seconds committed to the one direction where he and the engine disagree most (his 0.90 against the engine's 0.40), running as this is written. Nothing can be said until it scores.

Across the three programs he seeded that have run, programs 2, 5 and 7, the record is $12.32 for one hit, and that hit is a known effect. That is the honest cost side. The gain side is the rules above, which have no price in dollars, and which the record shows the engine would not have set for itself.

## What the four-bet odds column does and does not say

Sean has given odds on 26 directions. Only four of them have reached a scored result on a bet that carries his number, after the rule that one lucky run counts once (`ledger/brier.md`, rule 15; five counting the secondary bet). On those four:

| | Brier score (lower is better; a coin scores 0.250) |
|---|---|
| the engine | 0.057 |
| Sean | 0.513 |
| the two averaged | 0.208 |

What it says: on these four bets his numbers were far too high. He gave 0.70 and 0.60 to two tests that failed, 1.00 to a test that failed, and 0.55 to the one that passed; the engine was within a few points on all four (`ledger/bets.jsonl`: om-p2-7, ta-b1r2, ss-b12, ng-p3-18). Averaging his odds with the engine's made the forecast worse than the engine alone. Under the method, the odds steer (st-18) is therefore neutral, by the proposal's own rule, until eight such bets are scored.

What it does not say: anything about skill. Four bets cannot tell a bad forecaster from an unlucky one; the proposal needs twenty (`paper/the-engine.md`, H3). The four that resolved first are the cheap ones, which is a selection, not a sample. The engine's numbers on those bets were written after reading its own record and a lesson from its past misses (`CONTEXT.md` rules 9 and 14); Sean's were single numbers given blind, and the cockpit never showed him the base rate. That base rate is the one thing the column already says clearly: the engine's own bets come true about one time in four (7 of 31, `ledger/brier.md`), and he expects a hit on 21 of the 26 directions he rated (`paper/the-engine.md` section 6.11). A person who believes in the directions he chose is more hopeful than the record of testing them. Whether his hope is also better information is what the next sixteen bets will say.

## What to make of "helped 18 of 28"

It is a count of readings, not a measurement. A Claude session read each steer against the default the record shows and wrote down a label with the files that support it. The same model family runs the engine, carried out the steers, logged them and is the subject of the research. The counterfactuals were read from files, never run. The method is published so that anyone can take the same files and reach a different label, and the record keeps every earlier label when one changes (`operators/steers.md`, "How to disagree"). The one measurement the ledger has of Sean's judgment is the odds column above, and on it he is behind the engine at four bets. The things the count does show are plain: the rules the engine runs under were his, four of its mistakes were corrected from outside the loop, and the corrections have measurable effects already (two of seven follow-ups hit; three of nine results split by model; two cards parked for $1.30 because the literature had the answer). That is what the human adds, as far as the record can say it today.
