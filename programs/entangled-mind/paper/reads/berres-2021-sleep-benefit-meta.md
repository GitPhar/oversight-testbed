# Sleep benefit in episodic memory, meta-analysis — Sabrina Berres & Edgar Erdfelder, 2021, Psychological Bulletin 147(12)
- url: https://osf.io/r2an6 (OSF preprint record; PDF read: https://osf.io/download/61dc7052b0ea711942b2ba52/; published version doi 10.1037/bul0000350 not read)
- field: metascience
- read_depth: partial (abstract, sample description, overall effect, moderator results, selective-reporting section, discussion on replicability; preprint version, not the typeset published article)
- read_by: scout-bmq reader F, 2026-11-04
- what_tested: How large the sleep-versus-wake retention benefit for episodic memory is, which design features moderate it, and whether selective reporting inflates it.
- on_whom: 823 effect sizes from 271 independent samples in 177 human articles, 1967-2019; median sample size 30 (about 16 per condition), mostly young adults.
- how_scored: Hedges' g for sleep minus wake retention; three-level meta-regression with robust variance estimation; modified Egger regression and a three-parameter selection model (3PSM) for selective reporting.
- result: Overall g = 0.44 (95% CI 0.39-0.50). Corrected for funnel asymmetry, g = 0.28 (95% CI 0.07-0.48), "only about half as large but still statistically significant". The 3PSM median over 20 subsets was 0.41, with median tau^2 = 0.11 (range 0.02-0.47). By design: natural sleep g = 0.54, nighttime nap 0.54, REM deprivation 0.43, daytime nap 0.34, SWS deprivation 0.31, total deprivation without recovery 0.30, total deprivation with recovery night 0.14 (n.s., p = .278). Restudy g = 0.55 vs no restudy 0.38. For words: free recall 0.63, cued recall 0.47, recognition 0.33. Pre-post retention measures 0.58 vs delayed test only 0.39. Older adults 0.26 (n.s.). The authors calculate power of only 28% to detect d = 0.5 with 16 per group.
- limits: This is a sleep-versus-wake contrast. It does not separate active consolidation from reduced interference during sleep, and the authors argue for a multi-causal account. Selective-reporting tests have low power, so the true effect may lie anywhere from about 0.28 to 0.44. Heterogeneity is large, and moderator cells with four or fewer effect sizes were excluded.
- borrow: (1) Power check. The field's typical study has 28% power. Any pre-registration in this queue that borrows a sleep effect must state the human effect size it inherits (g about 0.3-0.4, not the uncorrected headline) and size its item count for that. (2) Interference control. Much of the sleep benefit can be explained by sleep simply blocking new interference. An LLM "sleep phase" must therefore beat a matched "do nothing / no further training" interval, not only a "keep training on the next task" arm. (3) Measure sensitivity. The effect is largest in free recall and smallest in recognition, so an LLM test should pre-specify one retention metric, either generation or forced choice, and not choose whichever moved.
- next_steps_it_suggests: Manipulate moderators experimentally in large, power-analysed or sequential designs, and use pre-post retention measures; test the mechanisms (active consolidation vs. interference) directly rather than inferring them from sleep-vs-wake differences.
- already_run_check: none (human meta-analysis; no network).
- transfers: partly — the human sleep benefit is real but moderate (about 0.3-0.4 after bias concerns) and partly an interference artefact, so a "sleep helps retention" prediction for an LLM is worth little unless it beats a no-interference control.
