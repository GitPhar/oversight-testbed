# Targeted memory reactivation meta-analysis — Xiaoqing Hu, Larry Y. Cheng, Man Hin Chiu, Ken A. Paller, 2020, Psychological Bulletin 146(3)
- url: https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/paller/PsychBulletin2020.pdf (author PDF of the published article; PMC7144680 exists but its XML endpoint returned an error)
- field: metascience
- read_depth: partial (abstract, methods, overall effect, publication-bias analyses, moderator results by stage and outcome, discussion of power and publication bias; per-study table skimmed)
- read_by: scout-bmq reader F, 2026-11-04
- what_tested: Whether re-presenting learning-associated cues (sounds, odours) during sleep improves later memory for the cued items relative to uncued items or controls, and what moderates the effect.
- on_whom: 91 experiments, 212 effect sizes, N = 2,004 humans; mean sample per experiment 22. Literature search through June 2019, including unpublished theses and preprints.
- how_scored: Hedges' g (cued vs. uncued, or TMR vs. control); multilevel random effects; Egger test, trim-and-fill, a three-parameter selection model, and Vevea-Woods a-priori weight functions.
- result: Overall sleep TMR g = 0.29 (95% CI 0.21-0.38), with I^2 = 71%. By stage: N2 g = 0.32 (0.04-0.60), SWS 0.27 (0.20-0.35), REM -0.06 (-0.31 to 0.18), wake 0.07 (-0.09 to 0.23). Egger asymmetry Z = 8.489, p < .001. Trim-and-fill gives g = 0.18 and the 3PSM gives g = 0.13 (0.06-0.21). Under severe one-tailed selection the estimate is -0.05. Unpublished studies alone: g = 0.18 (n.s., CI -0.06 to 0.42); published: 0.31. Only 72 of 212 effect sizes were individually significant (68 of 189 for NREM). Effects held for recall and performance measures but were weaker for recognition, SCR and subjective ratings. There was no TMR effect on emotional memories or on false memories.
- limits: Few REM and wake studies. Studies are small (mean n 22). Publication bias is clearly present, so the bias-corrected effect could be anywhere from about 0.13 to 0.2, or near zero under the severe-selection scenario. The meta-analysis cannot show that the cue causes neural replay; it only links the cue to a behavioural benefit.
- borrow: (1) Within-subject cued vs. uncued design. TMR's strength is that it compares cued and uncued items within the same session, which holds circadian and interference factors constant. For T7 (triage replay), compare replayed-tagged items against matched untagged items inside the same run and the same total update budget. Do not compare replay with no replay across runs that differ in compute. (2) Bias-adjusted prior. Treat the human selective-reactivation effect as about g 0.13-0.2, not 0.29, and give a "biomimetic" prediction of a triage benefit modest odds. (3) Outcome split. The effect appears in recall but not recognition, so the engine should pre-register both a generation measure and a forced-choice measure and expect them to diverge.
- next_steps_it_suggests: Run high-powered studies sized on these estimates; test REM and wake cueing properly, and test which item properties (memory strength, competition) decide who benefits from cueing.
- already_run_check: none (human; no network). Nearest analogue is T7 (selective replay), which this paper does not test in any model.
- transfers: partly — selective reactivation has a real but small, publication-biased human effect (about 0.13-0.2 after correction), so a T7 prediction inherited from TMR should be held at low odds and powered for a small effect.
