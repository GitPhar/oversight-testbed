- what_tested: Why should memory first be stored in the hippocampus and enter the neocortex only gradually? The argument rests on connectionist simulations. (a) The McCloskey & Cohen (1989) AB-AC paired-associate network shows catastrophic interference. (b) Rumelhart's (1990) semantic network, after 500 epochs on a base corpus, is taught that "penguin is a bird that can swim, not fly" by FOCUSED learning (only the new item) or by INTERLEAVED learning (new item mixed into the full corpus), at the same learning rate.
- on_whom: Small backprop networks (the Rumelhart semantic network: plants/animals, relations such as "can"; the McCloskey-Cohen two-part-input network). Human AB-AC data from Barnes & Underwood (1959) for comparison.
- how_scored: AB-list recall during AC training. Absolute error (sum over output units of |target - output|) on the penguin item, and average absolute error over the 15 base-corpus "can" cases as the interference measure.
- result: Humans "are still capable of operating more than 50% correct recall" of AB after AC learning reaches asymptote, whereas the network shows "virtually complete abolition" of AB before AC rises above 0%. Focused learning learns penguin much faster but makes the network treat all animals (and to a lesser extent plants) as swimming non-fliers. Interference is largest on output units where the old answer differs from the penguin's, and fish performance slightly IMPROVES. Interleaved learning is slower per exposure but produces "very little interference". Three principles: (1) structure is found by gradual interleaved learning; (2) fast learning in a structured network gives catastrophic interference; (3) new material can be added without interference if it is interleaved with the old.
- limits: The networks are tiny and abstract ("not detailed neural models"). The penguin example is schema-INCONSISTENT on purpose; Kumaran et al. 2016 later note that schema-consistent material can be learned fast without interference. The paper itself proposes that reducing pattern overlap (sparse codes) cuts interference only at the cost of losing shared structure.
- borrow: (1) The focused vs interleaved LoRA contrast is the base design for T3. Fine-tune a 7-8B model on new facts alone (focused), on new facts mixed with old data (real replay), and on new facts mixed with model-generated data ("dreamed" replay). Score per-item interference on the old set, using their unit-level finding to predict WHERE interference lands: items whose correct answer shares a slot with the new item's answer. (2) Their interference profile (harm concentrated where answers conflict, small benefit where they agree) is a specific signature to check in LLM forgetting curves, beyond average accuracy. (3) For our "consistency lives in the transcript" result: CLS says the transcript (hippocampus) holds the episode precisely BECAUSE writing it into the weights quickly would overwrite structure. So a fast LoRA write of the model's own transcript answers (T2 consolidation) should produce interference on unrelated judgments unless interleaved with the model's other judgments. That is directly testable.
- next_steps_it_suggests: Within the paper: gradual consolidation reflects a design principle, and it calls for study of how the speed of neocortical learning varies with age, species and task. (Update: Kumaran et al. 2016.)
- already_run_check: none on LLMs. It is the conceptual origin of replay-based continual learning (T3), run only on toy networks.
- transfers: yes. A claim about gradient-trained distributed networks, so it applies directly to LoRA fine-tuning. Interference under focused vs interleaved LoRA on a 7-8B model is a $0 laptop test. Its hippocampal anatomy is not needed for that test.
