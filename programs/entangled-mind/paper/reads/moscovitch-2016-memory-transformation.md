- what_tested: A review of the trace-transformation hypothesis set against standard consolidation theory (SCT). Detailed, context-specific episodic memories stay hippocampus-dependent at ANY age. With time and experience many memories are TRANSFORMED into gist- or schema-like versions held in neocortex (vmPFC, anterior temporal lobe) that lose context and detail. The two versions can coexist, and a reminder can restore specificity (and hippocampal dependence). It also covers the vmPFC as a hub for schemas, with schema distortion and confabulation after vmPFC damage.
- on_whom: Cited human amnesic patients (hippocampal lesions, transient global amnesia), human fMRI of autobiographical memory, and rodent context-fear studies with lesions, early-gene expression and optogenetics. No new data.
- how_scored: Cited measures: autobiographical detail, vividness, viewpoint specificity and recollection; contextual specificity of fear (freezing in the training context vs a novel one) at short vs long delay; lesion dependence.
- result: No pooled numbers in the sections read. Qualitative findings: (1) rodent context memories lose contextual specificity by about a month after learning, alongside less hippocampal and more ACC/prelimbic dependence. (2) A reminder a day before a long-delay test, or periodic reactivation of the context, restores or maintains contextual specificity and makes the memory hippocampus-dependent again ("system reconsolidation"). (3) After hippocampal damage, gist and semantic memory for personal events, public events and well-known stories is relatively spared, while perceptual detail and recollection are lost at all ages. (4) Laboratory episodic memory shows the same shift with delays of 1 to 6 weeks.
- limits: The SCT camp argues that hippocampal activity during remote recall reflects re-encoding, which the authors call "difficult to refute". The conditions that make recollection survive vs degrade to familiarity during sleep "have yet to be elucidated". Many claims rest on patient case series and correlational fMRI.
