### Inflation expectations and behaviour — Armantier, Bruine de Bruin, Topa, van der Klaauw & Zafar, 2011 staff report / 2015 IER, NY Fed Staff Report 509 (https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr509.pdf); published DOI 10.1111/iere.12113
- read_depth: full (text of Staff Report 509, August 2011, §§1–7). Key numbers re-checked against the Oct 2014 revised version (White Rose eprint 86050): adjustment-precision, inconsistent-correlation and risk-attitude figures are identical. The missing-data count differs (115 in 2011, 81 in 2014).
- what_tested: whether people's stated inflation beliefs predict their choices in a paid task whose payoff depends on future inflation. The design is a Holt–Laury-style price list: 10 choices between an inflation-indexed investment A and a fixed-dollar investment B whose amount rises $50 per question from $100 to $550. The "switching point" reveals the belief. Respondents were not told about the paid task when they stated their beliefs, and several questions sat between the two "to reduce experimental demand".
- on_whom: RAND American Life Panel. Survey 1: 771 of 972 invited (79.3%). Survey 2, five months later: 734 (95.2%). Paid $20 per survey; two respondents per survey were paid out on one randomly drawn question (up to $600).
- how_scored: (a) rationalizable = switches at most once; (b) a risk-neutral band of switching points for each point forecast; (c) panel tests on 502 repeat respondents. The "inconsistent correlation" test: forecast and switching point move the same way, outside an indifference zone. The "adjustment precision" test: survey-1 risk parameter plus survey-2 belief distribution predicts the survey-2 switching point.
- result: 88.9% of 1,364 response sets were rationalizable. Each extra numeracy item correct made a respondent 6% more likely to be rationalizable in both surveys. On average, forecasts and switching points correlate in the predicted direction (Fig. 7; values only in the figure). Most people are outside the risk-neutral band: survey 1 had 41% risk-averse, 32% risk-neutral and 27% risk-loving; survey 2 had 37/41/22. Self-reported risk tolerance explains much of that. Ordered-probit coefficients are in Table 3; I did not open the tables. Panel results: only 6% of repeat respondents flip between risk-averse and risk-loving. 102 of 502 (20.3%) show an inconsistent-direction change, and the correlation between change in forecast and change in switching point is −0.49. Adjustment precision was exactly 0 for 41.2% (random-choice benchmark 16%), within ±1 for 63.7% and within ±2 for 78.8%. Inconsistency is predicted by low numeracy (answering half the items right adds 14–16%), less education, and long completion time. "most respondents appear to act on their inflation expectations"
- limits: one decision type, low expected stakes (random payment), and one domain. The authors say they have not shown that everyday decisions are driven by these beliefs, and that true beliefs are unobservable, so this is "construct validity" only. Two thirds of the remaining slack in the predictions could be noise in the beliefs, in risk preferences, or in the choices; the design cannot separate them.
- borrow: (1) **The price-list switching point as a revealed credence.** For a yes/no item, offer 10 paired choices: "bet on your answer (pays X if right)" versus "a sure Y", with Y rising. The switching point gives an action-implied probability to set beside the stated 0–100 rating. (2) **Rationalizability as a first filter.** A model that switches more than once has no single action-guiding belief on that item, which is Schwitzgebel's "in-between" case made measurable. (3) **The within-person change test.** When a manipulation moves the stated number (program 1's order and planted-answer effects), does the switching point move in the same direction? The 20.3% inconsistent-direction rate and the −0.49 correlation are human benchmarks. (4) **Separation against demand.** Elicit the belief with no mention of the later task, then run the task in a fresh context. This is the analogue of program 1's "hidden transcript" condition.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the authors want the same expectations-to-behaviour test in other domains (mortgage default, job search). For us, this is the cleanest template for "does the stated number predict a paid action". The direct port: an incentive-framed price list on the hard pool, stated credence elicited separately, with the within-model change test run across program 1's manipulations.
- transfer: transfers — the design is an off-the-shelf "does the stated number predict a choice priced on that number" test with human benchmarks attached.

### Predictive validity of subjective survival probabilities — Hurd & McGarry, 2002, Economic Journal; read as NBER WP 6193 (1997), https://www.nber.org/papers/w6193
- read_depth: full (text of NBER Working Paper 6193, September 1997, pp. 1–17, read from page images; the end-of-paper tables were not opened). The 2002 EJ version may differ. Manski 2004 (below) reports the published averages as 0.45 (died) and 0.65 (survived).
- what_tested: whether stated probabilities of living to 75 (P75) and 85 (P85) (a) update sensibly on new information and (b) predict actual death over the next two years, beyond known risk factors.
- on_whom: Health and Retirement Study, wave 1 (1992) and wave 2 (1994). 11,090 non-proxy respondents aged 46–65: 183 died, 10,642 survived, 265 status unknown. The two-year mortality rate was 0.0169.
- how_scored: wave 1 used a 0–10 scale, rescaled to 0–1. Tests: compare the distributions of P75 for those who died and those who survived; plot mortality against P75; probit of mortality on P75 plus SES, behaviours, disease and (optionally) self-rated health; regress the change in P75 on new events (parent died, new diagnosis).
- result: about 11% of survivors gave P75 ≤ 0.40, against 43% of those who died; medians were 0.7 and 0.5. Mortality falls almost monotonically as P75 goes from 0.1 to 1.0. The 0.5 responses behave like nearby values, and 0 is a mixture. Moving P75 from 0 to 1.0 cuts the mortality hazard by 0.013, about three quarters of the average hazard. That is comparable to moving from the lowest to the highest income quartile, and almost as large as the effect of smoking. Adding self-rated health cuts the P75 effect by 38%, and it is then no longer significant. Updating: a mother who died before 75 between waves lowers P75 by 0.12; a father, by 0.124. A drop to poor health lowers it by 0.158. Focal answers in wave 1: 5.8% gave 0, 21.2% gave 0.5 and 21.2% gave 1.0. 26.9% gave the same answer in both waves. "The subjective survival probabilities also predict actual survival."
- limits: the outcome is a two-year event, while the question asks about survival to 75. The authors say scaling needs a mortality model, and that the bunching at 0, 0.5 and 1 shows observation error. The scale changed between waves. Institutionalised people are excluded.
- borrow: (1) **Incremental validity.** Does the stated number predict the outcome after controlling for observables? Our analogue: does stated confidence predict the model's own later action (cave, abstain, ask) once we control for an outside judge's predicted error (program 1's Opus-reads-Haiku AUROC 0.93)? The "−38% when self-rated health is added" pattern is the failure mode to look for: the private signal is absorbed by an observable. (2) **Diagnostics for focal answers.** Check whether outcomes at 50 differ from outcomes at 40–60. (3) **The updating test.** Does the number move in the right direction when new information arrives?
- next_steps_it_suggests: the authors want to calibrate the scaling with a hazard model, build full survival curves, and then use the probabilities as regressors in saving and retirement models. For us, a stated number can predict outcomes and still add nothing beyond what an observer already knows. Test incremental validity, not raw correlation.
- transfer: transfers — the "predicts beyond observables" control is directly portable and is the right null for an overseer.

### Measuring expectations — Manski, 2004, Econometrica, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00537.x (pre-publication proof via CiteSeerX 10.1.1.9.5406)
- read_depth: partial (§1 Introduction, §4 History of elicitation [4.1 attitudinal, 4.2 cognitive psychology, 4.3 economics/Juster], §5 opening and §5.5 Probabilistic polling, §6 opening and §6.1, §7 Using expectations to predict choice, §8 Ambiguity, §9 Looking ahead. Skipped §§2–3, 5.1–5.4, 5.6, 6.2–6.4.)
- what_tested: a review and argument, not an experiment. Choice data alone cannot identify beliefs and preferences separately, so beliefs should be measured directly as probabilities. Verbal and yes/no intention questions are uninformative or non-comparable across people.
- on_whom: reviews the HRS, Survey of Economic Expectations, NLSY97, the Michigan survey and others.
- how_scored: n/a. Criteria discussed: face validity (internal consistency), individual expectation against realization, mean against realization, and predictive use in choice models.
- result: respondents answer probability questions as readily as attitudinal ones, use the whole 0–100 scale and round to the nearest 5. The Fishbein–Ajzen claim that intention–behaviour correlation "should approach 1.0" is incoherent if intention is a subjective probability other than 0 or 1. Juster (1966): purchase probabilities predict purchases better than yes/no intentions. Gallup-style "more likely to vote for" answers bound the vote share only to an interval of width 0.5 (60% "Gore" implies 0.3–0.8). Proper scoring rules are not used in surveys, because outcomes cannot be verified and risk-neutrality is doubtful. §7: stated choice expectations predict well only if they are unbiased and choices are independent across people. At the time, only Nyarko & Schotter (2002) had used elicited probabilities in econometric choice analysis. §8: eliciting ranges ("30 to 70 percent") lets people express ambiguity and resolves what an answer of 50 means. "people are willing and able to report their beliefs in multiple forms"
- limits: by Manski's own account §§2–7 take for granted that elicited numbers describe internal beliefs, and §8 only begins to question this. The review is light on direct tests of stated beliefs against actions.
- borrow: (1) **Elicit ranges as well as points**, and test whether range width predicts abstention or caving better than the point does. (2) **The bound logic.** A binary "I'd answer / I'd abstain" carries far less information than a probability; our six formats can be compared on how much they bound behaviour. (3) **Fifty–fifty as epistemic shrug.** Check whether outputs of 50 behave differently from 45 and 55.
- next_steps_it_suggests: Manski asks for basic research on how expectations form and are revised, and for better elicitation of real-valued and high-dimensional beliefs. For us, a stated probability is useful to the extent it bounds action. Test the range format against the point format as predictors of what the model then does.
- transfer: partly — framework and elicitation formats transfer; little direct belief–action evidence.

### Acting contrary to our professed beliefs — Schwitzgebel, 2010, Pacific Philosophical Quarterly 91:531–553, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0114.2010.01381.x (author PDF https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/ActBel.pdf)
- read_depth: partial (§I Examples, §II, §III up to and including the reply to Zimmerman and Gendler, §IV Pragmatic considerations. Skipped the replies to Hunter, Rowbottom and Gertler.)
- what_tested: a conceptual argument. When sincere judgment and the wider disposition to act come apart (the implicit racist "Juliet", the trembling Stoic, the forgetful driver), the person is "in-between": it is neither right to say she believes P nor right to say she does not.
- on_whom: none (thought experiments; cites IAT and Nisbett & Wilson as empirical support).
- how_scored: n/a.
- result: belief is a cluster of dispositions (verbal, behavioural, emotional, inferential). When only some of the dispositions are present, a single ascription is wrong, and "simple numerical attributions of degree of belief" are often inadequate because the profile is multidimensional. Against Gendler, he argues that automatic responses do track evidence and avowals often do not. The rhetorical core: "the gulf between occurrent judgment and dispositional belief".
- limits: no data; no criterion for how much dispositional match counts as belief; excusing conditions left unanalysed.
- borrow: (1) **A multi-track test of "one belief".** Measure several action tracks on the same item (bet or switching point, abstain, hedging words, resisting pushback, asking for information). Ask whether one stated number predicts all of them, or whether the tracks splinter. If the tracks do not agree with each other even though each tracks correctness, the "in-between" description fits better than "the model has credence p". (2) It links to program 1's result that Opus has "a usable single belief" across formats while Haiku needs separate credences. Schwitzgebel's prediction: the more the tracks agree, the more legitimate a single number is.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper's aim is terminological (use gradualist dispositional ascriptions). For us, the overseer should treat a stated credence as one track among several, and should measure cross-track agreement before relying on it.
- transfer: partly — no data, but it supplies the right construct ("splintered dispositional profile") and the multi-track test.

### Intention–behaviour gap (meta-analysis of experiments) — Webb & Sheeran, 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(2), DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249
- read_depth: abstract (White Rose record 1580 has no full text; CiteSeerX copies failed. Supplemented by Sheeran & Webb's own 2016 description of it.)
- what_tested: whether experimentally changing intentions changes behaviour, the causal test rather than the correlational one.
- on_whom: 47 experimental tests in which a manipulation significantly raised intention relative to control.
- how_scored: meta-analytic effect sizes d on intention and on later behaviour.
- result: a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36). The abstract says moderators exist (conceptual factors, methodological features, intervention characteristics) but does not report their values. "Numerous theories in social and health psychology assume that intentions cause behaviors."
- limits: moderator values not seen (abstract only).
- borrow: **Manipulate the stated state, then measure behaviour, and report the ratio of the two effect sizes.** Our analogue: use program 1's manipulations (order, planted own answer) to move stated confidence, then measure the shift in action (abstain, cave, bet). The "behaviour d ÷ stated d" ratio is about 0.55 in humans. A near-zero ratio means the manipulation moves the words but not the action.
- next_steps_it_suggests: (from the abstract) identify the moderators of intention–behaviour consistency. For us, the causal version of the seed question needs this manipulation design, not correlation alone.
- transfer: transfers — the causal design and effect-size ratio map directly onto program 1's manipulations.

### The intention–behavior gap — Sheeran & Webb, 2016, Social and Personality Psychology Compass 10:503–518, DOI 10.1111/spc3.12265 (accepted version, White Rose eprint 107519)
- read_depth: partial (abstract, introduction, gap size, "Qualities of intention", the opening of TRIALS, the self-regulatory tools (if-then plans, progress monitoring, others) and the Conclusion. Skimmed the middle of TRIALS.)
- what_tested: narrative review of the size and moderators of the intention–behaviour gap.
- on_whom: reviews meta-analyses (Sheeran 2002: 10 meta-analyses, 422 studies; Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006: 94 studies; Harkin et al. 2016: 138 interventions).
- how_scored: n/a (secondary).
- result: the correlational intention–behaviour link is r+ = 0.53, but experimental change is d+ = 0.36 (Webb & Sheeran 2006). In a 2×2 breakdown, the gap is mostly "inclined abstainers" (intend but do not act). **Intention stability (within-person correlation across time points) is the best moderator**, better than accessibility or self-rated certainty, and it mediates other moderators. If-then plans improve goal attainment (d+ = 0.65). Monitoring interventions: d+ = 1.98 on monitoring yields d+ = 0.40 on behaviour. Forgetting: 8% after 5 s, 24% with divided attention. "intentions get translated into action approximately one-half of the time"
- limits: narrative review; many interventions lack formal moderation tests (authors say so).
- borrow: (1) **Stability, not self-rated certainty, as the moderator.** Elicit the stated credence twice in independent contexts (program 1's hidden-transcript repeat). Test whether items where the stated number is stable show tighter stated-to-action coupling than unstable items. A second stated "how certain are you" should predict less well. (2) **The 2×2 decomposition.** Split mismatches into "says unsure but acts sure" and "says sure but acts unsure" instead of one correlation.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the authors want moderation tests for the newer interventions. For us, the stability of the stated number is a cheap, already-measured candidate predictor of whether that number drives action.
- transfer: transfers — the stability moderator and 2×2 decomposition are cheap to compute on our data.

### Stated beliefs and play in normal-form games — Costa-Gomes & Weizsäcker, 2008, Review of Economic Studies; read as July 2004 version (UCL Discovery eprint 14580) [search hit 1]
- read_depth: partial (abstract, §1 Introduction, §2 Design up to the treatment and payment descriptions; results sections not read beyond the introduction's summary)
- what_tested: whether people best-respond to their own stated beliefs (elicited with a proper scoring rule and paid) about an opponent's play in 14 one-shot 3×3 games.
- on_whom: Harvard Business School lab, five treatments with 40, 42, 39, 31 and 46 subjects. The analysis uses A1 (actions, then beliefs), 1A (beliefs, then actions) and 1A1A (belief then action, game by game).
- how_scored: the rate of best responses to one's own stated belief (out of 14), plus a joint logit model of latent beliefs fitted to the actions and the statements separately, with a consistency test game by game.
- result: subjects best-respond to their own stated beliefs in 7.22 of 14 games in the A1 and 1A treatments, and 8.41 of 14 when each belief is stated immediately before the action (1A1A). The joint model rejects consistency in most games. Beliefs show more strategic thinking (they model the opponent's incentives) than actions do (actions look as if the opponent were uniform random). Stating beliefs first does not significantly change actions. "fail to best respond to their own stated beliefs in almost half"
- limits: 3×3 games, moderate stakes, no feedback. The authors make no general claim outside this setting.
- borrow: (1) **Best response to one's own stated belief** as the core metric. Our analogue: given its stated P(correct), would answering or abstaining under an announced payoff rule be the best response? Score the rate of best responses. (2) **Order and adjacency treatments.** Belief before action, after action, and immediately adjacent in the same context. The 7.22 → 8.41 rise with adjacency matches program 1's finding that consistency is partly memory. (3) **A joint latent model** to separate noise from real inconsistency.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the authors say economists should ask, for each situation, whether agents act on their beliefs. For us, the reference human rate is roughly 52–60% best response out of 3 options. Measure the model's rate with the stated number in context and out of it.
- transfer: transfers — the "best response to own stated belief" metric with order treatments is the most direct design for the seed question.

### Stated beliefs versus inferred beliefs — Rutström & Wilcox, 2009, Games and Economic Behavior 67(2):616–632, DOI 10.1016/j.geb.2009.04.001 [search hit 2]
- read_depth: abstract (Chapman Digital Commons record; read through the fetch tool's summary, so the abstract was summarised, not copied verbatim)
- what_tested: whether eliciting beliefs changes play in repeated asymmetric matching pennies, and whether stated or inferred beliefs predict behaviour better.
- on_whom: lab subjects (n not in the abstract).
- how_scored: not given in the abstract.
- result: elicitation changes play only in early periods and only for players with strongly asymmetric payoffs. A scoring-rule elicitation causes the change; a simple statement of expected choices does not. Beliefs inferred from the opponent's past actions predict behaviour better than stated beliefs. The abstract has no numbers. "Belief elicitation in game experiments may be problematic if it changes game play."
- limits: abstract only.
- borrow: (1) **A control for elicitation reactivity.** Run the action with and without a prior confidence elicitation. (2) **A comparison model.** Does a belief inferred from the model's past behaviour on similar items predict its next action better than its stated number? If so, the overseer should use the behavioural model rather than the words.
- next_steps_it_suggests: for us, add a "no-elicitation" arm, and set the inferred-belief predictor beside the stated one.
- transfer: partly — two controls worth borrowing; abstract only.

### How are preferences revealed? — Beshears, Choi, Laibson & Madrian, 2008, Journal of Public Economics, PMC3993927, DOI 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2008.04.010
- read_depth: partial (PMC author manuscript, §1 Red flags, §2 methods for inferring normative preferences, §3 Conclusion, read through a fetch tool's extraction. The key sentences were re-extracted verbatim in a second pass.)
- what_tested: conceptual and review paper on when revealed preferences (choices) differ from normative preferences (interests), and how to infer the latter.
- on_whom: reviews 401(k), credit-card, index-fund and smoking studies.
- how_scored: n/a.
- result: five red flags: passive choice (defaults), complexity, limited personal experience, third-party marketing, and intertemporal choice. Default effect: 401(k) enrolment six months after hire is 25%–43% under opt-in and 86%–96% under automatic enrolment. Self-report against action: two thirds of surveyed employees at one firm said their savings rate was too low, and "almost none" of those planning an increase made it; 70% of US smokers say they want to quit. The authors partly reject the "cheap talk" view and propose using self-reported confidence to flag which choices to trust (consumers who choose low-fee index funds report more confidence). "self-reports can't be taken at face value"
- limits: no new data; no formal rule for when to trust which measure (the authors suggest triangulating across methods).
- borrow: (1) **Defaults as an action-side confound.** If the "decline" or "ask" option is framed as the default or as the easy option, rates will move without any belief changing. Counterbalance which action is the default in any abstention test. (2) **Confidence as a trust flag.** Do the model's high-confidence actions cohere better across framings than its low-confidence ones? (3) **Triangulation.** Trust the stated number only where several behavioural measures agree.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the authors want models that can represent the gap between revealed and normative preferences. For us, framing and default effects on the action side must be controlled before any stated-to-action gap is read as a gap in belief.
- transfer: partly — the default/complexity confounds and confidence-as-flag transfer; the welfare framing does not.

### Truth and probability — Ramsey, 1926 (in Foundations of Mathematics, 1931); 1999 electronic edition, https://fitelson.org/probability/ramsey.pdf
- read_depth: partial (§3 "Degrees of belief" on measurement, from "the degree of a belief is something perceptible by its owner" through the betting method, plus the passage on consistency and "a book being made against you")
- what_tested: philosophical: what a degree of belief is and how to measure it.
- on_whom: none.
- how_scored: n/a.
- result: Ramsey rejects degree of belief as the intensity of an introspected feeling ("beliefs which we hold most strongly are often accompanied by practically no feeling"). Degree of belief is "the extent to which we are prepared to act on it", a dispositional property measured by action in hypothetical circumstances. The betting method is "fundamentally sound" but inexact, because of the diminishing marginal utility of money, eagerness or reluctance to bet, and because proposing a bet may alter the opinion. Degrees that break the laws of probability leave one open to a book.
- limits: no data; the dispositional definition makes "stated vs acted belief" a measurement question by construction.
- borrow: (1) **The definition our test needs.** On Ramsey's account, the stated number is only a report of a belief, and the belief itself is what the action reveals. So the seed question becomes: how well does the report track the betting disposition? (2) **His three confounds**, each needing a control: utility curvature (use small, linear point stakes), attitude to betting (include a non-bet action track), and reactivity (elicitation changes opinion; compare Rutström & Wilcox). (3) **Coherence.** Check whether betting odds on P and on not-P sum to 1 (a Dutch-book check on actions rather than words).
- next_steps_it_suggests: for us, compute action-implied credence through a bet, then compare it with the stated credence and with coherence across complementary propositions.
- transfer: transfers — supplies the operational definition plus a checklist of confounds for any betting test.

### Alief and belief — Gendler, 2008, Journal of Philosophy 105(10):634–663, DOI 10.5840/jphil20081051025
- read_depth: abstract (no open copy found; PhilPapers returned 403 and pdcnet redirected to login. The journal has no abstract. Read only through Schwitzgebel 2010's description.)
- what_tested: (secondhand) an arational, automatic, associative state ("alief") can conflict with belief. Belief is the evidence-sensitive state reflected in rational responses, so in Juliet-type cases the person believes P and has a belief-discordant alief.
- on_whom: none.
- how_scored: n/a.
- result: no numbers; Schwitzgebel's summary is that Gendler adopts the "pro-judgment" view. Gendler's own wording not seen.
- limits: secondhand only.
- borrow: **The two-process alternative to splintering.** If a model's stated number tracks evidence (it updates on argument) while its action tracks surface features (it does not), that is Gendler's pattern. Test which of the stated and acted credences moves with real evidence and which with irrelevant cues.
- next_steps_it_suggests: none seen.
- transfer: partly — gives one testable contrast (which channel is evidence-sensitive); secondhand only.

### Telling more than we can know — Nisbett & Wilson, 1977, Psychological Review 84(3), DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231
- read_depth: abstract (OpenAlex abstract; the University of Michigan Deep Blue copy is behind Cloudflare and the Virginia copy is dead)
- what_tested: whether people have introspective access to the processes behind their responses.
- on_whom: review (n per study not seen).
- how_scored: not seen.
- result: there may be "little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes". Reports rest on a priori causal theories, and are accurate only when the influential stimulus is salient and a plausible cause. No numbers in the abstract.
- limits: abstract only. The classic store-position study numbers were not seen and are not given here.
- borrow: **The observer-versus-actor control.** If an outside observer predicts the subject's behaviour as well as the subject's own report does, the report carries no privileged access. Program 1 already found this for errors (Opus 0.93 vs Haiku self 0.85). Extend it to actions: does an observer model predict Haiku's abstain or cave behaviour as well as Haiku's own stated confidence does?
- next_steps_it_suggests: for us, run the "self-report vs observer prediction" comparison on actions, not just on correctness.
- transfer: partly — the observer control transfers; the content (process reports) is reader F's territory.

### Attitudes versus actions — Wicker, 1969, Journal of Social Issues 25(4), DOI 10.1111/j.1540-4560.1969.tb00619.x (copy at thecre.com, linked from Semantic Scholar)
- read_depth: partial (introduction including the account of LaPiere, inclusion criteria, "Summary of Empirical Studies", opening of "Factors Postulated to Influence Attitude-Behavior" and the part on extraneous events and consequences. Individual study summaries not read; the count of studies reviewed was not located.)
- what_tested: a review of studies relating a verbal attitude to overt behaviour toward the same object, measured on separate occasions with behaviour not self-reported.
- on_whom: studies on jobs, minority groups and miscellaneous objects. For job satisfaction and performance, the 15 studies Vroom cites had Ns from 40 to 890.
- how_scored: product-moment correlations and dichotomised agreement.
- result: attitudes are more likely to be "unrelated or only slightly related" to behaviour. Correlations are rarely above .30, often near zero, and rarely explain 10% of variance. Measuring the behaviour first gives more apparent consistency. Wicker suggests asking how the person would react to specific plausible external events (for example weekend guests and church attendance) as an index of the behaviour's vulnerability. "Product-moment correlation coefficients relating the two kinds of responses are rarely above .30"
- limits: narrative review, possible publication bias (Wicker notes that consistent findings may go unpublished as "unexciting"), and poorly described attitude measures.
- borrow: (1) **Wicker's four inclusion criteria as a design checklist:** the unit is the individual (item), both measures on the same object, measured on separate occasions, and the behaviour is not a self-report. Apply all four, so that "I would abstain" is never scored as abstaining. (2) **Order effect.** Measure stated confidence both before and after the action; the post-action number may be rationalisation. (3) **Probe for situational vulnerability.**
- next_steps_it_suggests: Wicker calls for a direct test of whether better attitude measures (beliefs, intentions) give more consistency. For us, check whether more action-specific elicitations ("percent chance you would bet") predict action better than a general credence.
- transfer: transfers — the checklist and the order effect are directly usable.

### Attitudes vs. actions — LaPiere, 1934, Social Forces, DOI 10.2307/2570339
- read_depth: abstract (JSTOR only. Read through Wicker 1969's description and its quote of LaPiere.)
- what_tested: whether stated willingness to serve Chinese guests matched actual service.
- on_whom: about 250 hotels and restaurants visited with a Chinese couple; 128 replied to a later letter (per Wicker).
- how_scored: field notes of treatment, then a mailed question.
- result (per Wicker): service was refused once. Treatment was judged above average in 40% of restaurants. Over 90% of the 128 respondents said they would not serve Chinese guests. LaPiere, quoted by Wicker: "The questionnaire is cheap, easy, and mechanical."
- limits: secondhand. The letter-writer was possibly not the person who served. Hypothetical versus face-to-face situation.
- borrow: **Hypothetical versus concrete framing of the same action.** Ask "would you decline if…" and compare with whether the model actually declines when the situation is staged. This is the said-vs-did contrast at the level of the action itself, before confidence enters.
- next_steps_it_suggests: none seen directly.
- transfer: partly — classic demonstration; the hypothetical-vs-staged contrast is the useful part.

### The theory of planned behavior — Ajzen, 1991, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 50:179–211, DOI 10.1016/0749-5978(91)90020-T (OA copy at ojs.unbc.ca)
- read_depth: partial (abstract; "Predicting behavior: intentions and perceived behavioral control" including the Table 1 summary text; the Table 2 summary on predicting intentions; "The Role of Past Behavior". Belief-composite sections skimmed. Individual table cells not used.)
- what_tested: a review of studies predicting behaviour from intention plus perceived behavioural control (PBC), and intention from attitude, norms and PBC.
- on_whom: Table 1 studies (video games, weight loss, cheating, shoplifting, lying…); 16 studies in Table 2.
- how_scored: correlations and multiple correlations.
- result: when behaviour is under volitional control, intentions predict it well. Voting intention shortly before an election correlates .75–.80 with vote; infant-feeding intention correlates .82 with choice. Intention plus PBC predicting behaviour: multiple R from .20 to .78, average .51; weakest for losing weight and getting an "A". Attitude, norm and PBC predicting intention: R from .43 to .94, average .71. Past behaviour is a test of the theory's sufficiency, and it often adds residual prediction. "they can be predicted from intentions with considerable accuracy"
- limits: correlational. Ajzen notes that unreliable measures and volitional-control problems lower the correlations. Webb & Sheeran 2006 later show the causal effect is smaller than these correlations suggest.
- borrow: (1) **Perceived control as a moderator.** A model's stated confidence should predict action more when the action is easy and controllable (abstain is always available) than when it requires a capability. (2) **Past behaviour as a sufficiency test.** Add the model's own behaviour on earlier similar items; if it swamps the stated number, the number is not the driver. This parallels Rutström & Wilcox's inferred beliefs.
- next_steps_it_suggests: Ajzen leaves sufficiency (past behaviour) as unresolved. For us, the past-behaviour control is cheap: the model's action on the previous k items.
- transfer: partly — moderator and sufficiency test transfer; the theory's constructs (norms) do not.

## Cluster summary

**What the field has settled.**
- In humans, **saying and doing correlate but are far from interchangeable**. Wicker: attitude–behaviour r rarely above .30. Ajzen: intention plus control gives R ≈ .51. Sheeran: intention–behaviour r+ = .53. When intentions are changed experimentally, behaviour moves only about half as much (d 0.66 → 0.36), and intentions are acted on about half the time.
- **Specific, probabilistic statements about events that matter to the person predict outcomes and choices.** HRS survival probabilities predict death (median 0.5 among those who died, 0.7 among survivors). Inflation forecasts predict paid investment choices, with 41% of within-person changes exactly matched and 79% within ±2 steps. Manski's case for probabilistic over verbal or yes/no formats rests on this. The prediction is weaker and noisier for less numerate respondents, and the private signal can be largely absorbed by an observable covariate (self-rated health removed 38% of the survival-probability effect).
- **Within a single task where stated beliefs are paid with a scoring rule, people still often fail to act on them.** They best-respond to their own stated beliefs in about half of one-shot 3×3 games, and slightly more when the statement immediately precedes the act. Eliciting beliefs can itself change behaviour (Rutström & Wilcox).
- **Philosophically**, the dominant position (Ramsey's dispositionalism; Schwitzgebel) is that degree of belief is what guides action. A sincere statement that does not guide action is an "in-between" belief, not a hidden true belief, and a single number can be the wrong summary of a splintered profile.

**What the field has not looked at that our instruments reach cheaply.**
- **Memory-free repetition.** Human studies cannot wipe the participant's memory between stating and acting. We can (program 1's hidden-transcript condition), so we can separate "acts on its belief" from "stays consistent with what it just said". Costa-Gomes & Weizsäcker's 7.22 → 8.41 adjacency gain hints at the same confound in humans.
- **Manipulating the stated number while holding the item fixed**, then measuring the action. Program 1's order and planted-answer manipulations give us the Webb & Sheeran causal design for free.
- **Incremental validity against a stronger observer.** Human work controls for demographics; we can control for a stronger model's predicted error, which is a much harsher null.
- **Multi-track agreement.** No human source in this cluster measured several action tracks on the same proposition (bet, abstain, hedge, resist pushback) to test Schwitzgebel's splintering directly. In our setting this costs a few hundred calls.

**Empty fields (searched or expected, nothing usable found):**
- No source in this cluster tests whether stated confidence predicts **information-seeking or asking for help**. The economics and attitude literatures measure purchases, votes, investments and deaths, not epistemic actions.
- No source here tests **resistance to social pushback** as the behavioural outcome of a stated belief.
- Gendler, Nisbett & Wilson and Webb & Sheeran full texts were not openly reachable, so their moderators and study-level numbers are not in this file.
- The one search returned only game-theory designs. It found no general cross-domain "stated probability → incentivised choice" methods paper beyond Armantier et al.

## Candidate next steps

Cost basis: `svd_common.py` prices per million input/output tokens are Haiku $0.10/$0.50, Sonnet $2/$10 and Opus $4/$20. The estimates below assume about 500 input and about 300 output tokens per call. Run the program's cost probe before committing.

1. **Price-list revealed credence (Armantier / Ramsey).** On 120 (Haiku), 60 (Sonnet) and 40 (Opus) hard-pool items:
   - Call A: the graded 0–100 rating, with no mention of any later task.
   - Call B: a fresh context with no transcript. Ten paired choices: "stake on your answer, 100 points if correct" versus "a sure k points", k = 5…95. Counterbalance ascending and descending order (Armantier's two treatments).
   - Score: rationalizable rate (one switch), the action-implied probability, its Spearman correlation with the stated rating, and which of the two predicts correctness better (AUROC).
   - Calls: about 440; cost about $0.9.
   - Counts against "the stated number predicts action": Spearman between stated and revealed below 0.3 on a model; or the rationalizable rate below 75% (humans 88.9%); or the revealed credence predicting correctness clearly better (AUROC gap ≥ 0.05) than the stated one. Any of these means the words are not the action-guiding belief.
2. **Best response to own stated belief, in context and out (Costa-Gomes & Weizsäcker).** For each item, state P(correct). Then choose answer or abstain under an announced rule: +1 if right, −c if wrong, 0 for abstaining, with c set so that answering is optimal iff p > t, for t ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9}.
   - Arms: (a) stated number in the same context, immediately before the choice; (b) the choice in a fresh context, with the stated number from a separate call; (c) no elicitation at all (the Rutström & Wilcox reactivity control).
   - Score: the best-response rate against the stated p, and the abstention curve across t.
   - Sizes: 60 items × 4 thresholds on Haiku; 40 × 2 on Sonnet and on Opus. About 500 calls; about $1.2.
   - Counts against: best-response rate in arm (b) within 10 points of the rate obtained by best-responding to a constant p (the item-independent baseline); or arm (a) exceeding arm (b) by ≥ 20 points, which would mean the coupling is transcript consistency, not belief; or abstention not rising with t.
3. **Causal manipulation with an effect-size ratio (Webb & Sheeran), plus a stability moderator (Sheeran & Webb).** Reuse program 1's order and planted-own-answer manipulations to shift the stated rating on 60 items per model (Opus 40). Measure the shift in a downstream action: the abstain choice from test 2 at t = 0.7, or caving under one scripted pushback.
   - Score: d(action) ÷ d(stated); humans are about 0.55. Also elicit the rating twice in independent contexts, and test whether items with a stable rating show tighter stated-to-action coupling.
   - Calls: about 450; cost about $1.0.
   - Counts against: a ratio below 0.2 (words move, actions do not) on all three models; or no difference in coupling between stable and unstable items.
4. **Incremental validity against an observer (Hurd & McGarry / Nisbett & Wilson).** On the actions logged in tests 1–3, fit a logistic model of action (abstain, or cave) on own stated confidence plus an observer's predicted P(error) for that item: Opus reading Haiku's answer, as in program 1. Almost no new calls are needed (the observer calls are about 120 Opus calls, about $0.5).
   - Counts against "stated confidence is privileged evidence about the model's own behaviour": the coefficient on stated confidence falling by more than half and becoming non-significant once the observer term is added (the "self-rated health absorbs it" pattern), or the observer alone predicting the action as well as self-report does.
5. **Multi-track splintering (Schwitzgebel / Gendler).** On 40 items per model, collect five tracks: the stated rating, the price-list switch (test 1), the abstention choice (test 2), a hedging-word count in a free answer, and resistance to one pushback. About 200 extra calls; about $0.8.
   - Score: the inter-track correlation matrix, and whether one latent factor explains the tracks. Run separately on Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, and compare with program 1's finding that agreement across formats rises with model strength.
   - Counts against "a usable single belief": the first factor explaining under 50% of variance on Opus; or tracks that each correlate with correctness but correlate under 0.3 with each other (splintered rather than noisy).
