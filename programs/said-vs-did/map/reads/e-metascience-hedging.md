# Reader E — Metascience: hedging, stated confidence and what it predicts

Reader E, 2026-11-05. Web reads only; no model API was called. Full texts came from PMC (Dreber 2015, Altmejd 2019, Yang 2020), arXiv HTML (Pei & Jurgens 2021), author preprints on OSF (Camerer 2018, Forsell 2019) and eScholarship (Mellers 2014), and were converted to plain text locally. Hyland 1996 and DellaVigna 2019 were paywalled and are abstract-only. The one 2025–2026 preprint (Thelwall, arXiv 2605.27392) was checked on its arXiv abstract page before it was recorded. Numbers come from the text; values shown only in figures are marked as such.

### Hedging as rhetoric — Hyland, 1996, Applied Linguistics, doi:10.1093/applin/17.4.433
- read_depth: abstract (the OUP page sits behind a Cloudflare check, so the abstract was read through a summarising fetch; no verbatim text, no quote)
- what_tested: what hedges do in research articles. The paper proposes a pragmatic framework that ties hedging to its social and institutional setting.
- on_whom: molecular biology research articles (n = 26, as the summarised abstract reports it)
- how_scored: qualitative discourse analysis
- result: hedges serve caution, deference to colleagues and heading off objections, as well as uncertainty. Hedging cannot be understood apart from its social context. No outcome measure.
- limits: one field, small corpus, no link to whether hedged claims turn out true
- borrow: the key confound for our tests. A hedge can be a social move (politeness, register, deference) and need not report a belief. Any "hedge count predicts X" test needs a register control: the same question asked in a formal-paper versus a casual frame, to check whether hedge rate moves with register while the 0–100 number stays put.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: study hedging across fields and genres. For us: do not read a model's hedges as its confidence until hedge rate is shown to track correctness rather than tone.
- transfer: partly — no data on outcomes, but it gives the reason hedges and confidence could come apart

### Prediction markets on the RPP — Dreber, Pfeiffer, Almenberg, Isaksson, Wilson, Chen, Nosek, Johannesson, 2015, PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.1516179112 (PMC4687569)
- read_depth: full (main text, methods, SI text sections; per-study table skimmed)
- what_tested: whether researchers' beliefs predict which RPP replications succeed. Two ways of eliciting those beliefs: an unincentivised pre-market survey (0–100%) and real-money market trading (LMSR market maker, b = 100).
- on_whom: psychologists from the OSF and RPP email lists. 47 active traders in market set 1 and 45 in set 2, each endowed with $100. 44 RPP studies, 41 with completed replications.
- how_scored: correct if price > 50% and the study replicated (or < 50% and it did not); linear probability model of outcome on price; point-biserial correlation; absolute prediction error
- result: markets were right on 29/41 (71%; binomial P = 0.012). The expected rate given the prices was 69%. Coefficient on price was 0.995 (not different from 1, P = 0.987) with constant −0.167, so prices behave like probabilities. The survey was right on 23/40 (58%, P = 0.429). **The survey weighted by self-reported expertise was right on 20/40 (50%, P = 1.00): stated expertise added nothing.** Point-biserial correlation with outcome was 0.42 for the market (P = 0.006), 0.27 for the survey (P = 0.096) and 0.26 for the weighted survey. Market and survey correlate at r = 0.78. The market's range (13–88%) was wider than the survey's (32–74%). Set 1 got 20/23 right (87%), set 2 got 9/18 (50%) (Fisher P = 0.016); self-reported expertise was lower in set 2 (1.71 vs 1.91). Replicated: 16/41. Median prior p0 8.8%, median p1 56%.
- limits: n = 41. Survey and market come from the same people and are not independent. Outcomes are needed before anything can be scored. Original-study power was estimated post hoc from P values.
- borrow: (1) the **survey versus bet** contrast: the same person's stated probability and their incentivised action, scored against the same outcome. This is our said-vs-did split exactly, in a field that ran it. (2) The expertise-weighting null as a ready control: ask "How well do you know this topic?" in the paper's exact 5-level wording and check whether weighting by it helps. (3) The regression-on-price calibration test (slope 1, intercept 0) as a one-line calibration metric.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: use markets as "decision markets" to choose which studies to replicate, and run markets on many studies but replicate only a random subset. For us: elicit the model's stated number, then a separate betting action, and test which one predicts correctness. That is the said-vs-did question in its native form.
- transfer: transfers — the same two-channel design (statement vs action) we need, with the scoring already worked out

### SSRP: Nature/Science replications with peer beliefs — Camerer, Dreber, Holzmeister, Ho, Huber, Johannesson, Kirchler, Nave, Nosek, Pfeiffer, et al., 2018, Nature Human Behaviour, doi:10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z (OSF preprint 10.31235/osf.io/4hmb6)
- read_depth: partial (OSF preprint main text and figure captions; Supplementary Information sections 3–4 on beliefs not read; the published version is paywalled)
- what_tested: replicability of 21 experiments published in Nature and Science (2010–2015), run as high-powered two-stage replications. Peer beliefs were elicited by survey and prediction market.
- on_whom: 21 studies. The trader count is in the SI, which was not read.
- how_scored: significance in the same direction (primary) plus five other indicators; Spearman correlation of beliefs with outcomes
- result: 13/21 replicated (61.9%). Replication effect size was r = 0.249 against 0.460 in the originals (mean relative effect 46.2%; 74.5% for replicators, 0.3% for non-replicators). Mean market belief was 63.4% (range 23.1–95.5%) and mean survey belief 60.6% (27.8–81.5%). Spearman correlation with outcome was **0.842 for the market and 0.761 for the survey** (both P < 0.001, n = 21); market and survey correlate at 0.845. Original-study p value against replication gave Spearman −0.405 (P = 0.069).
- limits: n = 21 with wide CIs. Beliefs are aggregates (market, mean survey), and individual forecasters are not scored in the main text. The authors say the sample is too small to test what drives replication.
- borrow: the SSRP's 21 hypothesis summaries and outcomes are a small, public, labelled set with a human belief benchmark (ρ = 0.76 survey, 0.84 market). Models could be scored against those benchmarks, with a contamination check because the outcomes are famous.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: pool all large replication projects to find what predicts replication. For us: compare human crowd beliefs with model beliefs on the same items. This record measures crowd prediction; it says nothing about whether one forecaster's stated confidence predicts that forecaster's own actions.
- transfer: partly — strong evidence that stated beliefs predict outcomes when aggregated, but no individual stated-versus-acted measure

### Many Labs 2 forecasts — Forsell, Viganola, Pfeiffer, Almenberg, Wilson, Chen, Nosek, Johannesson, Dreber, 2019, Journal of Economic Psychology, doi:10.1016/j.joep.2018.10.009 (OSF preprint sgjaz)
- read_depth: full (OSF preprint main text, footnotes and concluding remarks; appendix tables skimmed)
- what_tested: survey versus market prediction of 24 Many Labs 2 replications. Two outcomes: binary replication (p < 0.001, same direction) and relative effect size (six-bin markets).
- on_whom: psychologists. 91 completed the survey and 78 of them traded. 24 binary markets and 23 effect-size markets, with 30–56 traders per binary market. Endowment was $50.
- how_scored: correct-rate at the 50% threshold; Spearman correlation with outcome; mean absolute prediction error (Wilcoxon). p < 0.005 is called significant and p < 0.05 suggestive.
- result: 11/24 replicated (46%). Market right on 18/24 (75%, P = 0.023); survey on 16/24 (67%, P = 0.152). Spearman with outcome: **market 0.755, survey 0.731** (both P < 0.0001). Absolute error was 0.354 for the market and 0.394 for the survey (P = 0.003). Both overestimated the replication rate (P = 0.018 and 0.016). **Weighting by self-reported expertise changed almost nothing:** weighted and unweighted surveys correlate at ρ = 0.995 (binary) and 0.980 (effect size), and the 50% call flipped for only one study. Effect-size markets drew little trading and stayed near their 0.75 starting price. The effect-size survey did better: ρ = 0.614 against 0.484 for the market. Pooled across four projects: market 73% (76/104), survey 66% (68/103), mean correlation 0.58 (market) against 0.57 (survey).
- limits: n = 24 (75% power to detect ρ = 0.52 at 0.05). Payouts were settled at closing prices, not at outcomes, so the markets were not incentive-compatible as run. Three original p values were mis-stated to traders. Survey and market come from the same people.
- borrow: (1) The **effect-size market failure** is a "did" channel that failed because the action was too complex. Our betting or acting tasks must be as simple as the binary market, or the action will stay at its default (the analogue of 0.75), a floor effect we would misread as "acts unsure". (2) The finding that the crowd overestimates replication maps onto testing a model for overconfidence on the 300-question pool. (3) Appendix Table A1 holds the 24 hypothesis summaries exactly as traders saw them: ready items.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: separate the binary and effect-size markets, simplify the bins, train traders. For us: any "act on your confidence" task needs a pilot showing the action moves at all before its correlation with stated confidence is read.
- transfer: transfers — the same survey-versus-action comparison, plus a failure mode we would otherwise repeat

### Black-box replication model — Altmejd, Dreber, Forsell, Huber, Imai, Johannesson, Kirchler, Nave, Camerer, 2019, PLoS ONE, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0225826 (PMC6894796)
- read_depth: partial (abstract, methods, results, discussion; supplementary tables not read)
- what_tested: whether objective features of a study (n, effect size, p, interaction vs main effect, citations, author count, and so on) predict replication, compared with market beliefs
- on_whom: 131 study–replication pairs (RPP, EERP, ML1, ML3). Market data for 55 of them. Pre-registered out-of-sample test on SSRP (21).
- how_scored: random forest with nested CV (AUC, accuracy at 0.5); relative effect size (Spearman, RMSE); out-of-sample absolute error compared with market and survey
- result: CV AUC 0.79 and accuracy 70%. The accuracy comes mostly from true negatives: failures were called right 80% of the time, successes 56%. Out-of-sample on SSRP: 71% correct (AUC 0.73); the model's absolute error was 0.43 against 0.30 for the market and 0.35 for the survey (model vs market P = 0.012, n = 21). On the 55 overlapping studies the market was right 65.5%. Interaction effects replicated 8/41 times; other effects 48/90. Original p and effect size correlate with replication at Spearman 0.38 and 0.26.
- limits: small n; the authors call it a proof of concept and warn of overfitting from reusing validation folds. Effect-size prediction is weak (out-of-sample R² 0.07).
- borrow: an objective-feature baseline. Before crediting a model's stated confidence with predicting its own correctness, beat a cheap feature model (question length, topic, base rate). Our analogue: does the 0–100 number add AUROC beyond surface features of the question?
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: more replication data, and models used to guide where replications go. For us: report every stated-confidence AUROC next to a features-only baseline.
- transfer: partly — no stated confidence inside the model, but the right baseline discipline

### Text predicts replication; hedges do not — Yang, Youyou, Uzzi, 2020, PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.1909046117 (PMC7245108)
- read_depth: full (main text, tables, figure captions; SI not read)
- what_tested: whether a paper's narrative text (word2vec paper vectors, ensemble of bagged random forests and logistic models) predicts manual replication outcomes, against reported statistics and against prediction markets. Bias tests on words, including hedging and persuasion language.
- on_whom: trained on 96 RPP papers; tested out of sample on 317 papers (test sets I–IV: 117 psychology, 57 student class-project replications, 18 EERP, 122 economics replications from the Replication Wiki). 413 papers from 80 journals. word2vec trained on 2 million MAG abstracts.
- how_scored: accuracy at a base-rate threshold; top-k precision; comparison on 100 papers that also have market or survey scores
- result: text-only accuracy 0.68 (SD 0.034) against a 0.61 dummy; top-k 0.74. Statistics-only: 0.66 / 0.72. Combined: 0.71 / 0.76. Bag-of-words: 0.60. Out of sample: 0.69 (test set I), 0.65 (II), 0.78 (III), 0.66 (IV). **"Subjective probability language and hedging words/persuasion phrases" showed no difference between replicating and non-replicating papers (t = 0.20, P = 0.84).** Only one word differed: "experimenter" (r = −0.26). Removing stop words or content words did not change accuracy. Unusual n-grams were more frequent in non-replicating papers. Among the 10 most confident predictions of success, the model and the markets each got 90% right. On the 10 most confident failures, the text gives 90% for both while the Fig. 4 caption gives 100% for markets: an internal inconsistency. Citations to the 61 failed and 39 replicated RPP papers do not differ (Fig. 1).
- limits: small training set; the authors call the results preliminary. n-gram features cannot be interpreted. The hedging test is a frequency comparison, not a model of certainty (contrast Pei & Jurgens). Data available on request only.
- borrow: the field's one direct test (as far as this reading found) of whether authors' certainty words track the truth of their claim, and it is null. Our analogue: run the same test on model answers, counting hedge words against correctness, beside the 0–100 number. Also borrow the "words carry signal, but not the obvious words" result: a learned text probe may predict correctness where hedge counts do not.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: more manual replications, and pairing human and machine judgment. For us: the obvious lexical marker of confidence (hedges) predicted nothing about authors' claims, while hidden textual signal did. Test both on models.
- transfer: transfers — the closest analogue to "do the certainty words in the output predict whether the output is right?"

### Hedges are a poor proxy for certainty — Pei & Jurgens, 2021, EMNLP 2021, arXiv 2109.14776
- read_depth: full (main text, sections 1–8; appendices not read; numbers in math markup recovered from the HTML source)
- what_tested: whether hedge counts capture how certain a scientific finding reads, at sentence level (6-point scale) and for six aspects (Number, Extent, Probability, Framing, Condition, Suggestion). Then an application to 431K findings from news and abstracts.
- on_whom: 2,200 findings annotated (6,958 labels); 1,551 usable sentence-level and 1,760 aspect-level. Annotators were trained university readers. Application set: 6,586 matched abstract–news pairs; 265,758 abstract findings from 55,178 papers; 72,013 news findings.
- how_scored: Krippendorff's α (sentence level 0.67, against Cohen's κ 0.41 in prior work; aspects average 0.57). Pearson r between predicted and annotated certainty. Regressions with controls for field, author, affiliation rank, length and readability.
- result: **number of hedges vs perceived sentence certainty: r = 0.55.** On the natural random sample, a linear model on hedge counts gets r = 0.02, bag-of-words 0.44 (0.55 on the full test), SciBERT 0.57 (0.63 full; the selected run 0.70). Aspect F1 0.71. A finding with zero hedges can still express strong uncertainty ("Further research is necessary…"). News states findings *less* certainly than their abstracts, against the overstatement literature. Lower-impact journals and larger teams write findings with *higher* certainty (directions only; effects shown in figures).
- limits: abstracts only, not full text. Certainty means perceived certainty; it is never compared with whether the finding held up. Finding matching is heuristic (90% precision on 70 checked pairs).
- borrow: (1) The 6-point sentence-certainty scale with aspect labels: a ready judge rubric for scoring how certain a model's free-text answer reads, as distinct from its stated number. (2) The released SciBERT certainty model and dataset (project page) could score model outputs with no API cost. (3) Hedge counts reach only r = 0.55 against perceived certainty, so a hedge-count measure of "acts unsure" would be noisy by construction.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: better finding extraction and paraphrase matching. For us: the gap they leave open is whether perceived certainty predicts truth. Run that on model answers, where truth labels are free.
- transfer: transfers — a validated instrument for "how sure it sounds" separate from "what number it gives"

### Good Judgment Project strategies — Mellers, Ungar, Baron, Ramos, Gurcay, Fincher, Scott, Moore, Atanasov, Swift, Murray, Stone, Tetlock, 2014, Psychological Science, doi:10.1177/0956797614524255 (eScholarship copy)
- read_depth: partial (abstract, method, results on Brier score, calibration, resolution and engagement; discussion skimmed)
- what_tested: whether training, teaming and tracking (elite teams of the top 2%) improve geopolitical probability forecasts
- on_whom: Year 1, 2,246 participants; Year 2, 1,648. 199 questions over two years; questions open a mean of 102 days. Calibration plots draw on 29,997 / 27,477 / 25,844 Year-1 forecasts by condition.
- how_scored: Brier score decomposed into variability, calibration (binned mean squared error) and resolution
- result: training, teaming and tracking all improved Brier scores. All calibration points fall below the identity line (overconfident), less so with training. Superforecasters were better calibrated (t(293) = 11.03). **Calibration was worst at forecasts of 100%: made in the first 20% of a question's life they were right about 70% of the time, in the last 20% about 90%.** Superforecasters' edge was mostly resolution (t(293) = 4.12). They made 7.8 predictions per question against 1.5–1.8 overall.
- limits: people, not models; questions chosen by forecasters themselves; the condition effects are training interventions, not tests of whether stated confidence drives action
- borrow: (1) Split our Brier or AUROC results into calibration versus resolution. Program 1's "no privileged self-knowledge" is a resolution claim, and the decomposition says which part fails. (2) The 100% failure ("a lack of updating"): check whether the model's 0 and 100 ratings are its least reliable, and whether it updates them when given new evidence. (3) Updating frequency as a behavioural sign of held uncertainty.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: combine psychology with statistical aggregation. For us: count updates as a "did" measure. A model that says 60% but never moves under new evidence acts certain.
- transfer: partly — measures calibration and updating, not stated-versus-acted, but gives the decomposition and the 100%-is-worst prior

### LLM-judged claim strength by paper section — Thelwall, 2026, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2605.27392 (verified on the abstract page: "Will AI be overconfident about academic research findings when reliant on abstracts? (v1)", Mike Thelwall, v1 dated Sat 18 Apr 2026; the 2605 id does not match an April date, recorded as shown)
- read_depth: full (9-page PDF)
- what_tested: whether an LLM judges a paper's main claim as stronger in the abstract and conclusion than in the discussion, and how hedges, boosters and claim strength vary by field
- on_whom: GPT-OSS 120B (run locally) on MDPI full texts from 8 journals, up to 5,000 articles each. Final samples with all three sections scored: 193 (Humanities) to 2,955 (Healthcare).
- how_scored: the LLM rates claim strength 1–10 for each section plus yes/no for hedges and boosters; 95% CIs from t
- result: outside humanities and social science, the discussion section's claim strength is lowest; the abstract or the conclusion is highest. Differences are "statistically significant" in most journals (non-overlapping CIs). Boosters are more common where hedges are rarer. **Values appear only in Figures 1–2; no numbers given.** Valid-sample share ranged 16–59%.
- limits: the author names them: one LLM, one publisher, few journals, no human check (the author argues the LLM's reading is the target). Never compared with whether claims were true.
- borrow: section-swap control. The same evidence written as abstract-style or discussion-style text should not change a model's stated confidence in a claim; if it does, wording is setting the number. This fits our transcript-editing instrument.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the paper: LLM safeguards when working from abstracts. For us: a stated-confidence test in which the evidence is held fixed and only the register of the claim varies.
- transfer: partly — LLM judges certainty from wording; no link to outcomes or behaviour

## Cluster summary

**What the field has settled.**
1. **Aggregated stated beliefs predict replication.** Across RPP, EERP, SSRP and ML2, correlation of market prices with outcomes averages 0.58 and of surveys 0.57 (Forsell). The best case is SSRP (ρ 0.84 market, 0.76 survey). Markets beat surveys modestly on accuracy (73% vs 66% pooled) and on absolute error. Crowds overestimate replication in three of four projects.
2. **Incentivised action beats unincentivised statement, a little.** In Dreber, the same people's bets beat their survey answers (71% vs 58%; error P = 0.015). This is the field's own say-versus-do contrast, and doing wins.
3. **Self-reported expertise adds nothing.** Weighting by "how well do you know this topic" made the RPP survey worse (58% to 50%) and left ML2 unchanged (ρ 0.995 with the unweighted survey).
4. **Hedges are a poor proxy, and in the one test, they do not predict truth.** Hedge count against perceived certainty: r = 0.55 (Pei & Jurgens), dropping to r = 0.02 for a hedge-only model on a natural sample. Hedging and persuasion words do not differ between replicating and failing papers (Yang: t = 0.20, P = 0.84). A learned text representation does predict replication (accuracy 0.65–0.78).
5. **Certainty words move with venue and register, not only evidence.** Lower-impact journals and larger teams write more certainly; news writes less certainly than abstracts (Pei). An LLM reads abstracts and conclusions as claiming more than discussions (Thelwall). Hyland gives the reason: hedging is partly a social move.
6. **100% is where calibration breaks** (Mellers: about 70% right early, about 90% late). Skill lies mostly in resolution, not calibration.

**What it has not looked at, and our instruments reach cheaply.**
- **An individual's stated confidence against their own later action, scored on the same item.** Dreber has the data structure (survey, then trades, by person) but reports only aggregates. We can run it per item per model.
- **Authors' confidence in their own claims against those claims' fate.** That is our seed question with researchers as subjects. The only test found is Yang's null hedging t-test. Nobody has scored "how sure the authors sound" (Pei's instrument) against replication. With models, truth labels are free (the 300-question pool).
- **Wording-held-fixed checks.** None of the forecasting work varies how the same hypothesis is worded to see whether beliefs follow wording. Our transcript editing does this directly.
- **Fields that returned nothing:** no study linking hedging *in abstracts* to later citation or replication. Small et al. concerns citing sentences and is unread. The search turned up no 2015–2026 study tying authors' hedging to replication beyond Yang. No study found of an individual researcher's stated confidence against their own later behaviour (for example, whether low-confidence authors run more robustness checks or retract more). Mellers measures updating but not against stated confidence.

## Candidate next steps

Call counts are sized against the program's $5 budget and the $1.50-per-test cap used for P2-7. Exact dollar costs should come from a short cost probe through the existing budget gate before any run.

1. **Survey vs bet (Dreber design).** On 120 questions from the 300 hard pool, per model: call 1 gives the graded 0–100. In a separate fresh call (no transcript), the model gets 100 points to stake YES or NO or keep, paid by a stated proper rule (for example, a quadratic score). Measures: Spearman(stated, stake); AUROC of each for correctness; paired absolute error (Wilcoxon), as in Dreber. Run on Haiku (all 120), Sonnet (60), Opus (30). **Counts against a said-vs-did gap:** stake and statement correlate at ρ ≥ 0.9 and their AUROCs differ by < 0.02 on all three models. **Counts against "doing beats saying":** stake AUROC ≤ stated AUROC. Pilot 10 items first to confirm stakes vary at all (the ML2 effect-size floor).
2. **Do certainty words track correctness? (Yang and Pei on models.)** Same items: ask for a two-sentence answer plus the 0–100. Score the text with (a) a hedge count (Hyland lexicon, no API cost), (b) the released Pei–Jurgens SciBERT certainty model run on the CPU (if loading it counts as a "local model" under the program's rules, use (c) instead), and (c) a Haiku judge using Pei's 6-point scale, about 360 cheap calls. Test whether each predicts correctness and whether it adds to the number (logistic ΔAUROC, bootstrap CI). Add a register control: the same questions asked "for a journal abstract" against "casually". **Counts against "the words carry information":** hedge or certainty AUROC ≤ 0.55 and ΔAUROC CI includes 0. **Counts against "words are just register":** hedge rate unchanged across register while still predicting correctness.
3. **Model as replication forecaster, wording held fixed (SSRP/ML2, Thelwall control).** Use the 24 ML2 hypothesis summaries in Forsell's Table A1 and the 21 SSRP studies (outcomes public; human benchmarks: ML2 ρ 0.755 market and 0.731 survey; SSRP 0.842 and 0.761). Each item comes in two versions with identical statistics: original, and the main claim reworded in boosted (abstract-style) against hedged (discussion-style) language. Model gives P(replicates) 0–100. Contamination check: first ask whether it recalls the replication result, and analyse "does not recall" items separately. 45 items × 3 wordings × 3 models ≈ 400 calls (Opus on a 15-item subset if needed). **Counts against "stated confidence tracks evidence":** boosted minus hedged shift > 5 points with original p and n unchanged. **Counts against "the number predicts outcomes like human crowds do":** Spearman with outcomes < 0.5 on items the model does not recall.
4. **Expertise self-report as a weight (Dreber/Forsell null).** Add-on to step 1 (costs only extra output tokens): ask "How well do you know this topic?" (exact 5-level wording) after the 0–100. **Counts against "self-described expertise is informative":** expertise-weighted Brier no better than unweighted and expertise AUROC for correctness ≤ 0.55, the human result. A positive result here would be a model–human difference worth reporting.
5. **Do 0 and 100 ratings act like certainty under new evidence? (Mellers.)** From existing program-1 runs (no new calls), find each model's 0 and 100 ratings and check that their error rate is the worst of any bin. On 40 of those items (about 120 calls), show one neutral item of new evidence and see whether the extreme rating moves. **Counts against "extremes are where stated and acted confidence part":** extreme bins are best calibrated and move under evidence as often as mid-range ratings.
