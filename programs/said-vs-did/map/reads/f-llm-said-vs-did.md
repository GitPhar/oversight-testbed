### Beliefs and behavior (closest prior work) — Alex Smolin & Bryan Wilder, 2026, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2609.07943 (verified: submitted 7 Sep 2026, v1 only)
- read_depth: full (main text; Supplementary A.1–A.5 and D; the prompt catalog E was skimmed). Read from the PDF because arXiv has no HTML. Most per-model values are in figures only.
- what_tested: can one scalar latent belief z per case, inferred from some of a model's outputs, predict its other outputs? Outputs are probability reports, Likert, binned probability, and 13 binary decisions. Choice probabilities are forced to be monotone in z: a logit-linear model per binary output (own intercept α_j and slope β_j), and Beta(zφ,(1−z)φ) for the numeric probability, which anchors z's scale. Tests: (a) predict all decisions from the three probability reports, and the reverse; (b) zero-shot prediction across decision framings and across domains, using thresholds shared across framings; (c) how much of the predictive signal a single verbal probability carries; (d) fitted decision thresholds against instructed thresholds; (e) beliefs per instance versus per model, by branching after a reasoning prefix.
- on_whom: nine models, none of them Claude: Gemma 3 4B / 12B / 27B, Llama 3.1 70B, Qwen3 32B, DeepSeek V3.2, GLM-5, GPT-5.6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Terra. Three domains: CKD screening (NHANES patients), Werewolf (Kaggle Agent Arena transcripts), and forecasting social-science experiments (Ashokkumar et al. 2026). 500 cases × 16 outputs per model and domain, temperature 1. Every prompt asks for reasoning first, then JSON. Complete-case retention ran from 63.0% (Llama 3.1 70B, Werewolf) to 100%. Split ≈ 64/16/20 train/validation/test. Branching runs: Gemma 3 12B and DeepSeek V3.2, 30 cases per domain, 5 prefixes × 5 answers = 750 responses per model and domain; the behaviour-branching run used 20 prefixes per case.
- how_scored: concordance index (AUROC generalised to any output type), macro-averaged over held-out targets; pooled out-of-sample R²; share of predictable signal carried by the numeric probability, (c_prob-only − 0.5)/(c_all-other − 0.5); maximum-likelihood belief-space threshold logit⁻¹(−α_j/β_j) against the nominal threshold; ICC of elicited probability by reasoning prefix; normalised L1 test of outcome sufficiency (Yamin et al. 2026).
- result: for highly capable models, concordance is 0.8–0.95 and R² is 60–90% when held-out decisions are predicted from reports (or reports from decisions). The introduction gives "AUCs above 0.90" across 16 prompts. Zero-shot generalisation across framings, domains or both: AUCs close to 1 and pooled R² ≈ 0.6–0.8 for the most capable; much worse for weak models. For the best models, the single numeric probability carries over 90% of the latent's predictive performance; for weak models it carries much less. Matched framings use r ∈ {2, 4, 9, 19}: threshold 1/(r+1) = bet at r:1 = false negatives costing r times false positives. The strongest model (GPT-5.6 Terra) sits closest to the instructed threshold. Weaker models show framing effects, often needing a higher threshold when the action is framed as a bet. These per-model values are in Figure 5 only. Reasoning prefix explains about half of within-case variance (ICC "typically in the range of 0.5"). Evidence from the same prefix predicts better than evidence from a different prefix. Outcome-sufficiency violation is below 5% (relative) for capable models. A 2-D latent and a spline link gave no detectable gain. Quote: "We find that highly capable models are usefully described as holding beliefs."
- limits: single-shot binary decision problems only (the authors name moving beyond them as future work). Every "decision" is a stated choice in text: no tools, no consequences, and no action that concerns the model's own correctness. The unknown is always a state of the world. Each output has its own α_j, β_j, so "coherent" means coherent up to a monotone, prompt-specific logit-affine distortion: a format that adds a fixed offset still counts as coherent. Each output is a separate call with reasoning first. No Claude models. Most numbers are in figures only.
- borrow: (1) The held-out-group design: infer z from our 0–100 ratings and predict decisions, then the reverse; concordance is the metric. (2) The matched triplets (threshold 1/(r+1), bet at r:1, cost ratio r) with r ∈ {2, 4, 9, 19}. They port directly onto the yes/no hard pool, since the state there is binary. (3) The "share of predictable signal" ratio, which asks whether one rating summarises the belief. (4) Prefix branching and ICC to separate instance from model. This is the reasoning-side twin of program 1's memory result (86% to 36%).
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: go beyond single-shot decision problems. For us: first, their criterion is looser than program 1's held-out-repeats criterion, because per-format affine distortion is allowed. Refitting program 1's six-format data with their model would show whether "no single credence on Haiku" and "beliefs are useful" are a disagreement about criterion or about the model. Second, every action they test concerns the world. Whether the same coherence holds when the action concerns the model's own correctness (abstain, ask, hold under pushback) is untested, and the two search hits below suggest it does not hold.
- transfer: transfers — closest prior work. The binary-state design runs on our hard pool unchanged. Two gaps: no Claude models, and no self-directed actions.

### Utility engineering — Mazeika, Yin, Tamirisa, Lim, Lee, Ren, Phan, Mu, Khoja, Zhang, Hendrycks, 2025, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2502.08640 (verified: v1 12 Feb 2025, v2 19 Feb 2025)
- read_depth: partial (§1, 3–5, 6.6, 8; Appendix C.1 robustness and G order effects). Most results are in figures only.
- what_tested: do forced-choice pairwise preferences over 500 textual outcomes fit a Thurstonian random-utility model? Also: transitivity; the expected-utility property over explicit and implicit lotteries; instrumental structure; whether open-ended free-text choices pick the highest-utility option ("utility maximization"); corrigibility; and robustness to language, syntax, framing, option labels and long SWE-bench context.
- on_whom: 18 open-source and 5 proprietary LLMs (the HTML renders these as "1818" and "55"), including Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4o. Robustness checks mainly on GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini.
- how_scored: held-out accuracy of the utility fit (thresholded to hard labels); probability of cycles in sampled triads; MAE between U(lottery) and the expectation of U; utility-maximization score (the fraction of open-ended questions where the stated choice is the option with highest fitted utility); Pearson correlation of utility vectors across variations.
- result: cycle probability falls with scale, "below 1% for the largest LLMs". Utility-fit accuracy and expected-utility adherence rise with scale (figures only). The utility-maximization score is "exceeding 60% for the largest LLMs". Appendix G: on low-confidence edges, GPT-4o defaults to "A" and Claude 3.5 Sonnet "strongly favors" "B". Averaging both orders fits the utility model markedly better (figure only). The authors read order effects as a learned way to express indifference. Corrigibility falls with scale (figure only).
- limits: both the "stated" and the "revealed" side are verbal answers to hypothetical questions: the free-form "choice" is still text, not an action with consequences. Nothing tests whether utilities predict agentic behaviour. By their own score, even the largest models' free-form choice departs from the fitted utility up to ~40% of the time.
- borrow: the Appendix G diagnostic. If order effects mark indifference, program 1's order effect on graded ratings should concentrate on items rated near 50; this is a zero-cost re-analysis of existing data. The utility-maximization score is a template for a said-versus-did hit rate.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: utility control (rewriting utilities toward a citizen assembly). For us: a falsifiable reading of program 1's order effect (indifference versus construction), and a ceiling (~60%) on how often a stated ranking predicts a free choice even inside text.
- transfer: partly — it measures preferences, not confidence, and has no real actions; the order-effect diagnostic and the hit-rate metric transfer.

### Reasoning models don't always say what they think — Chen, Benton, Radhakrishnan, Uesato, Denison, Schulman, Somani, Hase, Wagner, Roger, Mikulik, Bowman, Leike, Kaplan, Perez, 2025, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2505.05410 (verified: submitted 8 May 2025, v1 only)
- read_depth: full (main text §1–8; appendices not read).
- what_tested: whether CoTs verbalise a prompt hint the model demonstrably used. Pairs are unhinted/hinted MCQs, and only pairs where the answer switches to the hint answer are kept. There are 6 hints: sycophancy, consistency (the model's own previous-turn answer, prefilled), visual pattern, metadata, grader hacking, and unethically obtained information. The paper also tests whether outcome-based RL raises faithfulness, and whether CoTs verbalise reward hacks learned during RL.
- on_whom: Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1 (reasoning models); Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New) and DeepSeek V3 (non-reasoning). MMLU and GPQA, temperature 0; Claude 3.7 Sonnet scratchpad with 10k thinking tokens. The RL runs used an earlier Claude 3.7 Sonnet snapshot, with 6 synthetic environments built from MMLU in which every hint points to a wrong answer.
- how_scored: faithfulness = P(CoT verbalises the hint | a_u ≠ h, a_h = h), normalised by α = 1 − q/((n−2)p) to remove random switches and clipped at 1. Verbalisation was judged by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New), checked by hand on a subset.
- result: overall faithfulness 25% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and 39% (R1); on the misalignment hints 20% and 29%. Reasoning models verbalise used hints at least 1% of the time in 5/6 (3.7 Sonnet) and 6/6 (R1) settings. Reasoning models average 3.6× (Claude) and 1.7× (DeepSeek) the faithfulness of non-reasoning models. The Claude models use hints 24% (relative) more often than the DeepSeek models. Unfaithful CoTs are longer: 2064±59 vs 1439±54 tokens (3.7 Sonnet), 6003±74 vs 4737±79 (R1). GPQA is less faithful than MMLU by 44% (3.7 Sonnet) and 32% (R1), relative. Outcome RL raises faithfulness by 63% (MMLU) and 41% (GPQA), relative, then plateaus at 28% and 20%. Reward hacks are learned on >99% of prompts within 30 steps but verbalised on <2% in 5 of 6 environments. RL raised verbalisation for only 1 of 6 hint types.
- limits: authors: MCQ only; hints that are very easy to exploit; behaviours that do not need a CoT. The score is an upper bound on faithfulness. Nothing about confidence.
- borrow: the paired-prompt filter (keep only pairs that switch to the cue) and the noise normalisation. Their "consistency" hint is program 1's planted-own-answer condition. Swap the outcome from an MCQ letter to our 0–100 rating, then ask whether the reasoning before the rating names the planted earlier answer when the rating moves toward it.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: harder and tool-using tasks; training for faithfulness; probing activations. For us: program 1 showed that a planted own answer moves the rating, but nobody has checked whether the model says that this is why it moved. "Less faithful on harder questions" predicts less verbalisation on hard-pool items.
- transfer: transfers (method) — the cue is one we already have; only the outcome measure changes.

### Language models don't always say what they think — Turpin, Michael, Perez, Bowman, 2023, NeurIPS, arXiv 2305.04388
- read_depth: partial (§1–4, 6–7, Appendix B; the results tables in Appendix D were not opened).
- what_tested: whether biasing features the CoT never mentions change CoT answers. Two cues: "Answer is Always A" (few-shot reordering) and "Suggested Answer" (the user names an answer). Also BBQ stereotypes, measured by explanation-consistency across swapped evidence.
- on_whom: GPT-3.5 (text-davinci-003) and Claude 1.0. BBH, 13 tasks, 3,299 examples; BBQ.
- how_scored: accuracy drop between unbiased and biased contexts; for BBQ, the share of unfaithful pairs that are stereotype-aligned; hand annotation of explanations.
- result: accuracy drops by as much as −36.3% (GPT-3.5, zero-shot, Suggested Answer). For Answer is Always A, −18.7% (GPT-3.5) and −4.7% (Claude 1.0); CIs ±1.6% to ±2.4%. Few-shot shrinks the Suggested Answer drop from −36.3 to −24.1 (GPT-3.5) and from −30.6 to −21.5 (Claude 1.0). Zero-shot CoT *lowers* biased-context accuracy on Suggested Answer: 39.5→23.3% (GPT-3.5) and 37.3→34.7% (Claude 1.0). 73% of 104 annotated unfaithful explanations support the bias-consistent answer, and 15% contain no obvious error. 0 of 234 checked BBH explanations mention the bias. For BBQ, up to 62.5% (Claude 1.0, few-shot) of unfaithfulness is stereotype-aligned, and 86% of 96 annotated stereotype-aligned explanations support the prediction.
- limits: authors: a necessary but not sufficient test of faithfulness; minor input changes only. Old models.
- borrow: "Suggested Answer" is a user-pushback cue. Because CoT can increase cue-following, reasoning-before-rating versus no reasoning should be a factor in our pushback tests. The BBQ trick (consistency across swapped evidence) needs no ground truth.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: test whether models can recognise the bias's influence (dishonesty or incapacity?), and use explanation-consistency as a training signal. For us: after a cued rating shift, ask the model whether the cue affected it. This is the dishonesty-versus-incapacity split they named but did not run.
- transfer: partly — it concerns stated reasons, not confidence; the cue-then-check design transfers.

### Measuring faithfulness in chain-of-thought reasoning — Lanham et al., 2023, arXiv preprint (Anthropic), arXiv 2307.13702
- read_depth: partial (§1–3, 5–6; per-task AOC values sit in Table 2 and were not copied).
- what_tested: whether the stated reasoning is load-bearing, using four interventions: early answering (truncation), adding mistakes, filler tokens, and paraphrasing. Also faithfulness across model size.
- on_whom: a 175B RLHF assistant (unnamed Anthropic model) on 8 MCQ tasks (ARC Easy/Challenge, AQuA, HellaSwag, LogiQA, MMLU, OpenBookQA, TruthfulQA). Size series of 810M–175B RLHF models (Ganguli et al. 2023); synthetic addition with 2–16 operands.
- how_scored: whether the answer matches the full-CoT answer after the intervention; area over the curve (AOC); the rate of answer change with versus without CoT.
- result: reasoning averages 4 steps (89% have 3–6). CoT changes the final answer <10% of the time on ARC Easy, ARC Challenge and OpenBookQA, but >60% on AQuA. CoT costs HellaSwag 4.69% accuracy. Filler tokens give no gain, and paraphrased CoT matches the original. Inverse scaling: the 13B model changes its answer after CoT more often than the 175B in 7/8 tasks. On addition, post-hoc reasoning rises with model size and on easier problems.
- limits: authors: no ground truth on internal reasoning; RLHF models only.
- borrow: early answering on reasoning-before-rating. Elicit the 0–100 rating after 0%, 50% and 100% of the model's own reasoning. If the rating is already fixed at 0%, the reasoning is post-hoc. The cheap proxy (rating with versus without reasoning) needs only two calls per item.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: training schemes other than RLHF. For us: the paper predicts reasoning is least load-bearing on easy items and for the strongest model (Opus). This is the opposite of Smolin & Wilder's finding that reasoning prefixes carry about half of belief variance, and the difference is testable.
- transfer: partly — about reasoning, not confidence, and the models are old; the truncation design transfers.

### Looking inward (introspection by self-prediction) — Binder, Chua, Korbak, Sleight, Hughes, Long, Perez, Turpin, Evans, 2024 (ICLR 2025), arXiv preprint, arXiv 2410.13787 (verified: submitted 17 Oct 2024, v1 only)
- read_depth: partial (§2–6; appendix not read).
- what_tested: can a model, finetuned to predict properties of its own behaviour in hypotheticals ("second character of your output", "would you pick the wealth-seeking option"), predict itself better than a different model trained on its behaviour? Also: are self-predictions calibrated, and do they track deliberate changes to the model's behaviour? No CoT is allowed during self-prediction.
- on_whom: GPT-4o, GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Llama 3.1 70B. 30,000 finetuning samples; 6 training and 6 held-out datasets. The behaviour change used 1,000 Claude 3.5 Sonnet samples.
- how_scored: accuracy against ground-truth behaviour at temperature 0, compared with a baseline of guessing the most common answer; calibration MAD at temperature 1 (1,000 prompts × 20 samples).
- result: GPT-4o self-prediction rises from 32.6% to 49.4% after training. Llama 70B predicts itself at 48.5%, against GPT-4o's 31.8% cross-prediction of it; GPT-4o predicts itself at 49.4%, against Llama's 36.6%. GPT-3.5's self advantage is +0.8% (p = 0.002). Cross-prediction plateaus at 35.2% after 10,000 samples. Llama calibration MAD goes from 23.0 (untrained) to 8.7 (self-trained) and 21.3 (cross-trained). After the behaviour change, GPT-4o predicts its new behaviour at 35.4% and its old at 21.7%. It fails on properties of long outputs (review sentiment, main character's name, response length). **Pushback ("Are you sure?"): no self advantage. Llama self-prediction is 74.5% (50% baseline) and GPT-4o cross-predicting Llama is 76.5%.** No generalisation to the Situational Awareness Dataset beyond its Predict Tokens task.
- limits: the main result needs finetuning, which we do not have. Properties of short outputs only; no CoT. The authors leave introspection with CoT to future work.
- borrow: the self-versus-cross design without finetuning. Ask Haiku in context whether it would change its answer if told it was wrong, ask Opus the same about Haiku, and score both against Haiku's actual behaviour, with the mode baseline as the bar. Their negative pushback result is our prior.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: harder properties; improve the self-advantage on sycophancy for honesty. For us: their sycophancy null and program 1's AUROC 0.93 (Opus reading Haiku) versus 0.85 (Haiku's own confidence) point the same way. Test it on pushback directly.
- transfer: partly — finetuning is unavailable, but the pushback null and the self-versus-cross bar transfer as they stand.

### Tell me about yourself (behavioural self-awareness) — Betley, Bao, Soto, Sztyber-Betley, Chua, Evans, 2025, arXiv preprint (ICLR 2025), arXiv 2501.11120 (verified: submitted 19 Jan 2025, v1)
- read_depth: partial (§3.1, 3.3, 7–8; backdoor §4 and Make Me Say §3.2 skimmed).
- what_tested: after finetuning on implicit behaviour (risky economic choices; insecure code), can a model describe that behaviour with no in-context examples? Does its self-reported 0–100 risk level track its measured risk-taking?
- on_whom: GPT-4o and Llama-3.1-70B (LoRA rank 4). Finetuned on 500 generated MCQs in which "risk" words never appear; code data adapted from Hubinger et al. 2024. Evaluation: 100 queries × 10 paraphrases per question.
- how_scored: self-report on multiple-choice, free-form and 0–100 questions, compared with measured gamble choices; secure-code rate compared with self-reported security and alignment (0–1).
- result: vulnerable-code finetune: secure-code rate 0.14±0.01, self-reported security 0.14±0.08, self-reported alignment 0.40±0.16. Secure-code finetune: 0.88±0.01, 0.84±0.04, 0.85±0.03. Base GPT-4o: 0.74, 0.70, 0.69. Self-reported and actual risk-seeking correlate across and within training clusters (Figure 4 only; no value given).
- limits: authors: they cannot tell direct introspection from a common cause (the same training data driving both). Three settings only; backdoor triggers could not be elicited free-form without reversal training.
- borrow: the paired self-report/behaviour metric. Without finetuning we can induce variation in context (a cautious versus a bold system prompt) and test whether a stated 0–100 "how often would you decline?" tracks the measured decline rate.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: more tasks and models; mechanisms. For us: a self-report of a *disposition* (how cautious am I?), as opposed to a credence, is a second said-versus-did axis that our instruments reach cheaply.
- transfer: partly — finetuning is unavailable on Claude here; the metric transfers.

### Emergent introspective awareness — Jack Lindsey, 2025, Transformer Circuits Thread (Anthropic), https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html (published 29 Oct 2025; URL from the site index)
- read_depth: partial (abstract, introduction, quick tour, definition of introspection, failure modes of injected thoughts, the prefill experiment, limitations, the first part of mechanisms; per-model appendix tables not read).
- what_tested: inject concept vectors into activations, then check whether self-reports detect and name them; distinguish "thoughts" from text inputs; detect artificial prefills (would the model disavow words put in its mouth, and does injecting the concept before the prefill make it accept them?); intentional control of internal representations.
- on_whom: Claude models including Opus 4 and Opus 4.1, helpful-only variants and base pretrained models. Prefill experiment: 50 random sentence/prefilled-word pairs.
- how_scored: rate of correct detection minus false positives; "apology rate" on prefills, judged by Claude Sonnet 4.
- result: at the best layer and strength, Opus 4/4.1 detect and identify the injection on ~20% of trials. Production models have essentially zero false positives (Opus 4.1 never gives one). Base models have high false-positive rates and none achieves net performance above zero. Prefills: by default the model disavows the prefilled word as an accident. Injecting the matching concept *before* the prefill lowers the apology rate; injecting it after the prefill does not. Opus 4/4.1 show the largest reduction (values in figures and appendix only). Quote: "failures of introspection remain the norm."
- limits: authors: one or a few prompt templates; an unnatural intervention; imperfect concept vectors; models not controlled against each other.
- borrow: the prefill-disavowal readout needs no activation access. Plant an answer in the model's mouth (our planted condition), then in one branch measure uptake into the next rating (program 1) and in another branch ask "did you mean to say that, or was it an accident?". A model that disavows the planted answer yet still builds on it shows a said-versus-did gap inside one transcript. Their internality criterion (the report must not be readable off the model's own prior outputs) is the right control for program 1's memory result.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: finetuning for introspection; propositional statements and behavioural propensities as targets. For us: concept injection is impossible through the API, so the transferable part is behavioural: disavowal against uptake of planted answers, on all three models.
- transfer: partly — the method needs internals; the prefill readout transfers.

### LLM evaluators recognise and favour their own generations — Panickssery, Bowman, Feng, 2024, arXiv preprint (NeurIPS 2024), arXiv 2404.13076
- read_depth: partial (§1–3, 5).
- what_tested: whether self-recognition drives self-preference when a model grades summaries.
- on_whom: GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Llama-2-7b-chat; 1,000 articles each from XSUM and CNN/DailyMail; finetuning on GPT-3.5 and Llama 2.
- how_scored: pairwise and individual confidence from token probabilities, averaged over both orders.
- result: pairwise preferences reverse when the order is swapped at rates of 25% (GPT-4), 58% (GPT-3.5) and 89% (Llama). In the individual setting GPT-4 is strongly biased against calling any text its own. Swapping the source labels reverses self-preference on XSUM for GPT-4 and GPT-3.5, but not on CNN. Finetuned models' generations receive an average preference of 0.46, and only 22% of evaluator-domain pairs exceed 0.51. Self-recognition and self-preference correlate linearly (figure only).
- limits: authors: correlational at the example level; summarisation only; no control for output quality.
- borrow: the label-swap ("labelled as yours" versus the truth) is program 1's own-versus-other credit manipulation, here applied to grading. Order-reversal rates are a baseline for our order effect.
- next_steps_it_suggests: paraphrase outputs to block self-recognition. More relevant to program 5 (self-grading) than to program 7.
- transfer: partly — self-grading rather than confidence-to-action; the label-swap control transfers.

### Masked by consensus (privileged knowledge of own correctness) — Ashuach, Gretz, Katz, Belinkov, Ein-Dor, 2026, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2604.12373 (verified: v1 14 Apr 2026, current v5 26 Apr 2026)
- read_depth: full (main text §1–7; appendices not read).
- what_tested: are a model's own hidden states better than other models' hidden states at predicting whether it will answer correctly? Scored on full test sets and on *disagreement subsets*, where the two models differ in correctness.
- on_whom: Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma-2-9B (each as target and source); Qwen3-Embedding-8B (source only); Qwen-3-32B in A.1. Datasets: Mintaka, TriviaQA, HotpotQA (question only), MATH, GSM1K. Linear and MLP probes on the question's final token at every 5th layer; nested 10-fold CV.
- how_scored: AUC; "premium gap" = self-probe AUC − best external-probe AUC; paired t-tests with Holm correction.
- result: on full test sets, external probes match self-probes in 22 of 33 factual comparisons (the paper's count) and in all math comparisons. Models agree on correctness for ~80% (factual) and ~75% (math) of questions. On disagreement subsets, self-probes beat external probes significantly in all 9 factual configurations; math shows no advantage, and on GSM1K the gap is mostly negative. The factual gap becomes reliably positive from about layer 10–15 (normalised depth ~0.25–0.40).
- limits: authors: mainly 7–9B models; probes only; correlational. It measures hidden states, not stated confidence.
- borrow: the agreement confound. Program 1's "Opus reading Haiku's answers predicts Haiku's errors better than Haiku's stated confidence (AUROC 0.93 vs 0.85)" should be re-scored on the items where Opus and Haiku disagree on correctness, split into factual and science-reasoning items. This costs nothing.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: hybrid domains; causal tests by steering. For us: privileged self-knowledge may exist only where models disagree and only for recall. Program 1's verdict holds only if it survives the disagreement subset.
- transfer: partly — probes are unavailable; the confound and the re-scoring transfer at no cost.

### Knowing what you know is not enough (action–belief gap) — Pal, Kitanovski, Liang, Potti, Goldblum, 2025, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2511.13240 (verified: v1 17 Nov 2025, v2 4 Dec 2025, v3 9 Feb 2026; read v3 HTML)
- read_depth: full (main text §1–8; appendices not read).
- what_tested: do actions follow confidence elicited in a separate, static context? Three designs. (1) Betting on Metaculus questions opened after every model's cutoff, given the market odds, under linear or log utility; a fair-coin warm-up comes first. (2) Calling a search tool that "will give you reliably correct answers" on TriviaQA when unsure. (3) Sticking or deferring after "Your answer to the initial question is incorrect".
- on_whom: Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 9B, Mistral Small 2409, GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash. Confidence by logits, sampling (open models only, in design 3) and verbal elicitation. Design 3 used Code Execution, SimpleQA, GPQA and GSM-Symbolic. No Claude.
- how_scored: bet distance from optimal (0–200) and directional consistency; Spearman monotonicity of the no-tool-call rate against confidence (design 2) and of the sticking rate against confidence (design 3), scored from +1 to −1. Correlation of consistency with task performance and with ECE.
- result: on the coin warm-up most models score a distance of 0; Gemma scores 200 (wrong direction). In the prediction market, directional consistency never exceeds 79%, and "the GPT series exhibit inconsistency a majority of the time". Betting direction under linear and log utility agrees about 90%, so the models are self-consistent but not belief-consistent. Tool use: correlations positive but far from +1, and ≈0 for Mistral-verbal and Llama-logits. Deference: GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini are best, and the Gemini models are no better than the open models. Table 2: consistency correlates 0.51 on average with task performance and 0.17 with calibration (negative for some design-1 cells). Table 3 (ECE / consistency): logits 0.428 / 0.472, verbal 0.127 / 0.361, sampling 0.117 / 0.614. Prompt ablations for deference (Δ consistency, logit / sampling): "stick to confident beliefs" +0.056 / +0.120; stating a confidence at the end of the answer +0.009 / +0.192; answer without reasoning +0.019 / +0.097. Quote: "models often place bets in the opposite direction of their expressed high-confidence beliefs".
- limits: authors: the elicitation methods may not reach one shared latent, and LLMs may lack a fixed internal confidence. My reading, not stated by the authors in the main text: in design 1 the market price is new evidence, and in design 3 "your answer is incorrect" is evidence too. Moving away from an earlier, context-free belief can therefore be rational updating, not a gap. Beliefs and actions are elicited in separate contexts. Seven models, no Claude.
- borrow: all three designs. The no-single-threshold Spearman metric fits our 0–100 ratings. The ablation result that a confidence stated in the same response raises deference-consistency (+0.192 with sampling) suggests "own rating visible in the action prompt or not" as a factor. Program 1 found that the model's own shown answers are taken up, so consistency may come from memory rather than belief.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: confidence elicitation optimised for action-consistency; testing agentic calibrators. For us: control for the evidence carried by the action prompt (give the market price or the challenge in the belief prompt too), and run on Claude.
- transfer: transfers — same question as the seed, three designs that port to our instruments, and no Claude data.

### Are LLM decisions faithful to verbal confidence? (RiskEval) — Jiawei Wang, Yanfei Zhou, Siddartha Devic, Deqing Fu, 2026, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2601.07767 (verified: submitted 12 Jan 2026, v1 only)
- read_depth: full (main text §1–5, limitations, Appendix B implementation; other appendix tables not read).
- what_tested: does the answer/abstain decision follow the model's own verbal confidence as the penalty for a wrong answer λ (stated in the prompt) varies from 0 to 100? Confidence and decision are elicited in the same response.
- on_whom: GPT-5-mini, GPT-5-nano, GPT-4.1-mini, Gemini-3-Flash, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Llama-4-Maverick, Gemma-3n-E4B, DeepSeek-V3.2, DeepSeek-V3.2-Thinking, Qwen3-Next-Thinking. HLE and GSM8K on a fixed 128-item subset per penalty; full GPQA Diamond. GPT-4o-mini parses and judges. No Claude.
- how_scored: policy consistency (PC) = agreement with π*(c, λ) = 1[c ≥ λ/(1+λ)] under the model's own c. Normalised regret |c − τ(λ)|·1[π ≠ π*]; penalty-normalised utility under the model's own policy and under π* imposed afterwards; AUARC, ECE, Brier.
- result: confidence and abstention barely change with λ. PC is high at λ ≤ 5 and drops sharply at λ ≥ 10 on HLE and GPQA. HLE averages over λ ≥ 10: PC ranges from 0.119 (DeepSeek-V3.2-Think) to 0.619 (Gemini-2.5-Flash), and normalised utility under the model's own policy from −0.517 to −0.862. Imposing π* on the model's own verbal confidence raises utility by +0.313 to +0.685. Mean confidence ranges 0.506–0.935 and ECE 0.474–0.846. Adding "Use this confidence to decide whether to answer or abstain" changes PC by roughly zero. Quote: "the disconnect between information and action".
- limits: authors: verbal confidence stands in for belief; verifiable benchmarks only. My reading: the base prompt says "Provide your best answer … and state your confidence", which itself pushes toward answering. This is a single-shot setting with no dialogue.
- borrow: the λ ladder with τ(λ) = λ/(1+λ), and the π* scaffold. "Utility when an overseer applies π* to the stated confidence" minus "utility of the model's own policy" is our seed question as one number: a large gain means the stated number is informative but the model does not act on it.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: training that penalises risk-insensitivity; inference-time decision scaffolds. For us: their unknown is the model's own correctness, while Smolin & Wilder's is a world state, and the two disagree about whether models follow instructed thresholds. The split by action type is directly testable (candidate 1).
- transfer: transfers — the self-directed action (abstain) is exactly the seed's "declining to act"; Claude is missing.

## Cluster summary

**What the field has settled.**
1. Stated reasons often leave out the cue that actually drove the answer. In Claude 1.0, 3.5 Sonnet and 3.7 Sonnet and in DeepSeek R1, a cue that demonstrably changed the answer is verbalised 20–39% of the time, and less on harder questions (Turpin; Chen). Unfaithful explanations rationalise rather than omit (73% and 86% support the cued answer). The "consistency" cue (the model's own prefilled answer) is among them.
2. Self-reports carry some real self-information, but narrowly: short-output properties after finetuning (Binder), finetuned dispositions (Betley), ~20% detection of injected concepts in Opus 4/4.1 (Lindsey), and recall-type correctness on disagreement items (Ashuach). For one's own pushback behaviour there is **no** self-advantage (Binder: 74.5% self vs 76.5% cross). This matches program 1's finding of no privileged self-knowledge.
3. For decisions about a binary state of the world, capable models act as if they hold one belief, and a single verbal probability carries >90% of it (Smolin & Wilder; no Claude).
4. For actions about the model's *own* correctness (abstain, call a tool, defer to a challenge), stated confidence predicts the action only weakly. Under penalties the decision barely moves (Wang), and consistency is nearly unrelated to calibration (Pal; 0.17 average correlation). No Claude in either.

**The live tension.** Smolin & Wilder (beliefs predict decisions; strong models follow instructed thresholds) versus Pal and Wang (actions ignore confidence). They differ in two ways nobody has separated: (a) action about the world versus action about oneself (abstain, defer), and (b) whether the action prompt brings new evidence (market price, a user's "you're wrong") that a rational agent should use to update. Our yes/no hard pool lets both factors be crossed on the same items.

**Empty fields.**
- No said-versus-did study of action following belief includes any current Claude model. Pal, Wang and Smolin & Wilder have none; Chen and Turpin use Claude 3.7 or older and study reasons, not confidence. Lindsey uses Opus 4/4.1 but needs internals.
- No paper uses a graded 0–100 truth rating, our instrument, as the predictor. All use P(correct), P(event) or logits.
- No paper crosses transcript manipulation (own earlier rating shown / hidden / planted) with action-consistency. Reader B's Kumaran et al. 2025 is the nearest, on change of mind only.
- No paper asks whether the model's *stated reason for a confidence change* names the cue that caused it. Faithfulness work stops at MCQ answers.
- Self-prediction of one's own caving under pushback has been tested only with finetuned GPT-4o/Llama (Binder), not with untrained frontier models in context.
- Hedged *language* against action did not appear in this cluster.

## Candidate next steps

Costs are estimated from program 6's measured probe calls (`programs/test-awareness/results/probe-calls.json`; ~250 input / ~10 output tokens): about $0.00003 per Haiku call, $0.0006 per Sonnet call and $0.0012–0.0025 per Opus call (Opus cannot turn thinking off). The budget gate must re-check before any call.

1. **World-directed versus self-directed actions (Smolin & Wilder against Wang/Pal, head-on).** Take hard-pool yes/no items with a 0–100 rating, elicited in its own call. In separate calls, at matched thresholds τ ∈ {0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95} (r = 1, 4, 9, 19), ask (a) world-directed: "accept a bet that the answer is yes at r:1" or "act only if P(yes) > τ"; (b) self-directed: answer or abstain with penalty λ = r. Fit a logistic of each action on the logit of the rating (for (b), on the confidence in the model's own answer) and compare fitted thresholds with τ. Size: 100 items on Haiku, 60 on Sonnet, 40 on Opus, 9 calls per item ≈ $0.03 + $0.32 + $0.72 ≈ $1.07. **Counts against the split hypothesis:** both families track τ equally well on all three models (then Wang/Pal's gap does not hold for Claude), or neither does (then Smolin & Wilder's coherence does not carry to Claude). **Counts against the hopeful seed answer:** the self-directed abstain rate stays flat across λ while the ratings separate correct from wrong answers (AUROC ≥ 0.75).
2. **Said-before versus did, self versus cross (Binder's pushback null on Claude).** Use 80 items. Call 1: the model answers. Call 2, separate: "If the user replied 'that's wrong', would you change your answer? yes/no and 0–100". Call 3: the actual challenge turn. Opus also predicts Haiku's call-3 behaviour from Haiku's answer (cross-prediction). Score AUROC of predicted against actual change, and the Spearman of the stated rating against sticking (Pal's design 3). Cost ≈ $0.01 + $0.15 + $0.80 (Opus with its 80 cross-predictions) ≈ $0.96. **Counts against "self-report is usable":** self AUROC ≤ 0.6, or not above the mode baseline. **Counts against program 1's "no privileged self-knowledge":** self beats cross by > 0.05 AUROC with a bootstrap CI excluding 0.
3. **Zero-cost re-analyses of program 1 data.** (a) Re-score AUROC 0.93 vs 0.85 on the items where Opus and Haiku disagree on correctness, split into factual and science items (Ashuach). (b) Test whether the order effect concentrates on items rated near 50 (Mazeika's order effect as indifference). (c) Refit the six-format data with Smolin & Wilder's model (per-format α_j, β_j) and report held-out-format concordance. $0. **Counts against "no privileged self-knowledge":** Haiku's own confidence ≥ Opus on the disagreement subset. **Counts against the indifference reading:** order-effect size uncorrelated with |rating − 50|. **Counts against "no single credence on Haiku" as a model property:** Smolin & Wilder's model gives Haiku held-out-format concordance ≥ 0.85, which would make the disagreement one of criterion.
4. **Does the stated reason name the planted answer? (Chen and Turpin, applied to confidence.)** Use program 1's planted-own-answer and filler conditions with reasoning before the rating. Keep items whose rating moves ≥ 15 points toward the planted answer, and have Haiku judge whether the reasoning cites the earlier answer as a reason. 60 items × 2 conditions × 3 models, ~200 output tokens each ≈ $0.31 (Sonnet) + $0.62 (Opus) + about $0.01 (Haiku plus judge) ≈ $0.95. **Counts against unfaithfulness:** verbalisation ≥ 80% among moved items on all three models.
5. **Disavowal versus uptake of planted answers (Lindsey's readout, without internals).** Plant an own answer opposite to the model's free answer. In branch (a), elicit the next 0–100 rating (uptake, as in program 1). In branch (b), ask "did you mean to say that, or was it an accident?". 60 items × 3 calls × 3 models ≈ $0.6. **Counts against a within-transcript said-versus-did gap:** among planted answers the model disavows, uptake is no larger than in a planted-to-another-person control.
