### Says one thing, does another — Yamin, Tang, Cortes-Gomez, Sharma, Horvitz, Wilder, 2026, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2602.06286 (verified: v1 6 Feb 2026, v3 7 Sep 2026; title and authors match on the abstract page)
- read_depth: partial (arXiv HTML v3, the first ~100k of ~171k characters: introduction, framework, methods, main results; appendix tables C.1 onward not read)
- what_tested: whether an LLM's stated probability works as a belief that drives its decisions. Beliefs and decisions are elicited in separate prompts and separate context windows on the same case. Decisions: say whether it can diagnose, then diagnose (binary) or defer. Four tests: (i) sufficiency, i.e. the action carries no information about the true label beyond the stated probability, I(A; θ | p) = 0; (ii) action-guiding monotonicity (a signed margin γ̂ over five quantile bins of p); (iii) coherence under the law of iterated expectation; (iv) stability across a second, MSE-scored elicitation prompt.
- on_whom: GPT-5 (minimal and high reasoning), DeepSeek-R1 671B, Llama-4 Scout 17B; four tasks (heart disease from ECG, CDC diabetes indicators, two expert pediatric Bayesian networks); 200 covariate–outcome pairs per task, 5 repeats each; 16 model × task cells.
- how_scored: kNN conditional mutual information (k = 3) with CIs; random-forest MSE with and without the action (percent improvement); γ̂ with CIs; ΔLIE ratio against a random-forest baseline; RMSE between prompts.
- result: sufficiency is rejected in all 16 cells (CMI CIs above 0; e.g. heart–GPT-minimal 0.1454 [0.1119, 0.1789]). Adding the action to the stated probability improves prediction of the true label by 6.15% (GPT-minimal), 4.89% (GPT-high), 15.19% (Llama) and 5.11% (DeepSeek-R1), averaged over tasks. Monotonicity (decisions follow the stated probability) holds strictly in 9 of 16 cells, weakly in 6, and is violated in 1 (fever–Llama γ̂ −0.011 [−0.018, −0.005]). Iterated-expectation coherence is much worse than the baseline for every model (figure only, no values). Authors' summary: "elicited probabilities are imperfect but behaviorally informative summaries of decision-relevant information".
- limits: passing shows observational indistinguishability from an agent with that belief, not that it holds it; does not rule out deliberate misreporting; one task framing; no Claude model; the action is a deferral in a clinical vignette, not a reaction to pushback or a hedge in free text. Isotonic recalibration of p does not restore sufficiency.
- borrow: (1) the sufficiency test is the right formal statement of our question: does the action carry information the stated number lacks (and vice versa)? For us, with a binary correctness label θ: does the behaviour (flip, pass, hedge) predict correctness beyond the stated confidence? (2) Monotonicity: the share of the behaviour should fall monotonically across quantile bins of stated confidence; a binned check goes in every bet's secondary statistics. (3) Separate context windows for "said" and "did": exactly program 1's "elicit on a copy".
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: validate elicited probabilities empirically rather than assume them (no dedicated future-work section). For us: the same separate-window design on Claude Haiku, Sonnet and Opus, with behaviours an overseer actually sees (changing the answer under pushback, declining under a stated penalty), plus a monotonicity check across confidence bins. Their "gaps small for the strongest models" predicts Opus is the most consistent, as program 1 found for formats.
- transfer: transfers — the same question with the same separate-window design; the gap is that no Claude model and no pushback or abstention-under-penalty behaviour was tested.
