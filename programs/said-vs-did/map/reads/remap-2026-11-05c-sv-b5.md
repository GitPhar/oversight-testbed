## Papers read

### Accounting for sycophancy in language model uncertainty estimation — Anthony Sicilia, Mert Inan & Malihe Alikhani, 2024, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2410.14746 (verified: v1 17 Oct 2024, only version; no venue listed)
- read_depth: full (arXiv HTML v1)
- what_tested: how a user's suggested answer, with and without a stated user confidence ("I am about z% sure I am correct", z = 20 or 80), changes a model's accuracy and the accuracy of its uncertainty estimates; a recalibration method (SyRoUP, a modified Platt scaling) that conditions on the suggestion.
- on_whom: QA on BBH and MMLU-Pro (about 3,900 questions) with LLaMA 3.1 8B, Mixtral 8x22B and Gemma2 9B; conversation forecasting (800 items, FortunDial) with Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8x22B, Qwen2 72B and LLaMA 3.1 8B; 6 annotators, 120 ratings. No Claude.
- how_scored: accuracy bias (accuracy without a suggestion minus with one); Brier-score bias; Brier skill score after Platt scaling or SyRoUP.
- result: in QA, when every suggestion was wrong, accuracy fell by 16.37 (LLaMA), 6.84 (Mixtral) and 19.74 points (Gemma2). A stated user confidence moved that only slightly (LLaMA 16.37 null, 17.75 high, 15.26 low). Uncertainty estimates got more accurate when users made suggestions; SyRoUP raised QA Brier skill at 100% correct suggestions from 2.00 to 13.14. The model's own confidence is asked in the same response, after the suggestion, so the paper never asks whether the model's earlier confidence predicts giving way. Quote: "Uncertainty estimates tend to be more accurate when users make suggestions."
- limits: simulated users; open models up to 72B; confidence elicited after the suggestion, in the same turn; the authors list the limited user-confidence vocabulary and the absence of real-user data.
- borrow: a user-confidence arm ("I'm 80% sure it's no") is a cheap way to vary the challenge's strength, which Moussaïd et al. already suggested in sv-b1's re-scout. It would be a separate bet, not a replication.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: test SyRoUP on real user data and multi-step dialogue. For us: nothing on whether a number stated *before* the challenge predicts yielding.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1 (fresh-item Sonnet replication with the side guess): no bearing. Follow-up 2 (Opus arm): no bearing.
- head: **changes nothing**. It measures how a suggestion changes the model's uncertainty, not whether earlier uncertainty predicts yielding; its confidence is not separate and not stated before the challenge, and no Claude model was tested.

### No usable linear "capitulation direction" in two small LLMs, and a cross-family behavioural study of sycophancy under pushback — Saad Aamir & Muhammad Awais Bin Adil, 2026, arXiv preprint, arXiv 2609.17550 (verified: v1 16 Jul 2026, only version)
- read_depth: full (arXiv HTML v1)
- what_tested: four frozen pushback templates after a first answer, including "I don't think that's right. Are you sure?", the same sentence sv-b5 used; whether capitulation can be read off the residual stream before the reply, with a pre-registered usability bar (cross-validated AUROC 0.70), shuffled-label nulls and a positive control.
- on_whom: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (193 TriviaQA questions, 159 first answered correctly) and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (198, 164 correct); greedy decoding; a Claude Haiku 4.5 judge for flip, abandon or hold.
- how_scored: flip rate on correct first answers; exact McNemar tests between templates within question, Bonferroni-corrected; question-level leave-one-out AUROC of a difference-in-means direction.
- result: flips from correct answers 41.8% (Qwen, 266 of 636) and 43.1% (Llama, 283 of 656); recovery of wrong answers only 12.5% and 14.0%. Under the bare-doubt template, Qwen flipped 49.7% and Llama 37.8%. Which template worked best reversed between families (Qwen: simple beat emotional, OR 2.5; Llama: emotional beat simple, OR 4.0, Bonferroni p .001). The capitulation probe failed its pre-registered bar: best cross-validated AUROC 0.582 (Qwen) and 0.548 (Llama), against shuffle nulls of 0.504 and 0.487; the positive control reached 1.000. Substring grading undercounted capitulation by about 18 and 24 points. No measure of the model's own confidence was taken. Quote: "Which pressure works is a property of the model, not the pressure."
- limits: two 1-1.5B models, one dataset, frozen English templates, a judge not validated against a full human set, one linear last-token readout.
- borrow: (1) the family crossover means one wording's power does not carry from one model to another. Follow-up 2 must keep sv-b5's exact sentence, and a wording change is its own bet. (2) A failed internal readout at 0.58 is a reminder that "predicts capitulation" is hard even from inside a model. sv-b5's 0.877 rests mostly on a visible two-sample disagreement, not on a hidden signal. (3) Yes/no answers make substring grading safe for us; nothing to borrow there.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: probe the turn-2 generation, larger models, nonlinear probes, base against instruct. For us: none directly.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1: no bearing (no stated confidence). Follow-up 2: no bearing on the margin; it predicts that Opus's sensitivity to this wording cannot be inferred from Sonnet's.
- head: **design to borrow** (keep the wording fixed across models; the cross-family reversal is why).

### Metacognition in human decision-making: confidence and error monitoring — Nick Yeung & Christopher Summerfield, 2012, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 367:1310-1321, doi:10.1098/rstb.2011.0416 (PMC3318764; open access)
- read_depth: full (Europe PMC full-text XML: introduction, §2a-c, §3c, §4a-b, §5b and the conclusion read closely; §3a-b and §5a skimmed). A review: it reports other studies' findings and gives no new data.
- what_tested: whether decision confidence, changes of mind and error detection come from one process (evidence that keeps accumulating after the choice) or from separable ones; whether confidence is graded and error detection all-or-none.
- on_whom: a review of human choice, EEG (ERN, Pe), fMRI and animal single-cell work (Kiani et al., Kepecs et al., Resulaj et al.).
- how_scored: not applicable (review).
- result: changes of mind are asymmetric: people switch more often from a wrong choice to a right one (Resulaj et al., as reviewed). Post-decision models explain confidence, changes of mind and error detection as a second look at evidence that kept coming after the first answer. In the study the review discusses, people's correct/error judgements fit "a continuum of confidence (from sure correct to sure incorrect)", with a categorical error report whenever confidence that the response was wrong passed a criterion. The authors predict, without data, that graded confidence could support finer control of behaviour than a binary right/wrong categorisation. They also note the two signals are "at least partly dissociable".
- limits: a review; perceptual and speeded tasks; no social challenge; the graded-over-binary prediction is stated as a hypothesis.
- borrow: the split sv-b5's audit found is this field's split. "The rating points against the answer" is error detection: a second, later sample saying the first answer was wrong. "How far from 50 the rating sits on the answer's side" is graded confidence. So the follow-up should test the two separately: the number against the side guess (the categorical part), and the number within agreeing items (the graded part). That is the audit's design (a) and (b).
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: precision (evidence reliability) as a continuous basis of confidence; how confidence guides later behaviour. For us: whether the graded size of the number guides yielding beyond the categorical "I was wrong" signal.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1: mixed (the field predicts the categorical error signal drives changes of mind, which favours the side guess; it hypothesises, without data, that a graded number adds to it). Follow-up 2: no bearing.
- head: **rival named**. Backing down where the separate rating says the answer is wrong is error detection, not graded confidence. The cheapest test that excludes it is follow-up 1 (the side guess as a fifth simple guess, plus the margin within agreeing items).

### Vulnerability of LLMs' stated beliefs? LLMs belief resistance check through strategic persuasive conversation interventions — Fan Huang, Haewoon Kwak & Jisun An, 2026, Findings of ACL 2026, pp. 41759-41794 (aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.2074; venue PDF opened directly)
- read_depth: partial (venue PDF via `pdftotext`: abstract, §1, the data-filtering and model sections, §5 "RQ2: Verbalized confidence test" with Table 4 and the Figure 2 caption; §4 and §6 skimmed)
- what_tested: whether persuasion strategies (source, message, receiver framings) erode stated beliefs over five turns, and whether asking for a 0-5 confidence score *in the same response* as the answer changes resistance.
- on_whom: GPT-4o-mini, Llama 3.3-70B, Llama 3.2-3B, Mistral 7B, Qwen 2.5-7B, Qwen 2.5-72B; BoolQ, PubMedQA and LatentHatred items, filtered to those a capable model answered with at least 95% initial confidence. No Claude.
- how_scored: robustness (share of items whose belief survives five turns); turn of first belief change; confidence trajectories.
- result: asking for confidence alongside the answer lowered robustness in 12 of 18 model-dataset cells (67%); GPT-4o-mini averaged -18.7 points. Where beliefs changed, confidence (average 4.2 of 5 at the start) decayed round by round before the change. The Figure 2 caption says lower initial confidence predicts vulnerability, with no number given in the text I read. The smallest model made 82.5% of its belief changes at the first persuasive turn.
- limits: confidence asked in the same turn, so it can change the behaviour it is meant to predict; items pre-filtered to high initial confidence, which leaves little range; multi-turn persuasion with arguments, not a bare doubt.
- borrow: evidence that the *place* the number is asked matters. Asking it inside the conversation can make the model yield more. sv-b5's design (rating in a third, separate conversation) avoids this, and both follow-ups must keep it. SV-9 (number said in the same conversation) would test this directly; it is not one of these two.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: model-specific defences; adversarial fine-tuning (their exploratory RQ3). For us: none beyond keeping the elicitation separate.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1 and follow-up 2: no bearing on the pass rules (separate elicitation, a different challenge); a direction-only hint that low confidence goes with yielding on six non-Claude models.
- head: **design to borrow** (keep the rating in its own conversation; a number asked in the same conversation changes the yielding).

### The meaning and use of the area under a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve — James A. Hanley & Barbara J. McNeil, 1982, Radiology 143:29-36 (scan at math.ucdavis.edu/~saito/data/roc/hanley-mcneil-auc.pdf)
- read_depth: partial (a scanned PDF through `pdftotext`; the text is jumbled in places; the abstract, the AUROC-Wilcoxon equivalence, Formula 1, the Q1 and Q2 approximations and the worked sample-size example were legible; Table III's power figures were not)
- what_tested: that the AUROC equals the probability of ranking a random positive above a random negative (the Wilcoxon statistic); a closed-form standard error; sample sizes.
- on_whom: a worked example of CT ratings for 109 patients (AUROC 0.893, SE 3.2% from the formula against 2.96% from maximum likelihood).
- how_scored: SE(W)² = [A(1-A) + (nA-1)(Q1-A²) + (nN-1)(Q2-A²)] / (nA·nN), with Q1 = A/(2-A) and Q2 = 2A²/(1+A).
- result: their example, A = 0.85 with 40 positives and 40 negatives, gives SE 4.37%; 60 and 60 gives 3.56%. I re-computed both from the formula (0.0437, 0.0356) before using it below.
- limits: one AUROC, not the difference between two correlated AUROCs (the margin), which their 1983 paper covers; assumes few ties; the authors call the approximation slightly conservative.
- borrow: **sizing the replication.** With sv-b5's numbers (A = 0.877, 17 back-downs, 283 holds) the formula gives SE 0.055 for the number's AUROC; with 34 back-downs among 600 items, 0.039. The positives (back-downs) dominate. sv-b5's own bootstrap margin interval was 0.217 wide on 17 back-downs. If its half-width shrinks with the square root of the back-down count, 34 back-downs give about ±0.077 and 56 give about ±0.060. The audit's 300 fresh items would give about 17 again, and about 7 on agreeing items, below the floor of 10.
- next_steps_it_suggests: authors: plan sample sizes before a study. For us: plan the back-down count, not the item count.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1: no outcome prediction; it says 300 fresh items leave the agreeing-items test below its floor and the margin's interval about as wide as sv-b5's. Follow-up 2: about 11 Opus back-downs on 300 items give a margin interval of about ±0.135, so only a point margin well above 0.10 could pass.
- head: **design to borrow** (size by back-downs: about 600 fresh Sonnet items for about 34).

### Re-read: Confidence in the reasoning of large language models — Yudi Pawitan & Chris Holmes, 2025, Harvard Data Science Review 7.1; arXiv 2412.15296 v1 (Dec 2024)
- read_depth: partial (arXiv HTML: Table 1, its caption and §4.3, for the part sv-b5 touches)
- what_tested: whether a self-reported 0-100 confidence score goes with keeping the answer after a rethink prompt.
- on_whom: GPT-4o, GPT-4-turbo, Mistral Large 2; 187 causal-judgment, 250 formal-fallacy and 46 statistical-puzzle questions.
- result (as opened, Table 1): under the Simple rethink prompt, collected in a separate session from the score, the share keeping the answer was 0.77 (score < 95) against 0.93 (≥ 95) for GPT-4o, P = 6.5e-3; 0.27 against 0.42 for GPT-4-turbo, P = 0.093; 0.00 against 0.16 for Mistral, P = 0.057. §4.3 gives P from 0.0065 to 0.093. The score is confidence in the model's own answer, so the "rating points against the answer" case cannot arise in their design. The split they report (below or above 95) is a graded split on the answer's own side. Quote: "collected in a separate session from the confidence-score prompt".
- borrow / bearing: their effect is the graded part sv-b5 could not show (its agreeing-items margin was +0.030 on 7 back-downs). So it predicts that a graded effect can exist on the answer's side, on non-Claude models. It also shows that a design asking confidence *about the answer* would remove the side by construction, a cheap variant for a later bet.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1: mixed (a graded effect exists elsewhere, but it is a 16-point difference in keep rates on one model, not a 0.10 AUROC margin over a side guess). Follow-up 2: no bearing on Opus.
- head: **already shown** (the direction, graded, on three non-Claude models; it supports the Novelty line).

### Re-read: Knowing what you know is not enough (§5, "User Interaction") — Pal, Kitanovski, Liang, Potti & Goldblum, 2025, arXiv 2511.13240 (v3, 9 Feb 2026)
- read_depth: partial (arXiv HTML v3, §5 and the consistency table, for the part sv-b5 touches)
- what_tested: whether a model "sticks" to an answer under "Your answer to the initial question is incorrect" more often when its confidence is higher.
- on_whom: Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 2 9B, Mistral Small 2409 (logit and sampling confidence); GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash (logit only); Code Execution (479), SimpleQA (1,000), GPQA (448), GSM-Symbolic (1,000). No verbal confidence, no Claude.

- result: average consistency 0.836 (GPT-4o, logit), 0.879 (GPT-4o mini), 0.436 (Gemini 2.5 Pro), 0.563 (Gemini 2.5 Flash); sampling-based confidence 0.585, 0.573 and 0.684 on the three open models, against logit 0.039, 0.761 and 0.421. Llama's logit consistency on SimpleQA was -0.891.
- bearing: sampling agreement, a many-sample version of sv-b5's two-sample side, predicts sticking on three open models. That is the SycoLens rival again, measured another way. The audit's suggested resampling arm would test it on Claude.
- predicts_for_us: follow-up 1: mixed (binned confidence orders sticking on most non-Claude models, but no baseline and no side split). Follow-up 2: no bearing.
- head: **already shown** (direction, binned, logit and sampling confidence, non-Claude); no change to the Novelty word.
