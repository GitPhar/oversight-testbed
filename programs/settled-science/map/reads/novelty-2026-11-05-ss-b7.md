## Novelty line
No paper found states whether a "which finding is more striking" judgment is a shortcut for telling a published sentence from a one-word flip of it. Adjacent work: Li et al. 2025 (arXiv 2506.17311) report an LLM reviewer's score rising from 85.8 to 88.4 after exaggerated sentences were added (one paper, five runs); Marioriyad et al. 2025 and 2026 (arXiv 2509.26072, 2602.07996) test provenance, recency and demographic cues on LLM judges but no striking-ness cue; Hoogeveen et al. 2020 (AMPPS 3, 267-285) find for human raters that counterintuitive findings replicate least. Ours tests the cue on an LLM directly and finds it near chance. Verdict: new. Search: WebSearch for "LLM judge preference for striking sensational claims predicting published results", "LLM judge novelty surprising bold claims bias", "hype overclaiming LLM judge exaggerated claims", "laypeople predict replication surprising findings"; arXiv abstract and HTML pages for the hits.
## Papers opened
- Li, Hu, Xu, Li, Zhang, Cheng, 2025, "Can Large Language Models Be Trusted Paper Reviewers? A Feasibility Study", https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17311, https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17311. Experiment 3: adding exaggerated, unsubstantiated sentences to one paper raised the GPT-4o reviewer's mean score from 85.8 to 88.4 over five runs; "influenced by exaggerated language". An LLM judge preference for exaggeration, not a test of striking-ness as a predictor of which sentence was published.
- Marioriyad, Rohban, Soleymani Baghshah, 2025, "The Silent Judge: Unacknowledged Shortcut Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge", https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26072. Provenance and recency cues on GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Flash; "justifications almost never reference the injected cues". No striking-ness cue; does not bear on it directly.
- Marioriyad, Ghahroodi, Asgari, Rohban, Soleymani Baghshah, 2026, "The Judge Who Never Admits: Hidden Shortcuts in LLM-based Evaluation", https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07996. Six cue families (source, temporal, age, gender, ethnicity, educational status); no striking-ness cue; does not bear on it directly.
- Qiu, Chen, Li, 2025, "Counterfactual LLM-based Framework for Measuring Rhetorical Style", https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.19908. LLM judge compares rhetorical strength of counterfactual rewrites of 8,485 ICLR submissions; "visionary framing significantly predicts downstream attention". Measures rhetoric, does not relate it to which result was published or true.
- Hoogeveen, Sarafoglou, Wagenmakers, 2020, "Laypeople Can Predict Which Social-Science Studies Will Be Replicated Successfully", AMPPS 3(3), 267-285, https://dare.uva.nl/id/8f065863-0d6e-45ca-8329-846058325ccc and the APS Observer summary https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/laypeople-predict-replication. Laypeople 59% accurate (67% with evidence strength shown); authors quoted as writing that "intuitive (i.e., unsurprising) effects are more replicable than highly surprising ones". Human raters, replication not publication; adjacent only.
- Luo et al., 2024, BrainBench (see ss-b5), https://arxiv.org/html/2403.03230. No striking-ness or valence analysis; does not bear on it.
## Not found / caveats
- No paper was found that asks an LLM which of two result sentences is more striking and scores that against the published one, or that names sensationalism as a candidate shortcut in LLM prediction of published findings.
- The Hoogeveen plausibility sentences were read from the APS Observer article quoting the authors; the journal page returned 403 and the repository abstract gives only the accuracy numbers.
- Li et al.'s exaggeration result rests on one paper and five runs, as the authors' own text shows.
- Search summaries mentioned 2026 papers on LLM judges overrating the novelty of research questions (arXiv 2606.12071, 2608.25660); these were not opened and concern novelty ratings, not a published-versus-flipped prediction.
