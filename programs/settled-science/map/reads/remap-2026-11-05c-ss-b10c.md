## Papers read

### Pairwise or Pointwise? Evaluating Feedback Protocols for Bias in LLM-Based Evaluation. Tripathi, Wadhwa, Durrett, Niekum, 2025, arXiv preprint (v2, Aug 2025), https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14716
- read_depth: full (HTML v2)
- what_tested: pairwise preference (with a tie option) against absolute 1–7 scoring for LLM judges. Each was tested under three distractor features (assertiveness, prolixity, sycophancy) and in a variable-quality setup.
- on_whom: LLaMA3.2-3B, LLaMA3.3-70B, Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-72B as judges (o3-mini and o4-mini in one table). Datasets: IFEval-TweakSet (400 samples) and MT-Bench human-judgment split (80 questions, 1,689 turn-1 responses).
- how_scored: flip rate when a distractor is added, and the share of ties on identical-quality pairs.
- result: across models, distractors flip pairwise preferences in about 35% of cases against about 9% for absolute scores (assertiveness 35.5 against 9.0; prolixity 35.2 against 9.25; sycophancy 34.9 against 9.7). On identical-quality pairs, absolute scoring gives ties in 84.6–93.2% of cases and pairwise in 2.4–7.3%. Pairwise runs were done in both orders and aggregated. "Pairwise preferences are especially susceptible to distracted evaluation."
- limits: no Claude judge. One criterion (instruction following), three distractors, no chain-of-thought feedback. No clean accuracy comparison of the two protocols without distractors.
- borrow: the audit's "neutral presentation" in its pointwise form, with each abstract rated alone so that no order exists. The tie rate must be pre-registered and reported. BrainBench pairs differ by a few words, so a rater that cannot tell them apart will give equal ratings, and ties scored 0.5 pull accuracy toward chance.
- next_steps_it_suggests: use absolute scoring for correctness-type judgments; avoid binary preferences on data with weak preference strength, where they "exaggerate small gaps".
- predicts_for_us: for draft follow-up 1 (one abstract per call), it predicts the order artefact goes away. It also predicts many ties wherever the rater has no real preference. So it predicts pass if Haiku's averaged lean is a real judgment, and a drop toward chance if it is not. It has no bearing on draft follow-up 2.
- **Design to borrow.** Rating each version alone removes position by construction. Pointwise ratings tie often, so the tie rule and the tie rate belong in the pre-registration.

### Fragile Preferences: A Deep Dive into Order Effects in Large Language Models. Yin, Vardi, Choudhary, arXiv 2506.14092 (v4, revised July 2026), https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14092
- read_depth: partial (the first 100,000 of about 161,000 characters: methods, results, discussion, recommendations; the appendix was not read)
- what_tested: position effects in pairwise and three-way choices between resumes and between paint colours, in four quality tiers. It also tests how order interacts with name and gender bias.
- on_whom: GPT-4o-mini, Claude 3 Haiku and Llama 3 8B in the main text, and nine models (Claude Sonnet 4 among them) on colours. Each model saw 768 same-gender and 1,024 cross-gender resume pairs per temperature, and 50 repeats per permutation for colours.
- how_scored: selection frequency by position, binomial tests and Cohen's h.
- result: high-quality options show primacy and low-quality options recency. For Claude 3 Haiku on colours at T=1 (first/second %): Ideal 73/27, Fair 15/85. Order effects were stronger than gender effects for all three models. The authors note "when all options are high quality, models favor the first option", and they say averaging across orderings does not reliably recover the underlying preferences.
- limits: synthetic items written by GPT-4o-mini. No comparison of ratings with pairwise choices. Model size not analysed. No limitations section.
- borrow: report the position pull separately for items where the rater does and does not lean toward the original, as the "first only / second only / both" columns in ss-b10c already begin to do.
- next_steps_it_suggests: repeat queries at T=1 to reveal fragile preferences; treat strong primacy as a diagnostic that both options look good; audit new models for order bias.
- predicts_for_us: the primacy-when-both-look-good pattern fits Haiku's 52 "right only when shown first" items. If both BrainBench versions look like good news to Haiku, averaging rewards the first-shown magnitude, not a judgment. For draft follow-up 1 it predicts that Haiku's accuracy falls once order is removed. That is fail for the Haiku conjunct if those 52 items carry no real preference.
- **Rival named.** Haiku's averaged pass may be primacy on pairs it sees as equally good, which averaging does not undo. The pointwise test in draft follow-up 1 excludes it at the lowest cost.

### "Positive" Results Increase Down the Hierarchy of the Sciences. Fanelli, 2010, PLoS ONE 5(4): e10068, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0010068
- read_depth: full (the figure values for single disciplines were not readable in the text)
- what_tested: the share of papers that report full or partial support for the hypothesis they tested, by discipline.
- on_whom: 2,434 papers that stated they "test* the hypothes*", from 20 Essential Science Indicators disciplines.
- how_scored: the author coded each paper's first hypothesis as supported or not. An untrained assistant agreed with the coding in 18 of 20 cases.
- result: 2,045 of 2,434 (84%) report support. The range runs from Space Science (70.2%) to Psychology and Psychiatry (91.5%). Soft against hard disciplines OR 1.529 (1.037–2.116). Behavioural and social studies on humans against non-humans OR 2.046 (1.303–3.213). "Positive results were distributed non-randomly between disciplines."
- limits: not blind to discipline. Only papers that use the phrase. First hypothesis only. Low explained variance (Nagelkerke R² 0.030–0.051).
- borrow: the base rate behind a valence rule, in metascience words. If most published abstracts support their own hypothesis, any minimal alteration of the result tends to make the abstract worse news for that hypothesis. A "better news" question can then succeed without any knowledge of neuroscience.
- next_steps_it_suggests: subfield-level analyses, and tests of publication bias and HARKing by discipline.
- predicts_for_us: no bearing on either draft pass rule. It explains why the shortcut can work on any set of published-against-altered abstracts, which makes a second set a weak test of the mechanism unless its alterations are balanced for valence.
- **Changes nothing** for the follow-ups. It is the mechanism in another field's words, not a test, and it states nothing about LLMs or BrainBench.

### Can artificial intelligence predict clinical trial outcomes? Jin, Chen, Ding, Wang, Yu, arXiv 2411.17595 (v2, March 2025), https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17595v2
- read_depth: full (HTML v2)
- what_tested: LLMs and the HINT model predicting whether ClinicalTrials.gov trials succeed or fail from their registry text.
- on_whom: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-8B-Instruct and HINT. 2,163 trials in the overall table (1,709 successes, 452 failures). Per-model n varies (GPT-4 54, GPT-4o 74).
- how_scored: balanced accuracy, MCC, recall and specificity.
- result: recall/specificity was GPT-4o-mini 1.000/0.000, GPT-4 1.000/0.059, GPT-3.5 0.998/0.008, GPT-4o 0.913/0.150 and Llama3 0.964/0.092. HINT was 0.577/0.473. The LLMs show "a strong bias toward predicting all cases as positive". Balanced accuracy is 0.500–0.532 for every LLM.
- limits: 79% of trials succeeded, which confounds a success bias with the base rate. Some models have tiny n. The text and Table 1 disagree for GPT-4o. One prompt. No mechanism tested.
- borrow: report a rater's lean toward good news apart from its accuracy, as ss-b10c's "right both / first only" columns do.
- next_steps_it_suggests: hybrids of LLM and HINT; better prediction of negative outcomes.
- predicts_for_us: no bearing on either draft pass rule. BrainBench has one real and one altered version per item, so a blanket "say success" answer scores 50%.
- **Changes nothing.** A lean toward good news in LLM outcome prediction is in print here as an answer bias. It is not in print as a rule that picks the real result from an altered one, so the Novelty line is unaffected.

### Dead Science Walking: Publication Bias and the AI Scientist Pipeline. Chauhan, 2026, arXiv 2606.04220 (v1, 2 June 2026), https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04220
- read_depth: full (HTML v1)
- what_tested: nothing empirical. It is a position paper with a formal "null result gap" and multiplicative amplification estimates.
- on_whom: none. It cites Zheng et al. 2024 (judge position and verbosity bias), Peters and Chin-Yee 2025 (LLM summaries that overgeneralise) and Thelwall et al. 2025.
- how_scored: no scoring.
- result: it assumes an evaluator preference for "fluent, positive, and novel claims" (α₃ = 1.2) and a positive-framing multiplier (α₂ = 1.3), which give an amplification index of about 2.18. Psychology is put at "about 92%" of hypotheses presented as successful, against about 36% replicated. It does not mention BrainBench. "The null result gap is not a property of language models."
- limits: the multipliers are rounded translations from other literatures, not measurements. It assumes the stages are independent. The taxonomy is not validated.
- borrow: its proposed next step, an LLM-judge comparison of positive against null narratives under controlled factual support, is the mechanism test the ss-b10c audit names (pairs where the altered version is the better news).
- next_steps_it_suggests: corpora with known null-result coverage; matched-hypothesis tests; positive-against-null LLM-judge evaluations.
- predicts_for_us: no bearing on either draft pass rule (no data).
- **Changes nothing.** It is a 2026 hypothesis that evaluators favour positive claims, with no measurement, so it is not a prior statement of ss-b10c's result.
