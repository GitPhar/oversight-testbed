### "Positive" Results Increase Down the Hierarchy of the Sciences — Fanelli, 2010, PLoS ONE 5(4):e10068, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2850928
- read_depth: full (PMC full text)
- what_tested: whether the share of papers reporting support for their tested hypothesis differs by discipline.
- on_whom: 2,434 papers that declared testing a hypothesis, sampled across all disciplines in Essential Science Indicators.
- how_scored: each paper coded positive (full or partial support) or negative; logistic regression on discipline, controlling for pure or applied and single or multiple hypotheses.
- result: 84% positive overall (2,045 papers). Space science was lowest (70.2%) and psychology and psychiatry highest (91.5%). Odds of a positive result relative to space science: psychiatry/psychology OR 4.935 [2.381, 10.230], economics and business 5.073 [2.385, 10.792]. The models' predictive power "never exceeded a 5.1% reduction in error" (Nagelkerke R² 0.051).
- limits: one coder, not blind to discipline; only the first hypothesis per paper; English-language journals; broad categories.
- borrow: for F2, choose fields for a measured spread in outcome rates before building items, and expect even broad discipline differences to explain little.
- next_steps_it_suggests: "more refined analyses" by subfield and specific factors.
- predicts_for_us: F2 (new fields chosen to differ): field rates are real but weak, so the bettor is predicted to beat a field rule (pass). F1 (finer answer-free rule): no direct bearing.
- **already shown:** that field labels carry little outcome signal, even across broad disciplines with a 70% to 92% spread, is in print. ss-b11's audit finding (the four fields fit no better than shuffled labels) agrees with it, so "more than field habits" was never a strong rival on these items.

### Predicting replicability: analysis of survey and prediction market data from large-scale forecasting projects — Gordon, Viganola, Dreber, Johannesson and Pfeiffer, 2021, PLOS ONE 16(4):e0248780, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8046229
- read_depth: full (PMC full text)
- what_tested: how well surveys and prediction markets forecast replication outcomes, pooled across four replication projects.
- on_whom: 103 findings from RPP, EERP, ML2 and SSRP; academic forecasters.
- how_scored: binary accuracy at 0.5, correlation with outcomes, absolute error; meta-analysis across projects.
- result: base rate 51 of 103 replicated (49%); the projects ranged from 37.5% (RPP, 15/40) to 61.9% (SSRP, 13/21). Markets were right 73% (75/103), surveys 66%. Forecasters leaned positive: mean market belief 0.63 and survey 0.61 against the actual 0.49. One answer-free item cue, the original p-value, predicted outcomes (p ≤ 0.005: about 74% replicated; above: about 28%; R² 0.2).
- limits: four projects only, so heterogeneity is imprecise; p-values often reported as inequalities; low-powered replications.
- borrow: for F1, an item-level answer-free cue can carry far more signal than the field label (R² 0.2 against Fanelli's 0.051). F1's finer rule should use item-level text cues (the up word, the headline tag, option order, option length) under cross-validation, not more field cells.
- next_steps_it_suggests: calibrate elicited beliefs; more data; other evidence-strength measures such as effect sizes.
- predicts_for_us: F1: an answer-free item cue could be a strong rival, so no clean prediction (unknown). F2: project-level base rates differ (38% to 62%) yet forecasters beat them, which predicts pass.
- **design to borrow:** item-level answer-free cues as the stronger shortcut. Also a second sighting of the positive lean (beliefs 0.63 against 0.49) that the good-news rule stands for.

### A discipline-wide investigation of the replicability of Psychology papers over the past two decades — Youyou, Yang and Uzzi, 2023, PNAS 120(6):e2208863120, https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10164573/1/pnas.2208863120.pdf
- read_depth: full (main text, figures and tables, pp. 1–8 of the PDF; the SI appendix not read)
- what_tested: predicted replication likelihood for nearly every paper in six top psychology journals, by subfield, method and paper metrics.
- on_whom: 14,126 papers, 2000–2019; text model trained on 388 manual replications (overall success 43.3%).
- how_scored: an ensemble of random forest and logistic models on word2vec text vectors; threefold cross-validated AUC 0.74.
- result: predicted subfield means ranged from 0.36 (developmental) and 0.37 (social) to 0.55 (personality). Manual replications agree where n ≥ 30: personality 77% (n = 30), cognitive 50% (n = 90), social 38% (n = 256). Method explains part of the subfield gap: non-experimental papers averaged 0.50 and experimental 0.39 (t = 34.22, Cohen's d = 0.78).
- limits: one journal per subfield for five of six; estimates approximate; little training data for clinical and developmental psychology.
- borrow: for F2, a within-field design feature (experiment or not) can account for much of an apparent field difference. F2 should report whether its field rule still beats a pooled rule once such a feature is in it.
- next_steps_it_suggests: combine with prediction markets and numerical-feature models; repeat the census in other disciplines.
- predicts_for_us: F2: subfields can differ by about 0.2 in outcome rate, so a set where a field rule has real signal can be built (supports testability); the bettor beating it is not predicted either way. F1: no bearing.
- **design to borrow:** choose fields that differ and check that the difference is the field's, not a method mix.

## 3. What the re-scout changes

The weakness of field labels is already shown (Fanelli 2010: R² 0.051 across disciplines; Altmejd et al. 2019), and it is what ss-b11's audit found. So no follow-up on round 1's four fields can test "more than field habits". F2 adds new items in fields chosen for a measured spread, with rates fitted on pre-cutoff items: neither in-sample nor leave-one-out (P11). Gordon et al. 2021 shows item-level answer-free cues beat field labels (R² 0.2), so F1, the audit's $0 finer rule, uses item-level cues under cross-validation. F1 is a secondary of ss-b1 and ss-b13.

## 4. Novelty (added; the ledger row had none)

**Novelty:** partly new. No paper found scores a language model's predictions of published findings against a rule that knows each field's base rates. The nearest work finds that field labels carry little outcome signal: discipline explained at most 5.1% of the variance in positive results across 2,434 papers (Fanelli 2010, PLoS ONE 5:e10068), and discipline dummies had "little bearing" on a replication predictor (Altmejd et al. 2019, PLOS ONE 14:e0225826). Ours agrees: the Opus bettor beat the field-aware rule (Brier 0.163 against 0.223), but the four fields fit no better than shuffled labels, so in effect it beat pooled rates, which ss-b2 had already shown; what is new is only the comparison itself, on one run scored a fourth time.
