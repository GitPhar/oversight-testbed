## In plain English

BrainBench (Luo et al., *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2024) shows a real neuroscience abstract beside a copy an expert rewrote so the results come out differently. Language models picked the real one 81.4% of the time and 171 neuroscientists 63.4%, read as "AI beats experts at predicting science."

In ss-b10 we asked two Claude models only **which version is better news for the paper's own idea**, telling them to treat both as hypothetical. Sonnet picked the real abstract 81.8% of the time; Haiku 68.0%. But Haiku mostly picks whichever version is shown first. So here every item was shown both ways round and the two answers were averaged, which cancels a first-position habit. Because ss-b10 had already rated half the items both ways, and that half's result was public, the test was read only on the items whose second rating is new: 100 for Haiku, 126 for Sonnet.

- **Both models beat the neuroscientists by a wide margin. The bet passes.** Haiku picked the real abstract **77.5%** of the time (95% interval 69.5% to 85.0%), Sonnet **89.7%** (84.5% to 94.4%), against the experts' 63.4%.
- **Sonnet, asked only the "better news" question, beat the published 81.4% for language models.** Haiku was not shown different from it. Those models were scored by perplexity, a different measurement, so this is a reference point, not a matched comparison.
- **The forecasting prompt did only a little better.** Asked directly which abstract was published (both orders), Haiku was right 79.5% and Sonnet 93.7%. The "better news" prompt reached about nine-tenths of each model's above-chance accuracy (Haiku 93%, interval 67% to 130%; Sonnet 91%, 81% to 100%). The two prompts differ in three ways (instruction, a question line, a note that one version was altered), so the gap belongs to that bundle.
- **Opus, on 41 items, picked the real abstract 97.6% of the time with the "better news" question** (92.7% to 100%), and 97.5% with the forecasting prompt. That is a small secondary, about as many items as its $1.50 allowed.
- **The position habit is real and large.** With one order, Haiku was right 93% of the time when the real abstract came first and 42% when it came second; it put about 14 points on Option 1 whatever was there. Averaging both orders removes most of it.
- **On all 200 items** (half of them already in ss-b10's archive), Haiku's averaged accuracy was 76.2% and Sonnet's 88.2%; both clear the experts.

**What this does and does not show.** A one-line question that needs no knowledge of results, "which version is better news for the paper's own idea?", picks the real BrainBench abstract far more often than the neuroscientists did, on both smaller Claude models, once position bias is averaged out. It does **not** show how. The models may be judging valence, judging biological plausibility (ss-b10's audit found most "real-sounding" rationales argued from plausibility), or remembering the papers: BrainBench and its 2023 abstracts were public before these models were trained. It does not show that the models in the original study used any such rule. One benchmark, one wording of each prompt, one model family.

## What it cannot do

- **Memory and plausibility.** Nothing here separates a valence judgment from recognising the paper or from prior plausibility.
- **One order known before data.** On every scored item one of the two flattery ratings is ss-b10's, already published; only the reversed rating is new.
- **Not the original study's method** (perplexity on base models), one benchmark, one prompt wording each, one model family; Opus on 41 items only.
- No "LLMs", "every model" or "all" claim.
