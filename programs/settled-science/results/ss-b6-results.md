## In plain English

In round 2 the bettor picked the published sentence over its one-word-flipped twin 93% of the time. The audit pointed out a simpler explanation it had not ruled out: published sentences usually report the paper's win, so "pick whichever version makes the paper look good" might do as well, without knowing any result.

To test that, a fresh session of the same model, with no tools, saw the same 92 sentence pairs and was asked only which version is *better news for the method the paper itself proposes*. It was told to treat both as hypothetical. **That flattery rule alone picked the published sentence 90% of the time** (Brier 0.106), against the bettor's 93% (Brier 0.070). **So a same-model rater asked only which version flatters the paper matches most of round 2's result.**

The bettor did slightly better, and that clears the pre-registered bar, but only just:
- **Raw comparison:** the bettor wins by 0.036 (interval 0.016 to 0.057).
- **Recalibrated comparison** (both sides given the "higher / more / better" word and fitted the same way): it wins by 0.027 (interval 0.002 to 0.054). The upper bound sits a hair below zero.
- **Fragile:** removing any one of three items (r2-056, r2-059, r2-063) turns it into a null, and so does dropping either the interpretability or the model-psychology query bucket. Removing the single most favourable item flips it.
- **The rater may be noisier than the bettor, though the difference is not shown.** Re-asked on 46 items with the options reversed, the rater gave the same side 91% of the time, against the bettor's 98% on the same items. Its average inconsistency was 5.0 points against 4.2 (paired difference +0.8 [−0.8, +2.6]). The design review warned that a noisier rater can lose to a bettor that knows only flattery: a false pass about 8% of the time at the bettor's own retest noise, and 20–66% at larger noise. If the bettor knew only flattery, the rater's ratings would differ from the bettor's by about 0.9 on the logit scale, which is what they do; at that noise a bettor that knows nothing beyond the rater would still pass this test about 58% of the time (auditor's simulation, 100 draws). **So the test cannot say whether the bettor knows anything beyond what flatters the paper: the rule it set is passed about half the time by a bettor that knows nothing more (round-3 audit, 53% in 100 draws with independent code), so the verdict is undetermined, not pass.**

**The bettor and the rater both ran as claude-opus-5-5 with adaptive thinking at effort low, with reasoning hidden; the result is about that setting.** (D18, D20.)

## What it cannot do

- **The rater is the same model as the bettor.** It may share the bettor's sense of which version is real. G3 found 8 rationales that reasoned that way, below the threshold of 10.
- **"Better news for the paper's method" is one prompt's judgment.** A "more striking finding" (publishability) rival is not tested by this prompt (D32).
- **It covers only the round-2 template items.** It says nothing about round 1.
