## In plain English

Round 2's bettor picked the published sentence 93% of the time. A same-model rater asked which version flatters the paper matched most of that, at 90% (ss-b6). The ss-b6 audit named one more simple explanation it had not tested: the bettor might be picking whichever version reports the more **striking** finding.

So a fresh session of the same model saw the same 92 sentence pairs, with only the field and the two sentences, and was asked which version reports the more striking finding. **That rule was about as good as a coin.** It picked the published sentence 52% of the time (Brier 0.272, slightly worse than always saying 50%). The bettor beat it by a wide margin, on both the raw comparison and the recalibrated one:
- the bettor's edge stays well beyond the rater's call-to-call noise margin;
- no single item or query bucket changes the verdict;
- all 20 extra seeds agree.

On the 39 items where the two favoured different sentences, the bettor was right on 37.

**What this rules out, and what it doesn't.** It rules out "the bettor picks the more striking version" as an explanation of round 2: this rater's sense of what is striking barely tracks which sentence was published. It does **not** show that the bettor knows results. The flattery rule from ss-b6 still matches most of round 2. This rater is also so different from the bettor that almost any reasonable bettor would beat it: a bettor that differed from it only by stable item-level noise of the size observed would pass about 98% of the time. So the pass is decisive against this one rival, and it says nothing beyond that.

**Both the bettor and the rater ran as claude-opus-5-5 with adaptive thinking at effort low, with reasoning hidden; the result is about that setting.**

## What it cannot do

- **The rater is the same model as the bettor.** "More striking" is one prompt's reading of publishability.
- **A pass rules out this rival only.** World B shows the comparison was easy: the rater barely tracks the published sentence.
- **It does not test the flattery rule (ss-b6), round 1, or whether the bettor knows results** rather than reasoning within the sentence.
