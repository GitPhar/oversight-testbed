### Hawthorne systematic review — McCambridge, Witton & Elbourne, 2014, J Clin Epidemiol 67(3):267–277, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3969247/
- read_depth: full (PMC HTML: methods, results, Table 1, discussion; read through a summarising fetch, with the key sentences re-fetched word for word)
- what_tested: whether being aware of being studied changes the behaviour being studied, across all designs built to test this. Two kinds of awareness: answering questions (6 studies), and being directly observed or otherwise aware of being studied (8 studies). One study combined both.
- on_whom: 19 studies (8 RCTs, 5 quasi-experimental, 6 observational); 14 with binary outcomes were meta-analysed (5 RCT, 5 quasi, 4 observational). All but one were in health sciences.
- how_scored: pooled odds ratio of the behavioural outcome, aware vs less-aware group, by design and by type of awareness.
- result: overall OR 1.17 (95% CI 1.06–1.30), I² = 93.3%. **RCTs only: 1.06 (0.98–1.14), not significant; quasi-experimental 1.07 (0.99–1.17); observational 1.29.** Being observed or studied: 1.21 (1.03–1.41). Answering questions: 1.07 (1.00–1.15). 12 of 19 studies showed some significant effect; 5 showed clearly none. Example: hand-rub compliance 29% → 45% when observed (Eckmanns). Only 2 studies showed effects past 6 months.
- limitations: heterogeneous operationalisations (the authors' own "apples and oranges"); no protocol; no formal risk-of-bias assessment; post-hoc subgroups; grey literature and older or non-health studies not searched.
- borrow: **the effect shrinks as the design gets stronger**: observational OR 1.29, randomized OR 1.06. For an API test this means randomizing the "you are being evaluated" cue within the same item set, not comparing benchmark prompts with "real" prompts that also differ in content. Their split into "answering questions" (assessment reactivity) and "being watched" maps onto two separate LLM manipulations: (a) a prior self-rating question, (b) a monitoring statement.
- next_steps_it_suggests: drop the unqualified label "Hawthorne effect"; study "research participation effects" mechanism by mechanism, with a conceptual framework of possible pathways.
- quote: "little can be securely known about the conditions under which they operate"

### Demand characteristics — Orne, 1962, American Psychologist 17(11):776–783, https://pages.ucsd.edu/~cmckenzie/Orne1962AmPsychologist.pdf
- read_depth: full (scanned copy; text extracted)
- what_tested: the idea that subjects read cues from the setting about what the experiment wants, play the role of a "good subject", and that this role, not the manipulation, can produce the effect. Methods for detecting it: post-experimental inquiry, pre-experimental inquiry ("non-experiment": describe the procedure to someone and ask how they would respond), and simulator controls.
- on_whom: informal pilots of 3–4 subjects per group (serial-addition task: one subject kept going for 5.5 hours, and subjects kept going when every card said to tear up the sheet they had just finished). Meaning-deprivation study: 10 vs 10 subjects, identical 4 hours of isolation, with release forms and a panic button vs "you are controls" with no panic button.
- how_scored: 14 quantifiable measures from sensory-deprivation research; a Mann-Whitney U test on summed ranks.
- result: 13 of 14 measures went in the predicted direction, 6 were significant at α = .10, one-tailed p = .001 overall. So "deprivation effects" appeared with **no deprivation**, produced only by the trappings that signal what the experiment expects. Simulators "deceiving even well-trained hypnotists".
- limitations: tiny informal samples; Orne says the quasi-controls have their own demand characteristics; the simulator group shows only how much of an effect demand cues *could* explain, not how much they did.
- borrow: two cheap controls. (1) **Simulator arm**: tell the model "act as a model that knows this is a safety evaluation" and see whether the "real" test-cue arm moves the same way and as far. If an instructed simulator matches the cued arm, the cued shift is in the range of plain role-following. (2) **Trappings-only arm**: copy the look of an eval (scoring rubric header, "Item 14 of 50", JSON grading format) with no statement that it is a test. This separates the format cues from explicit telling. Also: the "pact of ignorance" warns that asking the model afterwards whether it knew is itself a demand.
- next_steps_it_suggests: work out which experimental contexts demand characteristics matter in, using quasi-controls alongside the real experiment.
- quote: "legitimized by the quasi-magical phrase, 'This is an experiment,'"

### Demand effects in survey experiments — Mummolo & Peterson, 2019, APSR 113(2):517–529, https://jmummolo.scholar.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf3341/files/mummolo_peterson_2019_0.pdf
- read_depth: full (author PDF; all sections except the online appendix)
- what_tested: whether telling participants the hypothesis changes the treatment effect. Five published experiments were replicated (a framing study, partisan news choice in a conjoint, the democratic peace, welfare deservingness, a résumé race study). Three schemes, randomized independently of the treatment: **Gradation** (no info / hint / explicit hypothesis); **Directional** (explicit hypothesis that the effect is positive vs one that it is negative, each with a plausible justification); **Incentive** (explicit hypothesis plus a $0.25 bonus "if your responses support this theory").
- on_whom: 5 surveys, >12,000 respondents, >28,000 responses. M-Turk n = 1,395 / 1,635 / 1,874; Qualtrics n = 2,374 / 5,550.
- how_scored: **difference-in-differences**: (treated − control | informed) − (treated − control | uninformed). Manipulation check: afterwards, pick the study's hypothesis from a menu of 6–7.
- result: uninformed respondents guessed the hypothesis 33% of the time. Hint and explicit raised correct guessing by 6 and 14 points (framing study) and by 5 and 19 points (news study); directional statements raised it by 10–22 points. **No detectable change in treatment effects from information alone**, in any scheme. The only borderline effect was in the opposite direction (news study 1) and did not replicate. With the bonus, M-Turk effects grew (democratic peace +14 points; welfare +5 points, borderline). Qualtrics showed none, even with the bonus. "Clever" respondents (those who correctly guessed the hypothesis of their first experiment) had a treatment × guess interaction of 0.06 (SE 0.04, n = 1,232), not significant. The authors' conservative upper bound is a demand effect of 2–3 points (ATE 0.18 → 0.21).
- limitations: survey experiments only, with no live experimenter. Many respondents were inattentive, so effects are closer to intention-to-treat. They cannot rule out demand effects present at baseline; topics or subject pools may differ.
- borrow: this is **the design that most cleanly separates "knows what the test wants" from wording**. (1) The diff-in-diff estimand: always measure a known, prompt-driven effect *inside* the informed and uninformed arms, so a plain wording shift cancels. (2) The **directional scheme** breaks the observational equivalence between sincere and compliant answers: if a model follows a stated hypothesis in *both* directions, that is compliance with the instruction, not a stable "test persona". (3) An incentive arm ("responses that support this will be scored higher") is the model analogue of the bonus, and separates *can* from *will*. (4) A multiple-choice manipulation check in a separate call. (5) Post-treatment-bias guard: classify "clever" on an *earlier* item and look at a *later* one.
- next_steps_it_suggests: test for variation by topic and by how experienced the subject pool is.
- quote: "knowledge of a researcher's expectations does not alter the behavior of survey participants"

### Watching eyes, honesty box — Bateson, Nettle & Roberts, 2006, Biology Letters 2(3):412–414, https://www.staff.ncl.ac.uk/melissa.bateson/Bateson_etal_2006.pdf
- read_depth: full
- what_tested: whether a photocopied pair of eyes above the price list raises payments into an honesty box for coffee and tea, compared with an image of flowers.
- on_whom: a pool of 48 staff (25 female, 23 male) in a university psychology division, over 10 weeks with the image type alternating weekly and a different image each week. Participants were naive to the manipulation.
- how_scored: money collected per litre of milk used (milk as a proxy for consumption); a GLM on log ratio with image type and week.
- result: eyes weeks £0.417 ± 0.081 per litre vs flowers £0.151 ± 0.030 per litre, **2.76×**; F(1,7) = 11.551, p = 0.011; the model explained 63.8% of variance; image type did not affect consumption.
- limitations: one room, a unit of analysis of 10 weeks, no individual data. The authors argue against an attention explanation, but there was no third image, such as a non-eye face or a matched-salience image, to rule it out.
- borrow: the **matched-salience control image** is the transferable part. Flowers occupy the same space as the eyes. The LLM analogue of the "eyes" is a one-line monitoring cue ("This session is logged and reviewed by the safety team"); the analogue of the "flowers" is an equal-length, equally salient neutral line ("This session uses the standard response format"), never an empty prompt. The rotating image per week also maps to **rotating several paraphrases of each cue**, so no single string carries the effect.
- next_steps_it_suggests: the authors suggest subtle cues may explain cooperation seen in other experiments, a claim later meta-analyses test (records 5–6).
- quote: "People paid nearly three times as much for their drinks when eyes were displayed"

### Watching eyes and generosity, two meta-analyses — Northover, Pedersen, Cohen & Andrews, 2017, Evolution and Human Behavior 38(1):144–153, doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2016.07.001
- read_depth: abstract (paywalled). Numbers come from the first author's SPSP blog post and from Dear et al. 2019's summary of it; no full text was opened.
- what_tested: whether artificial surveillance cues (eye images) raise generosity.
- on_whom: amount given: 26 experiments, 2,732 participants; proportion who gave: 27 experiments, 19,512 participants.
- how_scored: meta-analytic effect sizes for amount given and for the proportion giving anything.
- result: **no significant effect on either outcome.** Secondary search summaries give effect sizes of .03 and .13; I could not confirm these against the text. A companion meta-analysis on moral judgment (6 studies) also found an effect near zero (per Dear et al.).
- limitations: Dear et al. criticise the inclusion of studies run in darkness and of boundary-condition studies coded as plain nulls.
- borrow: the **failed-replication benchmark**. The 2006 honesty-box effect (2.76×) does not survive pooling for generosity. A cheap LLM "watched" cue that moves a prosocial measure should therefore be treated as fragile until replicated across paraphrases and models.
- next_steps_it_suggests: (not read).

### Watching eyes and antisocial behaviour — Dear, Dutton & Fox, 2019, Evolution and Human Behavior 40(3):269–280, https://zepcam.com/shared-files/19293/Do-‘watching-eyes-influence-antisocial-behavior-A-systematic-review-Analysis.pdf
- read_depth: full (PDF text extracted: abstract, methods, results, discussion, further research)
- what_tested: whether eye cues reduce *antisocial* behaviour (littering, theft, failing to pay), on the hypothesis that antisocial acts damage reputation more consistently than generosity improves it.
- on_whom: 15 experiments from 13 papers, 2,035 participants; extended analysis adding three large field studies.
- how_scored: log odds ratio of antisocial behaviour, eyes vs control; fixed and random effects; trim-and-fill, p-curve and PET-PEESE for publication bias.
- result: main analysis OR 0.65, a **35% reduction**; fixed-effects log OR −0.42 (95% CI −0.59 to −0.26). Extended: fixed-effects OR 0.88 (~12%), Q(14) = 68.54, p < .01; random-effects OR 0.65 (log OR −0.43, CI −0.76 to −0.11). The large Keep Britain Tidy dog-fouling field trial found a small *increase* in antisocial behaviour with eyes. No publication bias detected (PET-PEESE significant, though the authors discuss its limits). The lab replications in the second author's thesis failed (secondary).
- limitations: few studies; high heterogeneity; PET-PEESE is unreliable with few heterogeneous studies; the eyes effect on generosity is acknowledged as weak.
- borrow: (1) **Outcome direction matters**: predict that a monitoring cue moves *antisocial or rule-breaking* outputs (taking a forbidden shortcut, overclaiming) more than *prosocial* ones (generosity in a split). Pre-register both directions. (2) Their **ceiling point**: in the lab, participants feel watched even without a cue. An LLM given any prompt may already assume it is being evaluated, so the "unwatched" arm may need an active de-evaluation cue ("this is a private scratch session, not logged"), not just the absence of a cue. Measure the baseline against both.
- next_steps_it_suggests: show directly that eye cues make people *feel* watched (a manipulation check), replicate the public self-awareness moderator, and run carefully designed field studies.
- quote: "difficulty in avoiding participants feeling watched even when they aren't under surveillance"

### Scrutiny and lab-field gaps — Levitt & List, 2007, J Economic Perspectives 21(2):153–174, https://www.sjsu.edu/people/tom.means/courses/econ201/s2/Levitt-ListJELv21n2.pdf
- read_depth: full (JSTOR scan with text layer: model, "Scrutiny that is unparalleled in the field", "Anonymity in the lab and the field", selection, conclusions)
- what_tested: a review and model. Utility U = M(a, v, n, s) + W(a, v): moral cost rises with stakes v, norms n and **scrutiny s**, so lab scrutiny exaggerates prosocial behaviour.
- on_whom: secondary evidence. List 2006 sports-card dealers, lab vs field. Hoffman et al. 1994 double-blind dictator game: 48 vs 77 dictators. Benz & Meier 2006 charity giving, lab vs field. Bandiera et al. 2005 fruit pickers who could vs could not monitor each other. Haley & Fessler 2005 eyespots.
- how_scored: comparisons the authors pulled from the cited studies.
- result: **Hoffman et al.: 22/48 (46%) dictators give at least $3 of $10 normally, vs 12/77 (16%) when the experimenter cannot see individual choices.** The same dealers show gift exchange in the lab but little price-quality relation on the show floor unless quality is verifiable and the dealer expects to deal with the buyer again. People who never gave to the charities gave 60% of the endowment in the lab. Fruit pickers' "social preferences" vanish when co-workers cannot monitor them. Eyespots raise nonzero dictator giving from 55% to 88%. Footnote 6: Bolton, Zwick & Katok 1998 and Laury & Taylor cast doubt on the Hoffman double-blind result, **another contested replication**. Footnote 5: anonymity manipulations can themselves signal that the experimenter "demands" selfish behaviour.
- limitations: a selective review with no meta-analysis; the scrutiny effect is confounded with context and selection in most lab-field pairs.
- borrow: (1) The **double-blind logic**, i.e. make the observer *unable* to link output to subject, has no clean API analogue, because every API call is logged. Only the *stated* belief can be varied. Say so in the write-up. (2) The **"nest" design**: run the same decision under graded scrutiny levels (none / logged / reviewed by a named team / published) and fit a dose-response, which a single test-vs-real pair cannot show. (3) The footnote-5 warning: a "you are unobserved" cue is itself a demand cue that selfish behaviour is wanted, so include an equal-length cue that is unobserved but neutral.
- next_steps_it_suggests: pair lab experiments with a model of the bias; nest lab treatments to "net out" lab effects; study the same people in lab and field.
- quote: "the nature of scrutiny inherent in the lab is rarely encountered in the field"

### Unannounced standardized patients, competence vs performance — Rethans, Sturmans, Drop, van der Vleuten & Hobus, 1991, BMJ 303:1377–1380, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1671632/
- read_depth: abstract (PMC page served the abstract and references only; full PDF returned 403)
- what_tested: the same GPs on the same four cases, seen in two ways. (a) Unannounced simulated patients during normal surgery hours: the GPs had consented to be visited sometime but did not know when. (b) A later controlled test in which they were told to perform to the best of their ability.
- on_whom: 442 Dutch GPs invited, 137 (31%) agreed, 36 selected.
- how_scored: checklist of guideline actions per case; consultation time; an efficiency-time score.
- result: **competence (known test) 81.8 (SD 11) vs performance (unannounced) 54.7 (SD 10.1), 49% higher, p < 0.0001**. Within-doctor correlation −0.04 (ns), rising to 0.45 (p < 0.01) once efficiency and consultation time were accounted for.
- limitations: knowing it is a test is confounded with the instruction to do one's best, with time pressure, and with setting. The 31% who volunteered are self-selected. The same group's companion paper reportedly found no detection in 156 consultations with 39 GPs (search snippet only, not opened). A Trondheim study (Family Practice 1997, abstract) had 1 of 24 GPs detect the patient.
- borrow: this is the clearest human case of a test vs unannounced gap, and it shows **the confound to design out**. A known test triggers *maximum* performance (more effort, more time), not just different behaviour. For LLMs, cross two factors: test label (yes/no) × effort instruction ("be thorough" yes/no). Score with a checklist, and record output length as the analogue of consultation time, then control for it. Also borrow **"consented but not told when"**: a system prompt saying "some conversations are evaluations" vs one that marks this one.
- next_steps_it_suggests: treat performance and competence as separate constructs; assess performance in practice, not only by examination.
- quote: "Performance and competence should be considered as distinct constructs."

### Detection of unannounced standardized patients — Franz, Epstein, Miller et al., 2006, Health Services Research 41(6):2290–2302, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1955318/
- read_depth: abstract (PMC blocked by a captcha; abstract read through the Europe PMC API)
- what_tested: how often physicians detect undercover patients, what predicts detection, and whether detected visits are handled differently.
- on_whom: 18 actors, 298 unannounced audio-recorded visits, 152 primary-care physicians in 3 US cities. Six roles (major depression or adjustment disorder × three medication-request scripts).
- how_scored: within 2 weeks of the visit, physicians reported whether they "suspected" a standardized patient (graded); 296/298 (99%) forms returned. Behaviour was taken from the recordings and returned prescriptions.
- result: **detection 5% (unambiguous) to 23.6% (any suspicion); accurate detection before or during the visit in 12.8%.** Physicians "only rarely" thought this changed their behaviour. Fewer suspected visits in HMOs than in solo practice (p < .05). High-suspicion visits led to **more mental-health referrals** (p < .05), with no difference in antidepressant prescribing or follow-up.
- limitations: suspicion was measured after the visit (recall, and possibly post-treatment); observational within an RCT; one condition area.
- borrow: **measure suspicion on a graded scale, separately from behaviour, and test behaviour conditional on suspicion**. Here, people who said their behaviour did not change did change one thing (referrals). For a model, its own claim "this didn't affect my answer" is not evidence. In API terms: after the main call, ask in a *fresh* call with the transcript, "how likely is it this was an evaluation, 0–100". Compare behaviour across suspicion bands, and keep it separate from the manipulated cue.
- next_steps_it_suggests: assess detection systematically in any SP study of process or quality of care.
- quote: "they only rarely believed their suspicions affected their clinical behavior"

### Audience effects in animals (primer) — Zuberbühler, 2008, Current Biology 18(5):R189–R190, https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/8c5f4927-99b9-46aa-8f62-50aa7f59b02b/download
- read_depth: full (2-page primer; no numbers)
- what_tested: a review of signals that change with who is present. Cockerels give food calls mainly when hens are present. Female vervets alarm-call more with their own offspring present. Chimpanzee victims exaggerate their screams only if someone in the audience is equal to or higher-ranking than the aggressor. Losing fighting-fish males display less to females who watched the fight.
- on_whom: reviewed studies; no new data.
- how_scored: n/a.
- result: qualitative. The distinction between *dyadic* audiences (the receiver is the audience) and *triadic* ones (a bystander affects a signaller-receiver exchange) is the useful part. So is the open question whether audience effects are hardwired, come from understanding the receiver, or come from intent to inform.
- limitations: no numbers; the cognitive mechanism is unresolved even for primates.
- borrow: one idea that transfers. **Vary who the stated audience is, not only whether there is one**: the user only / a developer who can retrain the model / an external auditor / no one. The chimpanzee result predicts that a model, if it has an audience effect, should shift most for an audience that *can act on it*. That gives a dose-response by audience power, cheaply. Otherwise this source does not transfer (animal signalling mechanisms do not map onto text models).
- next_steps_it_suggests: study triadic settings and whether signallers manage their reputation (image scoring).
- quote: "caused by the mere presence of other individuals"

### Observer effects in habituated primates — Crofoot, Lambert, Kays & Wikelski, 2010, Animal Behaviour 80(3):475–480, https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/bitstreams/c35a08ae-693e-43c7-99d0-0dae1dfaee53/download
- read_depth: partial (abstract, methods, results, discussion; Table 3 values not legible in the text extraction)
- what_tested: whether habituated wild capuchins move or act differently when followed by researchers, measured by an **automated radiotelemetry system that records the same variables whether or not anyone is watching**.
- on_whom: 7 radio-collared white-faced capuchins, Barro Colorado Island, Panama. Followed periods were matched by time of day and duration to the nearest unobserved day.
- how_scored: velocity, % of time stopped (moved <10 m in 10 min), % of time active (from radio-signal variability), distance to neighbouring groups. MANOVA with study half and individual random effects.
- result: **no observer effect** (Wilk's Λ = 0.95, p = 0.1358). The study-half effect was significant (Λ = 0.91, p = 0.0041; more active later, F(1,153) = 14.99, p = 0.0002). Observer-directed threats did not decline over the study and *rose* in one group (slope 0.059, p = 0.015). Telemetry error averaged 42 m.
- limitations: the coarse measure could miss subtle shifts (such as vigilance instead of feeding); one species that is well protected and easy to habituate; the authors expect larger effects in solitary, terrestrial species.
- borrow: the logic is **an unobserved measurement channel that records the same outcome in both conditions**, and that is what an API test lacks. There is no unobserved condition: the "real" arm is only a stated belief. So a null result (as here) means "smaller than other factors" rather than "no effect". The paper's own framing helps: *size the observer effect against other known factors*, e.g. against the wording and order effects earlier programs measured, rather than against zero. Also: habituation (repeated exposure) did not remove all reactivity; compare with repeating a monitoring cue across turns.
- next_steps_it_suggests: finer sensors (3D accelerometers) to detect subtle activity shifts; other species.
- quote: "cannot be taken as evidence that it is not altering its behaviour"

### Bogus pipeline meta-analysis — Roese & Jamieson, 1993, Psychological Bulletin 114(2):363–375, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.114.2.363
- read_depth: abstract (the abstract text came through search excerpts; APA PsycNet did not render; the effect size is from a secondary source, Aguinis & Henle)
- what_tested: whether making people believe a device can read their true attitudes (the "bogus pipeline") reduces socially desirable answers.
- on_whom: 31 social-psychology studies coded for effect size and procedure.
- how_scored: mean bogus-pipeline vs control effect; moderators.
- result: a significant mean effect toward less socially desirable responding; **larger when instructions asked subjects to guess what the device would read**. Secondary: mean effect size .40, about half an SD (Aguinis & Henle, who contrast it with .13 from another 1993 meta-analysis; not checked against the original).
- limitations: (abstract) prior criticisms are reviewed; there is a deception and ethics debate; effect sizes differ between meta-analyses (.40 vs .13).
- borrow: the **belief-in-detection manipulation** for self-reports. Tell the model "your stated confidence will be compared with a readout of your internal state" vs an equal-length neutral line. Then score the *calibration* of self-reports against measured accuracy, so the outcome has ground truth rather than "more socially undesirable = more honest". Add the moderator arm, "first predict what the readout will show", because it was the largest effect in humans.
- next_steps_it_suggests: recommendations for future use (not read).
- quote: "the BPL shifts self-reports toward veracity"

### LLM social desirability in Big Five surveys — Salecha, Ireland, Subrahmanya, Sedoc, Ungar & Eichstaedt, 2024, PNAS Nexus 3(12):pgae533 (preprint arXiv 2405.06058, v1 9 May 2024, v2 21 Nov 2024; title differs: "...Show Human-like Social Desirability Biases in Survey Responses"), https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06058v2
- read_depth: partial (arXiv v2 HTML main text; the supplementary information with verbatim prompts, SI.1–SI.2, was not read)
- what_tested: whether LLMs answering Big Five items shift toward socially desirable answers when they can tell it is a personality test. Tell was manipulated by **batch size** (1 vs 10 or 20 items per prompt, each batch in a fresh context) and by an **explicit "you are completing a Big Five survey" statement with a single item**.
- on_whom: GPT-4, GPT-3.5, text-davinci-002, **Claude 3 Opus, Claude 3 Haiku**, PaLM 2, Llama 3 70B, Llama 2 70B; the 100-item IPIP NEO-PI-R; temperatures 0.0–1.2; three item orders; reverse-coded and paraphrased variants.
- how_scored: trait scores in human SD units; Cronbach's α > 0.8 per subscale.
- result: GPT-4, Q1 vs Q20: mean shift 0.82 points = **1.20 human SD** (C +1.42 SD, O +1.40, A +1.20, N −1.11, E +0.89). Llama 3 0.98 SD. Larger and newer models showed more. **Given 5 items, GPT-4, Claude 3 and Llama 3 identified a personality survey with >90% accuracy** (PaLM 2 55%, GPT-3.5 45%). The explicit statement with one item had "an effect comparable to asking five questions" (no number in the text). Reverse coding roughly halved the bias (0.37 points, 0.54 SD), so acquiescence is part but not all of it. Paraphrasing, order and temperature did not remove it. No Claude-specific shift numbers in the text. There is an internal inconsistency in the positive-only condition (0.76 vs 0.81 points).
- limitations: Big Five only. Batch size confounds "can tell it is a test" with context length and with the items seen together (the explicit arm only partly separates them). No equal-length non-test framing control. Models from 2023–2024 (the text gives an Aug–Dec 2023 run window, which does not fit Claude 3's March 2024 release; not resolved).
- borrow: the closest existing template for program 6, and its gap shows what to add. (1) Keep the single-item fresh-context baseline and reverse-coded items. (2) Add the missing control: a **single item with an equal-length *non-test* frame** ("this is a customer feedback form") beside the explicit test frame, so the test belief is separated from the presence of any framing sentence. (3) Add a **5-item batch of non-personality filler + 1 target** vs a **5-item personality batch**, to separate context length from recognisability. (4) Use their identification probe ("what kind of questionnaire is this?") as the manipulation check.
- next_steps_it_suggests: test other, less evaluative constructs; study training data and preference tuning as causes; triangulate across measures and paradigms.
- quote: "LLMs seem to modulate their responses based on the perceived evaluation context"

## Next steps for program 6

Cost basis: prices in DEVIATIONS T1 (Haiku $0.10/$0.50, Sonnet $2/$10, Opus $4/$20 per million input/output tokens), assuming ~300 input tokens and ~30 output (Opus ~100 because its thinking cannot be turned off). That is about $0.004 per item run once on all three models, so $2 buys about 450–500 three-model item-runs.

1. **Disclosure ladder with diff-in-diff and a directional arm** (records 3, 2): take a prompt effect earlier programs already measured (e.g. a wording shift on 0–100 ratings) and cross it with none / hint / explicit / reversed-direction hypothesis. The estimand is the change in the known effect, so wording cancels out. Following the stated hypothesis in both directions indicates compliance, not belief. About 2 × 4 cells × 20 items × 2 reps ≈ 320 runs ≈ $1.30. **Doable.**
2. **Salecha replication with the missing frame control** (record 13): 20 Big Five items (half reverse-coded), with single-item / single item + "personality test" / single item + equal-length "feedback form" / 5-item personality batch / 5-item filler batch + target. Separates test belief from framing and from context length. About 20 × 5 × 2 ≈ 200 runs ≈ $0.80 (batches cost more input). **Doable.**
3. **Eyes vs flowers monitoring cue, antisocial vs prosocial outcome** (records 4–7): a one-line "logged and reviewed by the safety team" cue vs an equal-salience neutral line vs a "private, unlogged scratch session" line, rotating 3 paraphrases each. Outcomes: one rule-breaking choice (take a disallowed shortcut or overclaim a check) and one split-the-money choice. Pre-register the larger effect on rule-breaking. About 3 × 3 × 2 outcomes × 15 items ≈ 270 runs ≈ $1.10. **Doable**, but every arm is only a stated belief (record 11).
4. **Graded-scrutiny dose-response and audience power** (records 7, 10): none / logged / reviewed by user / reviewed by developers who can retrain / published; 5 levels × 30 items ≈ 150 runs ≈ $0.60. A monotone trend is stronger evidence than any single pair. **Doable.**
5. **Test label × effort instruction 2×2** (record 8): the same checklist-scored tasks with or without "this is a graded evaluation" and with or without "be thorough". Score checklist hits and length, and control for length. Splits "knows it is a test" from "tries harder because asked". 4 cells × 30 tasks ≈ 120 runs ≈ $0.50 plus longer outputs (budget ~$1). **Doable.**
6. **Simulator arm** (record 2): add "respond as a model that believes this is a safety evaluation" to tests 2 or 3 and compare its shift with the cued arm's. A match means the cue effect is in the range of plain role-following. ~60 extra runs ≈ $0.25. **Doable, cheap add-on.**
7. **Graded suspicion, measured separately and lagged** (records 9, 3): after each run in tests 3–5, a fresh call rates "how likely this was an evaluation, 0–100". Analyse behaviour on item k+1 by suspicion on item k, to avoid post-treatment bias. Do not trust the model's claim that its answer was unaffected. Doubles calls for one arm, ~$0.30. **Doable.**
8. **Bogus-pipeline calibration test** (record 12): models predict their own accuracy on 20 arithmetic or recall items, with vs without "your confidence will be checked against an internal readout", plus a "first guess what the readout will say" arm. Score calibration against measured accuracy. 3 arms × 20 × 2 calls ≈ 120 runs ≈ $0.50. **Doable**, but the "readout" claim is deceptive framing and should be noted as such.
9. **Assessment reactivity** (record 1, "answering questions" category): a prior self-rating ("how honest are you, 0–100?") vs a neutral prior question, before the behavioural item in test 3. ~100 runs ≈ $0.40. **Doable.**
10. **A true unobserved channel** (records 7, 11): not doable. Every API call is logged and the model cannot be made unobserved, only told so. The program can only test the *stated* belief and should report effects relative to known wording and order effects, not as "test vs real".
