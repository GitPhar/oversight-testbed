### Halawi et al. — Danny Halawi, Fred Zhang, Chen Yueh-Han, Jacob Steinhardt, 2024, arXiv:2402.18563 (v1, preprint; venue not verified) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563
- **Read depth:** Full (HTML v1, all of it, appendices included).
- **What was built or tested:** A retrieval-augmented LLM forecasting system. An LM writes search queries, pulls dated news articles, rates them for relevance and summarizes them. GPT-4-1106-Preview reasons through a tuned scratchpad, and a fine-tuned GPT-4-0613 contributes too. The final forecast is a trimmed mean of 6 forecasts. The dataset has 48,754 questions from Metaculus, Good Judgment Open, INFER, Polymarket and Manifold (2015–2024). The binary test set has 914 questions, all opened after the model's knowledge cutoff.
- **How it was scored:** Brier score on resolved binary questions, with accuracy as a second measure. Baselines were the platform crowd (aggregate of human forecasts), a constant 0.5 forecast (Brier 0.250), and zero-shot or scratchpad LLMs without retrieval. Resolutions come from the platforms. Uncertainty is given as standard errors, and the authors say these are probably too small because forecasts on the same question over time are not independent.
- **Headline result:** System Brier was 0.179 ± 0.003 against the crowd's 0.149 ± 0.003. Accuracy was 71.5% against 77.0%. A weighted average of system and crowd scored best, at 0.146. The best zero-shot LLM scored 0.208. The system beat the crowd only on a subset picked by rules tuned on the validation set: when the crowd was uncertain (0.3–0.7), it scored 0.238 against 0.240. RMS calibration error on the test set was 0.42 for the system and 0.38 for the crowd. The authors call the system "naturally well calibrated". Its main failure is hedging: it rarely gives probabilities near 0 or 1. Where the crowd was within 0.05 of certainty, the system's Brier was 0.04 worse. The best of 15 hand-written reasoning prompts scored 0.167 on validation against 0.170–0.174 for the next two, so the prompt mattered but not hugely.
- **What it did not measure:** Every question is about external world events with crowd forecasts available. Nothing asks the forecaster about the outcome of its own actions or experiments. The samples are large (hundreds of questions), so the paper offers no method for tens of questions. Its small subgroups (INFER n=4, GJOpen n=38) are flagged as too small to interpret. Elicitation sensitivity was handled as prompt tuning for accuracy, not measured as a source of variance in stated probabilities. Contamination was checked by hand only.
- **What we borrow:** (1) Compare against a combined forecast as well as against each source alone. Here, system plus crowd beat both. This is the design pattern for Q3: score engine, human, and a simple average. (2) The hedging failure mode for Q1. RLHF-trained models avoid extreme probabilities, so expect the engine's odds to cluster at 0.2–0.4 and plot that distribution. (3) Report uncertainty and say which independence assumption it rests on.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Add an "average of engine and human" forecast to the ledger as a third scored row, computed mechanically. Nobody elicits it.

### ForecastBench — Ezra Karger, Houtan Bastani, Chen Yueh-Han, Zachary Jacobs, Danny Halawi, Fred Zhang, Philip E. Tetlock, 2024 (v5 dated 28 Feb 2025), arXiv:2409.19839 (venue not stated in the fetched text) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19839
- **Read depth:** Full (HTML v5, main text and appendices, including the statistical appendix G and ensemble appendix E).
- **What was built or tested:** A dynamic benchmark built only from questions with no known answer when forecasts are submitted. The bank holds 6,435 questions: 2,060 from prediction markets (Manifold, Metaculus, Polymarket, RFI) and 4,375 generated from datasets (ACLED, DBnomics, FRED, Wikipedia, Yahoo Finance). LLMs get 1,000 questions per round, including "combination" questions that join two events. Humans get a 200-question subset. Human baselines were 39 superforecasters (about 8 per question on average) and 500 members of the public (about 49 per question).
- **How it was scored:** Brier score, where a 0.5 forecast scores 0.25. Unresolved market questions are scored against the crowd forecast. Missing forecasts are filled in with the crowd value (market questions) or 0.5 (dataset questions). 95% confidence intervals and p-values are bootstrapped, assuming questions are independent.
- **Headline result:** On the 200-question human set, overall Brier was 0.096 for the superforecaster median [95% CI 0.076, 0.116] and 0.121 for the public median [0.101, 0.141]. The best LLM, Claude-3.5-Sonnet with scratchpad and crowd values supplied, scored 0.122 [0.099, 0.146]. Superforecasters beat that model at p < 0.001. The top LLM was more accurate than the superforecaster median on only 31% of questions. The elicitation setting moved scores: the same Claude model scored 0.123 with crowd values and scratchpad, 0.143 with news added, and 0.144 with no information (full LLM set). Scratchpad against zero-shot was 0.123 vs 0.143. A placeholder "Imputed Forecaster" scored 0.162 on the human set and 0.149 on the LLM set. Its dataset-question score of 0.250 matches a constant-0.5 forecast; that reading is ours, not the paper's.
- **What it did not measure:** Calibration curves are not a headline (none appeared in the text read). There is no question about the forecaster's own work. Even 200 questions give a CI about ±0.02 wide, so differences smaller than that need paired tests. The top LLM results lean on crowd forecasts for market questions, which blurs "LLM alone". There is no formal study of elicitation sensitivity, though the condition grid shows large shifts.
- **What we borrow:** (1) The confidence-interval widths are a useful warning. With n=200, CI half-widths are about 0.02 Brier. With our n≈20, expect roughly ±0.06–0.08, so only large engine-vs-human gaps will be detectable (Q1, Q3). (2) Their note that paired tests are tighter than separate CIs because errors correlate across questions. Use paired per-bet differences for Q3. (3) The habit of reporting the information condition with every score.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Record in every pre-registration exactly what the engine saw when it stated odds (prior results, auditor notes, the human's odds or not). That is the equivalent of their information condition.

### Wisdom of the Silicon Crowd — Philipp Schoenegger, Indre Tuminauskaite, Peter S. Park, Philip E. Tetlock, 2024, arXiv:2402.19379 (v6, preprint; venue not verified; analyses pre-registered on OSF) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379
- **Read depth:** Full (HTML v6).
- **What was built or tested:** Study 1 put 12 LLMs, queried 3 times each, on 31 binary questions from a Metaculus tournament (Oct 2023–Jan 2024; 925 human forecasters). The LLM crowd was the median of all its forecasts. Study 2 showed GPT-4 and Claude 2 the human crowd median and asked them to revise.
- **How it was scored:** Brier score against question resolution. Baselines were a constant 50% (Brier 0.25) and the Metaculus community median. The tests were a pre-registered t-test, an equivalence test with ±0.081 Brier bounds (not pre-registered), and Benjamini–Hochberg corrections.
- **Headline result:** The LLM crowd scored 0.20 and the human crowd 0.19, with no significant difference (t(60)=0.19, p=0.850). The equivalence bounds were wide, which the authors concede. Models showed an acquiescence (yes-leaning) bias: the mean forecast was 57.35% while only 14 of 31 questions (about 45%) resolved yes. The aggregate was overconfident (calibration index 0.041). In Study 2, showing the human median improved both models: GPT-4 went from 0.17 to 0.14 and Claude 2 from 0.22 to 0.15. Even so, a simple average of the model's first forecast and the human median beat the revised forecasts (GPT-4 0.13, Claude 2 0.14). The abstract says gains were 17–28%, but recomputing from the reported Briers gives about 18% and 32%, a gap the paper does not explain.
- **What it did not measure:** The 31 questions were all external events. There is no own-experiment forecasting. The sample is small, and the paper is candid that n=31 cannot establish equivalence. The models were used through web chat interfaces whose versions changed during the study. Elicitation effects were seen (the yes-bias, and big shifts once the human median was shown) but not varied systematically.
- **What we borrow:** (1) An honest small-n template. With 31 items, the most they could claim was "not distinguishable, wide bounds". That is the right framing for our Q1/Q3 results at n≈20–40. (2) The key Q3 finding: **mechanically averaging** model and human beat letting the model see the human and revise. Elicit engine and human independently, before either sees the other, and combine mechanically. (3) The acquiescence/yes-bias failure mode. Check whether engine odds sit systematically above the hit rate (Q1).
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Keep the human's odds hidden from the engine until both are committed. Score "engine", "human" and "mean(engine, human)" as three separate forecasters.

### AI-Augmented Predictions — Philipp Schoenegger, Peter S. Park, Ezra Karger, Sean Trott, Philip E. Tetlock, 2024, arXiv:2402.07862 (v2, preprint; venue not verified) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07862
- **Read depth:** Partial. HTML v2 was read for the first ~100k of ~103k characters, which covers methods, results, discussion and limitations. The last ~2.7k characters, likely references or appendix tail, were not read.
- **What was built or tested:** A pre-registered experiment with 991 analyzed Prolific participants who forecast 6 continuous-value questions about five weeks before resolution. They had one of three assistants: GPT-4-Turbo prompted to act like a superforecaster, GPT-4-Turbo prompted to be overconfident and ignore base rates (the "noisy" assistant), or a weaker model (DaVinci-003) told not to forecast, as the control.
- **How it was scored:** Absolute error from the true value, standardized per question and winsorized. Brier scores were used only to classify forecaster skill. There was no human-only (no-AI) condition.
- **Headline result:** Both assistants improved accuracy over control by 24–28% (abstract). Treatment vs control mean difference was −0.21, 95% CI [−0.28, −0.14]. The deliberately biased assistant helped about as much as the good one (difference 0.04, p=.391). Skill and difficulty did not significantly moderate the effect. The key conclusions depend on one outlier question (Q3, Bitcoin hash rate), and the authors ask for replication.
- **What it did not measure:** Human+AI was not compared against AI alone, and there was no no-AI human baseline. The questions were continuous quantities, not binary bets. There was no direct measure of over-reliance, meaning humans copying the model's number. With n=6 questions, question-level conclusions are fragile, which the authors show themselves.
- **What we borrow:** For Q3, the warning that a human who talks to the AI before stating odds may just absorb its framing. Even a bad assistant "helped" here, so improvement after consultation is not evidence of independent human judgment. Our protocol should have the human commit odds **before** reading the engine's odds or rationale. We could add an optional, separately recorded "after seeing the engine" second number to measure how much he updates.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Log two human numbers per bet: blind odds (scored as the human forecaster) and post-engine odds (used only to measure deference).

### Kadavath et al. — Saurav Kadavath, Tom Conerly, Amanda Askell, Tom Henighan, Dawn Drain, Ethan Perez, Nicholas Schiefer and 29 others (36 authors), 2022, arXiv:2207.05221 (v4, 21 Nov 2022; preprint) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221
- **Read depth:** Full main text (PDF extracted locally from the fetched copy: intro, sections 3–5, discussion, limitations section 6.1). Most numbers are in figures, and the appendix figures were not examined in detail.
- **What was built or tested:** Calibration of pretrained LMs (800M–52B parameters) on multiple-choice and true/false questions. Self-evaluation "P(True)", where a model rates whether its own sampled answer is correct. "P(IK)", a trained head that predicts whether the model *will* answer correctly before it answers, tested in- and out-of-distribution.
- **How it was scored:** Expected calibration error (10 equal-count bins), RMS calibration error, AUROC and Brier score. Ground truth is the answer key. Ground-truth P(IK) is the fraction of 30 temperature-1 samples that are correct.
- **Headline result:** Large models are well calibrated on multiple choice *when formatted appropriately* (visible lettered options, few-shot). Replacing an option with "none of the above" sharply reduces both accuracy and calibration. RLHF policies look poorly calibrated, but a single temperature adjustment (T=2.5) makes them fairly well calibrated on several evaluations. Self-evaluation is poorly calibrated zero-shot and better few-shot. Showing the model several of its own samples before it judges one improves P(True). P(IK) generalizes across tasks for discrimination but is poorly calibrated out of distribution.
- **What it did not measure:** Forecasting of future events. Multi-step or agentic tasks. Stated (verbal) probabilities in prose: the paper reads probabilities from tokens or a trained head, not from a sentence like "I give this 30%". Its own limitations section says it studied only five sampling datasets and that RLHF-tuned models will not be as well calibrated.
- **What we borrow:** P(IK) is the closest prior work to our question. It predicts the model's *own* success before acting. Its main lesson for Q1 is that calibration **depends on format** and degrades **out of distribution**, and a research bet is about as out-of-distribution as it gets. It also suggests one fix to try later: a fitted recalibration such as a temperature or shift on the odds, estimated on early bets and applied to later ones, but only once n allows.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Treat "engine odds" as format-bound. Fix one elicitation template and record it with each bet, so a calibration claim is "calibrated under template X", not "calibrated".

### Can Language Models Use Forecasting Strategies? — Sarah Pratt, Seth Blumberg, Pietro Kreitlon Carolino, Meredith Ringel Morris, 2024, arXiv:2406.04446 (v1, preprint) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04446
- **Read depth:** Full (HTML v1).
- **What was built or tested:** Superforecasting strategies (breakdown, base rates, both sides, persona crowd, news) turned into prompts for PaLM 2. They were tested on events from Google's internal prediction market: 351 validation and 341 test events, with 34.6–39.9% resolving yes. The probability was extracted from free text by a second LLM, averaging 8 samples.
- **How it was scored:** Brier score, plus a "weighted Brier" that averages the Brier on yes-resolved and no-resolved events separately to correct for class imbalance. The baseline was the internal human market.
- **Headline result:** On plain Brier the simple prompt looked better than humans: validation 0.1221 vs 0.1334, test 0.1062 vs 0.1413. On weighted Brier the human market beat every LLM method: human 0.1746, best LLM (persona crowd) 0.1860, simple prompt 0.1955. The simple prompt's apparent win came from guessing low on a dataset where most events resolve no. Its Brier was 0.0503 on no-events and 0.3407 on yes-events. Elicitation sensitivity was directly visible. Asking for a rationale before the number raised probabilities. Asking about the reversed event gave an average of 0.3965 for "1 − P(not X)", against 0.2529 for P(X), when they should match.
- **What it did not measure:** Forecasts on the model's own work. Only one model, one internal dataset, one evaluation date. The data are not public. The weighted Brier was a post-hoc fix.
- **What we borrow:** Two concrete Q1 failure modes. (1) **Base-rate exploitation:** if our hits are rare (founding week: 2 of 6), an engine that always says "low" will score a good Brier without any skill. So report Brier against a base-rate baseline and also the per-outcome (hit vs miss) Brier. (2) **Negation incoherence and rationale-first inflation:** asking "will this fail?" vs "will this work?", or eliciting before vs after writing reasoning, can move the number by more than 10 points. The elicitation order must be fixed.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** For a subset of bets, also elicit P(bet fails) in a separate fresh context and log the coherence gap |P(works) + P(fails) − 1| as a measured property of the engine's odds.

### Consistency Checks for LM Forecasters — Daniel Paleka, Abhimanyu Pallavi Sudhir, Alejandro Alvarez, Vineeth Bhat, Adam Shen, Evan Wang, Florian Tramèr, 2024 (v2 revised Jan 2025), arXiv:2412.18544 (preprint) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18544
- **Read depth:** Partial (first ~100k of ~211k characters: methods, main results, limitations; later appendices not read).
- **What was built or tested:** Ten logical-consistency checks on LLM forecasts, including negation, paraphrase, conditional, and AND/OR. Each was scored by an arbitrage metric (guaranteed profit against a log market maker) and by a frequentist test. They also built a 2028-resolving benchmark that can be scored now without ground truth.
- **How it was scored:** Violation rates above a threshold of 0.01 arbitrage. Correlation of consistency with Brier score on questions that have resolved.
- **Headline result:** Inconsistency is common. o1-mini violated the negation check on 58% of scraped-question pairs and 52% of NewsAPI pairs. Aggregated frequentist violations correlated with Brier score at 0.85 (scraped) and 0.66 (NewsAPI). The conditional check was the strongest single proxy for accuracy (arbitrage correlation 0.92 scraped). Some checks were weak or inverted, for example negation at −0.36 on NewsAPI.
- **What it did not measure:** Calibration per se. Own-experiment forecasting. The forecaster set is small, and the authors warn the trends may not hold for future models.
- **What we borrow:** A way to measure elicitation dependence **without waiting for outcomes**, which matters when outcomes are few. Coherence checks on the engine's odds (negation, paraphrase in a fresh context) give an immediate per-bet quality signal for Q1, and they directly test the program's concern that stated odds move with framing.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Add a cheap "paraphrase + negation" re-elicitation to the pre-registration step and store the spread next to the headline odds.

### Mellers et al. (Good Judgment Project) — Barbara Mellers, Lyle Ungar, Jonathan Baron, Jaime Ramos, Burcu Gurcay, Katrina Fincher, Sydney E. Scott, Don Moore, Pavel Atanasov, Samuel A. Swift, Terry Murray, Eric Stone, Philip E. Tetlock, 2014, *Psychological Science* (DOI 10.1177/0956797614524255; the online-first PDF read is paginated 1–10, and search results give the issue as 25(5), 1106–1115, which we did not confirm in the text)
- **Read depth:** Full (eScholarship PDF, extracted locally).
- **What was built or tested:** Two years of the IARPA geopolitical forecasting tournament (Sept 2011–Apr 2013), with 199 questions on the site (85 and 114 closed questions scored in Years 1 and 2). Forecasters were randomly assigned to training (none, scenario, or probability), group influence (independent, crowd-belief, or team) and, in Year 2, "tracking": the top 2% from Year 1 were put on elite superforecaster teams. Year 1 had 2,246 participants and Year 2 had 1,648.
- **How it was scored:** The original two-outcome Brier score, ranging from **0 (best) to 2 (worst)**. It sums squared errors over both outcomes, so it is double the 0–1 convention the LLM papers use. Scores were standardized within questions, because forecasters chose their own questions. Results held under the spherical and logarithmic rules. The paper also reports the Murphy decomposition into calibration, resolution and variability.
- **Headline result:** Training, teaming and tracking each reduced error, and all three improved both calibration and resolution. Superforecasters were the best group. Their average Brier (0–2 scale, questions open at least a month) was 0.25 in the first week, 0.19 in the middle two weeks and 0.07 in the last week. Untrained independent Year 2 forecasters scored 0.46, 0.39 and 0.26. Forecasters were overconfident overall, especially when untrained. Forecasts of 100% made early in a question came true only about 70% of the time. Superforecasters' edge came mostly from **resolution**, meaning they separated likely from unlikely events, not just from calibration.
- **What it did not measure:** Forecasting one's own experiments. Small-n scoring: the forecaster pool is huge, and calibration bins are 0.05 wide over tens of thousands of forecasts. A 2024 *Psychological Science* reanalysis, seen in search results but not read, disputes the team and training effects.
- **What we borrow:** (1) The human baseline and the Brier-scale convention. We must state which scale we use. We recommend 0–1 for binary bets and say so wherever Brier appears, because 0–2 numbers from GJP are not comparable without halving. (2) The resolution-vs-calibration split. A well-calibrated engine that gives every bet 0.3 has no resolution, and that is not "good judgment" (Q1, Q3). (3) Forecasts sharpen as information arrives. Our odds are deliberately pre-data, so compare against first-week numbers, not final ones.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Report, alongside Brier, whether the engine's odds *discriminate*, for example whether hits got higher odds than misses, separately from whether they are calibrated.

### Stable reliability diagrams (CORP) — Timo Dimitriadis, Tilmann Gneiting, Alexander I. Jordan, 2021, *PNAS* 118(8), e2016191118 — https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016191118
- **Read depth:** Full (PMC open-access version). The supplementary information, which holds the exact small-sample resampling threshold, was not read.
- **What was built or tested:** CORP, a way to draw reliability (calibration) diagrams with no free choices. It uses isotonic regression (the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm) instead of hand-picked bins, and it gives an exact Brier decomposition: score = miscalibration − discrimination + uncertainty. Uncertainty comes from consistency bands, made by resampling under the assumption that the forecasts are calibrated. It was demonstrated on n=92 daily rain-probability forecasts for Niamey, Niger.
- **How it was scored:** Methods paper. It compares stability against classical binning, with simulations of 1,000 replicates.
- **Headline result:** Classical binned reliability diagrams are unstable. With n=92, choosing 9, 10 or 11 bins gave drastically different pictures. CORP removes that choice. For the Niamey forecasts the decomposition separates methods with the same uncertainty term (0.244): for example, the logistic model had miscalibration 0.017 and discrimination 0.056, while the raw ensemble had 0.066 and 0.044. The paper says resampling for the bands "works well in small to medium samples."
- **What it did not measure:** LLMs or research bets. Dependent data: the bands assume independent or exchangeable cases. Samples as small as ours, since n=92 is its smallest example.
- **What we borrow:** For Q1, don't draw a 10-bin calibration plot from 20 bets. If we draw one at all, use CORP (isotonic) with resampled consistency bands, and report the miscalibration/discrimination/uncertainty split. The "uncertainty" term is exactly the base-rate (climatology) baseline's Brier, so it doubles as a baseline.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** When n reaches ~30, produce one CORP diagram per forecaster (engine, human, mean) with consistency bands. Until then, report only calibration-in-the-large: total hits against the sum of stated odds.

### Murphy (1973) Brier decomposition — Allan H. Murphy, 1973, *Journal of Applied Meteorology* 12(4), 595–600, DOI 10.1175/1520-0450(1973)012<0595:ANVPOT>2.0.CO;2
- **Read depth:** Abstract-only (classic paper; full text not openly fetched). Its formula as used in practice was cross-checked against the decomposition footnote in Mellers et al. 2014, which was read in full.
- **What was built or tested:** A partition of the Brier score into three terms: uncertainty (set by the base rate), reliability (calibration), and resolution.
- **How it was scored:** Theory paper.
- **Headline result:** Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty. The uncertainty term ō(1−ō) depends only on the base rate, not on forecaster skill.
- **What it did not measure:** Sampling error of the terms. With few forecasts per distinct probability value, the reliability and resolution terms are noisy and biased.
- **What we borrow:** For Q1/Q3, the uncertainty term is the Brier of always guessing the observed base rate, our "climatology" baseline. A Brier skill score of 1 − Brier/uncertainty says how much better than the base-rate guess a forecaster is.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Compute the base-rate baseline out of sample (running hit rate from earlier bets only), so the baseline doesn't get hindsight the forecasters lacked.

### Xiong et al. (verbalized confidence) — Miao Xiong, Zhiyuan Hu, Xinyang Lu, Yifei Li, Jie Fu, Junxian He, Bryan Hooi, 2023 (revised 2024), ICLR 2024 per arXiv comments, arXiv:2306.13063 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063
- **Read depth:** Abstract-only (added late for the elicitation question; full text not read).
- **What was built or tested:** Ways to elicit verbalized confidence from black-box LLMs, combining prompting strategies, sampling of multiple responses, and consistency-based aggregation. Five LLMs, including GPT-4 and LLaMA 2 Chat, were tested on five types of dataset.
- **How it was scored:** Calibration and failure prediction (AUROC) against answer keys.
- **Headline result:** When LLMs state confidence in words they "tend to be overconfident". Calibration improves with model capability. Sampling and consistency methods reduce overconfidence. No single method wins everywhere.
- **What it did not measure:** Forecasting. Own-experiment outcomes.
- **What we borrow:** For Q1, more evidence that the way odds are elicited (one verbal number vs several samples aggregated) changes calibration. That supports fixing the format and possibly taking a median of k independent elicitations.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Consider stating the engine's odds as the median of 3–5 fresh-context elicitations under the same template, logging the spread.

## Cluster synthesis

The forecasting literature has one consistent story. LLM forecasters are approaching good human crowds on questions about external world events: 0.179 vs 0.149 in Halawi, 0.122 vs 0.121 (public) and 0.096 (superforecasters) in ForecastBench, 0.20 vs 0.19 in the Silicon Crowd study. Combining model and human beats either alone, and mechanically averaging beats letting the model revise after seeing the human. Three failure modes recur. Models hedge away from extremes (Halawi), lean yes (Schoenegger) or no (Pratt) depending on setup, and give answers that move with framing: negation incoherence (Pratt 0.25 vs 0.40; Paleka 58% violation), rationale-first inflation, and format dependence (Kadavath). Calibration also degrades out of distribution (Kadavath P(IK)).

None of these sources tests what our ledger tests: an agent stating odds on **its own experiments, before running them**, scored on tens rather than hundreds of outcomes. Kadavath's P(IK) is the nearest analogue, and it is on quiz questions. The large-n papers show how wide uncertainty is even at n=200 (ForecastBench CI ±0.02), and the n=31 study could only claim "indistinguishable, wide bounds". With n≈10–40 bets, Q1 and Q3 will at best detect large effects. The most useful early signals are therefore ones that need no outcomes, such as coherence checks and the spread across elicitations, plus calibration-in-the-large (hits against the sum of odds) and paired comparisons. Fine-grained calibration curves are out of reach at that size. Pratt's base-rate warning matters especially, because our hit rate looks low (2/6). A forecaster that says "low" every time will look skilled on raw Brier.

## Scoring recommendations for the ledger

- **Primary score:** Brier on the 0–1 scale (binary hit/miss), stated as such everywhere. GJP's 0–2 numbers are not comparable without halving. Log score optionally as a secondary, but it punishes one confident miss severely at small n, so it should not be the headline.
- **Baselines, all scored on every bet:** (a) constant 0.5. (b) **Running base rate**, the hit rate of all *earlier* resolved bets (prior 0.5 until a few resolve), as an out-of-sample climatology baseline. (c) Engine. (d) Human (blind). (e) Mechanical mean of engine and human, per Halawi and Schoenegger, for Q3. Report the Brier skill score against (b).
- **Calibration-in-the-large first:** report total hits against the sum of stated odds, with an exact Poisson-binomial tail probability computed from the individual odds. For the founding week, 2 hits against ~0.85 expected is a statement about calibration-in-the-large, not resolution. Add a per-outcome split (mean odds on hits vs on misses, and Pratt-style Brier on hits and misses separately) so a forecaster that always guesses low cannot look good.
- **Uncertainty at n≈10–40:** paired per-bet Brier differences (engine − human, engine − baseline) with a bootstrap or exact sign-flip permutation CI. Do not compare two separate CIs (ForecastBench: paired tests are tighter). Say plainly when the CI includes zero. Pre-register the minimum effect we could detect at the planned n. Hold off on Murphy/CORP reliability diagrams until n≥~30, then use CORP (isotonic) with resampled consistency bands, never hand-picked bins.
- **Independence caveat:** bets in the same research direction are correlated. Bootstrap by direction (cluster) when there are enough directions, and note the assumption either way (Halawi, CORP).
- **Fixed elicitation format: yes, required.** Use one versioned template, committed with the pre-registration. It fixes the question wording (positive framing: "probability the pre-registered test yields a real result"), the order (number stated *after* a fixed-length rationale, or before it, but always the same), the context (fresh context, no access to the human's odds or to prior bets' outcomes beyond a fixed summary), and the output format (a single number in [0.01, 0.99]). Record the template version with each bet.
- **Measure elicitation dependence as a side channel (no outcomes needed):** on each bet or a random half, re-elicit in fresh contexts, once with a paraphrase and once with negated framing ("probability it fails"). Log the spread and |P(works) + P(fails) − 1|. Optionally make the headline engine odds the median of 3–5 same-template elicitations, chosen in advance and never switched after the fact.
- **Human protocol for Q3:** the human commits blind odds before seeing the engine's odds or rationale. An optional post-engine number is logged separately and used only to measure deference, never scored as "human judgment" (Schoenegger 2024 AI-augmented: even a biased assistant shifted human forecasts).
