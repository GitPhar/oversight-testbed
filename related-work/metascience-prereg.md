# Related work — pre-registration, Registered Reports and the metascience of research taste

Scanned 2026-11-04 by a scout session; read depths marked per entry.

Scope: (a) pre-registration and Registered Reports (RRs); (b) metascience on predicting which results are real, from prediction markets and surveys through text-based machine learning to LLM forecasters. All numbers below come from the text fetched in this session. Where I read a preprint or accepted manuscript instead of the version of record, the entry says so. Supplementary materials were generally not read.

How the sources connect: the Reproducibility Project: Psychology (OSC 2015), the Experimental Economics Replication Project (Camerer 2016), the Social Sciences Replication Project (Camerer 2018) and Many Labs 2 are the ground truth. Dreber 2015, Camerer 2016/2018, Forsell 2019 and Gordon 2021 are the forecasts scored against that ground truth. Nosek 2018, Chambers & Tzavella 2022 and Scheel 2021 describe the commitment devices (pre-registration, in-principle acceptance) and the evidence on what they change.

## Part A — Pre-registration and Registered Reports

### The preregistration revolution — Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven & Mellor, 2018, PNAS 115(11):2600–2606, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856500/
- **Read depth:** full (PMC full text: introduction through conclusion, footnotes, references; the linked letter and reply were not read).
- **What was built or tested:** A conceptual and policy argument, not an experiment. Science alternates between postdiction (generating hypotheses from data) and prediction (testing them on new data). Hindsight bias and forking analysis paths make it easy to present postdiction as prediction, and that strips p-values of their meaning. Pre-registration means committing to analysis steps before seeing outcomes, in an independent registry that preserves the plan. The paper works through nine practical challenges, each with a named example researcher: procedure changes mid-study; assumption violations found during analysis; pre-existing data; longitudinal or large multivariate data; many experiments; a programme of research; few a priori expectations; competing predictions; and selective narrative in the write-up.
- **How it was scored:** Not applicable (no outcome measure). The authors say the evidence that pre-registration improves reproducibility is mostly correlational and theoretical so far.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** More than 8,000 preregistrations on OSF at the time of writing. Dozens of journals offered RRs. The Preregistration Challenge offered one thousand $1,000 awards. Cited evidence: in Franco et al., 40% of papers failed to report at least one manipulation and 70% failed to report at least one outcome; 96% of consistently significant findings were published versus 65% of null effects. Kaplan and Irvin found a sharp drop in positive results after clinical trials had to register primary outcomes.
- **What it did not measure:** Whether pre-registration actually changes error rates or replication. Whether the people who pre-register are calibrated about their own hypotheses (our Q1). What happens when one agent both writes the plan and grades the result, which is our founding-week failure.
- **What we borrow:** (1) The distinction between confirmatory and exploratory work as the basic label on every result. Every number our engine reports should be tagged "pre-registered test" or "exploratory", and only the first counts toward Q1 and Q2. (2) Their remedies, mapped onto our pre-registration commit: a preregistered decision tree for assumption violations (if the pass rule's statistic turns out ill-posed, the fallback is already written down); a sealed holdout split for "few a priori expectations" bets; and transparent logging of deviations instead of silent edits. (3) The "program of research" challenge maps onto our rule that every bet's outcome is recorded, including nulls, so the Brier score is not computed on a filtered set.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Add a "deviations" field to the pre-registration commit template, so any later change to pass rule, split, seed or budget is an explicit, timestamped diff and never a rewrite.
- Quote: "Preregistration does not favor prediction over postdiction".

### The past, present and future of Registered Reports — Chambers & Tzavella, 2022, Nature Human Behaviour 6(1):29–42 (online 15 Nov 2021), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01193-7
- **Read depth:** full main text of the author's accepted manuscript (MetaArXiv record 10.31222/osf.io/43298, file "Chambers&Tzavella2021-MS.pdf", v4), including Boxes 1–4. Table 1, the figures and the Supplementary Information were not read. The publisher and Cardiff repository PDFs returned 403. Counts may differ slightly from the version of record.
- **What was built or tested:** A review of the RR format. At Stage 1, authors submit the question, theory, hypotheses, methods and analysis plan. If the review is favourable, the journal grants in-principle acceptance (IPA) and commits to publish whatever the result, provided the authors follow the protocol and interpret the data in line with the evidence. At Stage 2, reviewers check only compliance and whether the conclusions follow; they cannot re-argue the design. The review also covers variants (results-blind review, accountable replications, post-publication-review RRs, publisher-level RRs, Peer Community In RR), "seven virtues" of a good RR, known limitations, and a metascience agenda.
- **How it was scored:** A narrative review of early metascience. No new experiment, apart from a small analysis of author demographics.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 295 journals offered RRs, with 483 Stage 2 articles from 81 outlets. 77% of 141 Stage 1 submissions to four journals were first-authored by PhD students or postdocs. Cited evidence on bias control: about 60% of RRs report null results, roughly five times the rate in regular articles (Allen & Mehler, 296 hypotheses in 127 RRs). In psychology, 4% of regular articles versus 56% of RRs failed to confirm their first hypothesis (Scheel et al.). 57% of 35 RRs with open data and code were computationally reproducible, versus 31% in an earlier sample of regular articles. In a preprint by Soderberg et al., 353 scientists rated RRs higher on rigour and overall quality, with no difference on novelty or creativity. Protocol transparency: in 2018 only 50% of 70 adopting journals required the Stage 1 protocol to be public; by the time of writing, 87% of 213 permanent adopters did. The Stage 2 rejection rate at the journals Chambers edits was zero.
- **What it did not measure:** Causal effects, since the review itself calls for an RCT (Box 4, Q5). Whether RR results replicate better (Box 4, Q2, which is open). How often Stage 2 papers deviate from Stage 1 (Box 4, Q3, open). Nothing on forecasters' calibration. The format is built for confirmatory, hypothesis-driven work and the authors say it is not designed for exploratory science, which is where most of our scout's bets start.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **IPA as the model for our pre-registration commit.** Our commit is Stage 1; the auditor session is the Stage 2 reviewer. The Stage 2 rule carries over directly: the auditor judges adherence to the commit and whether the claim follows from the data, and it may not change the pass rule after seeing the result. (2) **Outcome-neutral checks** (positive controls, manipulation checks, data-quality checks) declared at Stage 1, so a failed test can be told apart from a broken test. This is a direct guard against our "artefact" failure mode, where two founding-week results were pipeline artefacts. (3) **Box 2 Stage 2 questions** as the auditor's checklist: did the authors stay true to the protocol, are deviations documented, did the positive controls pass, are post hoc analyses clearly separated, are the conclusions centred on the preregistered outcomes. (4) **"Computationally-generated RRs" with machine-readable hypotheses**: our pass rule is already machine-readable, so the auditor's first check can be mechanical. (5) **Observer-evaluator review and "rolling IPA"** describe almost exactly a human steering an AI loop in real time, and they give us vocabulary for the human's steering log (Q3, Q4).
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Require every pre-registration commit to name at least one outcome-neutral positive control that must pass before the primary statistic is read.
- Quote: "reviewers do not relitigate the theory, hypotheses or methods".

### An Excess of Positive Results: Comparing the Standard Psychology Literature With Registered Reports — Scheel, Schijen & Lakens, 2021, Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science 4(2), https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467
- **Read depth:** full (PsyArXiv preprint p6e9c, current file version, which states that a copy-edited version is published in AMPPS 4(2), 2021). The publisher HTML returned 403.
- **What was built or tested:** A preregistered comparison of positive-result rates. The sample was every psychology RR in the COS database as of November 2018 that met strict inclusion criteria (N = 71 after 80 of 151 entries were excluded), against a random sample of 152 standard reports from 2013–2018 drawn with Fanelli's (2010) search phrase "test* the hypothes*". The outcome was whether the authors reported support for the first (preregistered) hypothesis.
- **How it was scored:** One coder coded every paper; difficult cases were double-coded (24 RRs and 47 standard reports), with Cohen's κ = .808 (3 major disagreements). Coding could not be blinded to format. The preregistered test was a one-sided proportion test plus an equivalence test with a smallest effect of interest of ±6 percentage points.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 31/71 RRs had positive results (43.66%, 95% CI 31.91–55.95) against 146/152 standard reports (96.05%, 95% CI 91.61–98.54); χ²(1) = 77.96, p < .001. 57.75% of RRs were close replications, against 2.63% of standard reports. Excluding replications, the rates were 50.00% (15/30) against 95.95% (142/148). Within the three journals that published both formats: 11/13 standard reports against 7/14 RRs were positive. Only 2 of 71 RRs would have been found with Fanelli's search phrase.
- **What it did not measure:** Causation. The authors list confounds themselves: riskier hypotheses may be sent to the RR format, RR authors may be more conscientious, and RR journals may have different editorial policies. Not measured: the prior plausibility of hypotheses in each format, effect sizes, replication of the RR results, or anything about forecasters.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **The positive-result rate as a health metric for our own output.** If the engine's "real result" rate on pre-registered bets is near 96%, that signals its grading has drifted, as it did in our founding week. A rate between 40% and 60% is what a well-guarded pipeline in this literature produces. This gives Q2 a concrete reference: compare the overclaim rate before and after audit, and the positive-result rate of audited against unaudited write-ups. (2) **The ±6-point smallest effect of interest with an equivalence test** is a template for stating our own Q2 pass rule, i.e. how big a change in overclaim rate counts. (3) **Their method also shows a failure mode.** Several decisions were not preregistered (resampling, replication coding, dropping the random double-coding), and the authors list them openly. Our auditor should look for exactly this class of disclosed and undisclosed deviation. (4) The first-hypothesis rule is a simple, gameable unit of analysis. We should score every pre-registered bet, not the one the engine chooses to lead with.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Track a running "fraction of pre-registered bets passing" and flag the run if it rises above about 0.8 without a matching rise in the engine's stated odds.
- Quote: "not much credence may be left".

## Part B — Ground truth: large replication projects

### Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science (Reproducibility Project: Psychology) — Open Science Collaboration, 2015, Science 349(6251):aac4716, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716
- **Read depth:** full main text (authors' peer-reviewed copy, from OSF osf.io/phtye via the Edinburgh Research Explorer). The long supplement attached to the same PDF was not read.
- **What was built or tested:** 100 replications by 270 contributing authors. The studies came from 2008 articles in Psychological Science, JPSP and JEP:LMC, drawn quasi-randomly from a sampling frame. By default the last study in each article was replicated, with one key effect identified before data collection. Protocols were reviewed by the original authors and registered publicly. An independent analyst reproduced every analysis from a controller R script.
- **How it was scored:** Five replication indicators: significance in the original direction; whether the original effect falls in the replication's 95% CI; effect-size comparison; a fixed-effect meta-analysis of original and replication; and the replication team's subjective "did it replicate?" verdict. Moderators were correlated with these indicators (Spearman).
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 97% of original effects were significant. 35/97 replications were significant (36.1%, 95% CI 26.6–46.2) when about 89 were expected from the mean power of 0.92. Effect sizes fell from mean r = 0.403 to 0.197, and 82/99 originals had the larger effect. 47.4% of original effects fell inside the replication CI. 68% (51/75) were significant in the meta-analysis. The subjective "yes" rate was 39/100. By field: social psychology 14/55 (25%), cognitive 21/42 (50%). Interaction effects replicated less often (8/37, 22%) than main effects (23/49, 47%). 20/32 (63%) originals with p < 0.001 replicated. Strength of original evidence predicted replication; team expertise and self-assessed replication quality did not. More surprising effects replicated less often.
- **What it did not measure:** Prospective forecasts (Dreber 2015 added those to a subset). Generalisation beyond three journals in one year. Only one study and one effect per article. The authors say outright that no effect was established as true or false.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **The multi-indicator verdict.** The auditor should report more than one replication criterion (sign plus significance, CI coverage, effect-size ratio), because a binary verdict hides "same direction, much smaller". This feeds the Q2 "verdict reversal" measure. (2) **The independent re-analysis from a single script** is the model for the auditor's first act: rerun the committed code on the committed seed before reading the write-up. (3) **"Surprisingness" predicting failure** gives Q1 a prior: the engine's most exciting bets should get lower odds, and we can test whether its stated odds already reflect this. (4) **Effect-size shrinkage of about 50%** is a base rate the engine's write-ups should be discounted against.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Have the auditor record three verdicts per result (direction/significance, CI coverage of the claimed effect, ratio of replicated to claimed effect) and use disagreement between them as an overclaim signal.
- Quote: "how many of the effects have we established are true? Zero."

### Evaluating replicability of laboratory experiments in economics (EERP) — Camerer, Dreber, Forsell, Ho, Huber, Johannesson, Kirchler, Almenberg, Altmejd, Chan, Heikensten, Holzmeister, Imai, Isaksson, Nave, Pfeiffer, Razen & Wu, 2016, Science 351(6280):1433–1436, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf0918
- **Read depth:** full main text of the submitted manuscript dated 3 March 2016 (MPRA Paper No. 75461). The Science version of record and the supplement were not read, so final figures may differ slightly.
- **What was built or tested:** Replications of all 18 between-subject lab experiments published in AER and QJE in 2011–2014. Analysis plans were posted publicly beforehand and sent to the original authors. Replications had at least 90% power to detect the original effect (mean 0.92). Before the replications ran, there were prediction markets and a pre-market survey with 97 traders.
- **How it was scored:** The RPP indicators, plus market and survey beliefs compared with outcomes. Original p-value and sample size were tested as predictors.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 11/18 replicated (61.1%), when 92% was expected (binomial p < 0.001). Mean r fell from 0.474 to 0.279, a mean relative effect of 65.9%. 12 (66.7%) had the original inside the replication CI. 83.3% fell inside the prediction interval. 14 (77.8%) were significant in the meta-analysis. The mean market belief was 75.2% and the mean survey belief 71.1%, both above the observed 61.1% but not significantly so. The market–outcome correlation was not significant, and in this sample markets were not more accurate than surveys. Original p-value and sample size correlated with replication at about 0.5. Only 1 of 8 studies with original p < 0.01 failed. (Forsell 2019 later reports both markets and survey here as 61% correct, with Spearman 0.30 for markets and 0.52 for survey.)
- **What it did not measure:** The forecasters' track record over time. Whether forecasts improve with feedback. Anything about steering or audit. The sample of 18 is small.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **A cheap statistic can beat a crowd.** The original p-value and sample size predicted replication at least as well as the market. For Q1 this is the baseline to beat: a naive predictor built from the bet's own design statistics (power, effect size, budget). If the engine's odds do not beat it, the engine is adding nothing. (2) **Peer beliefs were optimistic** (75% predicted against 61% observed). Watch for the same direction of miscalibration in the engine and in the human (Q1, Q3).
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Before scoring engine odds, fit a two-feature baseline (planned power, prior effect size) on the same bets and report the engine's Brier score relative to it.
- Quote: "peer surveys and market beliefs did contain some information".

### Evaluating the replicability of social science experiments in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015 (SSRP) — Camerer, Dreber, Holzmeister, Ho, Huber, Johannesson, Kirchler, Nave, Nosek, Pfeiffer, Altmejd, Buttrick, Chan, Chen, Forsell, Gampa, Heikensten, Hummer, Imai, Isaksson, Manfredi, Rose, Wagenmakers & Wu (24 authors), 2018, Nature Human Behaviour 2(9):637–644, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z
- **Read depth:** full main text (published letter PDF, OCR text layer). The Supplementary Methods, which include market participant counts, were not read.
- **What was built or tested:** All 21 experimental social-science studies in Nature and Science from 2010–2015 that met the inclusion criteria. A two-stage design: stage 1 had 90% power to detect 75% of the original effect; if it failed, stage 2 pooled data for 90% power to detect 50% of the original effect. Plans were pre-registered on OSF and reviewed by the original authors. Each final report includes an "Unplanned protocol deviations" section. Prediction markets and surveys were run in parallel.
- **How it was scored:** A primary significance criterion, plus relative effect size, meta-analysis, 95% prediction interval, small telescopes, one-sided default Bayes factor, a Bayesian mixture model of the true-positive rate, and peer beliefs.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 12/21 (57.1%) after stage 1 and 13/21 (61.9%) after stage 2. Mean r fell from 0.460 to 0.249, a mean relative effect of 46.2%: 74.5% among the studies that replicated and 0.3% among those that did not. Estimated true-positive rate 67%; relative effect size of true positives 71%. Mean market belief 63.4% and mean survey belief 60.6%, against 61.9% observed. Spearman correlation with outcome: markets 0.842, surveys 0.761. Deviations: 7 none, 12 minor, 1 unintended methodological, 1 stage-2 continuation by mistake. The authors' informal range for average reproducibility in the social and behavioural sciences is 35–75%. (Forsell 2019 reports both markets and survey as 86% correct here.)
- **What it did not measure:** Individual forecasters' calibration. Whether forecasts would survive a non-prestige sample. With n = 21 it cannot test determinants of replication.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **The two-stage power design** is a template for budgeted falsification tests. Our "cheapest falsifying test" could be stage 1, with a pre-declared stage-2 extension used only if stage 1 is inconclusive, so the budget is not extended after peeking. (2) **The required "unplanned protocol deviations" section with a four-level severity scale** (none, minor, unintended methodological, procedural mistake) can be copied directly into our auditor report. (3) **True-positive rate versus effect inflation** separates two kinds of overclaim: "not real" and "real but smaller". Q2 should score both. (4) Peer beliefs were close to calibrated here (63% against 62%), unlike EERP and RPP, so calibration varies with the population of claims. Our Brier score should be reported per bet category, not only pooled.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Split the overclaim metric into "false positive" and "inflated effect" and have the auditor label each overclaim with one or the other.
- Quote: "failures to replicate were not the result of chance alone".

## Part C — Forecasting replication: markets, surveys and their calibration

### Using prediction markets to estimate the reproducibility of scientific research — Dreber, Pfeiffer, Almenberg, Isaksson, Wilson, Chen, Nosek & Johannesson, 2015, PNAS 112(50):15343–15347, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4687569/
- **Read depth:** full (PMC: significance, abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods, SI excerpts). The last ~23k characters of the page (end of the reference list) were not read. The Harvard PDF mirror returned 403.
- **What was built or tested:** Prediction markets on 44 RPP studies (41 completed) in two rounds: November 2012 (23 studies, 47 active traders) and October 2014 (21 studies, 45 active traders). Each trader had a $100 endowment. Contracts paid $1 if the study replicated (P < 0.05, same direction). Markets used a logarithmic market scoring rule (b = 100) and were open for 2 weeks. A pre-trading survey collected individual probabilities and self-rated expertise. Traders could not trade on studies they were replicating.
- **How it was scored:** Market prices and survey beliefs against binary RPP outcomes, using the fraction correct at a 0.5 threshold, the point-biserial correlation, absolute prediction error, and a calibration slope. A Bayesian reconstruction estimated the prior p0, the probability after the original study p1, and after replication p2.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 16/41 replicated (39%). Mean market price 55% (range 13–88%). Markets were correct on 29/41 (71%, binomial p = 0.012), against 69% expected under calibration. Correlation 0.42 (p = 0.006). Calibration regression slope 0.995. The survey was correct on 23/40 (58%, n.s.), with correlation 0.27. Market absolute error was significantly lower than the survey's (p = 0.015). Round 1 got 20/23 (87%) correct and round 2 only 9/18 (50%), a significant difference (p = 0.016); round 2 reported lower expertise. Prior p0 had a median of 8.8%; p1 after an original positive finding had a median of 56%.
- **What it did not measure:** Forecaster-level track records. The cost of markets compared with alternatives. Original power was estimated post hoc. A few replicators knew results before the markets closed (8 in round 1, 1 in round 2). One co-author is employed by the market platform provider.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **Market price as a baseline for engine odds (Q1).** We cannot run a market, but the comparison structure carries over: individual pre-data probabilities (our engine and our human) scored against an aggregate, with the fraction correct, a correlation, and a calibration slope reported alongside the Brier score. (2) **The p0 → p1 → p2 decomposition** puts the engine's "probability this is real" in Bayesian terms. Engine odds before the test are p0, and the auditor's post-test verdict should move them to something like p2. (3) **Round-to-round instability** (87% against 50%) warns that one batch of bets can make a forecaster look good or bad. Q1 needs enough bets, reported with confidence intervals. (4) **The leakage failure mode** (replicators knew results before close) maps onto our rule that odds must be committed before any data, enforced by git timestamp.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Report engine and human calibration with a slope and intercept (outcome regressed on stated probability) as well as the Brier score, with bootstrap CIs.
- Quote: "prediction markets are a promising tool for assessing the reproducibility".

### Predicting replication outcomes in the Many Labs 2 study — Forsell, Viganola, Pfeiffer, Almenberg, Wilson, Chen, Nosek, Johannesson & Dreber, 2019, Journal of Economic Psychology 75:102117, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2018.10.009
- **Read depth:** full (open-access version of record from the Stockholm School of Economics repository: abstract, introduction, design, results, concluding remarks, appendix).
- **What was built or tested:** Markets and surveys on 24 of the 28 Many Labs 2 effects. Binary markets asked whether the effect would be significant in the original direction at p < 0.001. Six-bin markets asked for the replication effect relative to the original. 107 people expressed interest, 91 completed the survey, and 78 traded. Each trader had a $50 endowment. Markets ran for two weeks in fall 2014. Because Many Labs 2 results were delayed until 2018, payouts were settled at closing prices, not outcomes.
- **How it was scored:** Fraction correct at 0.5, Spearman correlation with outcome, mean absolute prediction error, and comparison of mean predicted against observed replication rate and relative effect.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** 11/24 (46%) replicated. Mean market belief was 0.644 and mean survey belief 0.647, both overestimating replication (p = 0.018 and p = 0.016). Markets got 18/24 (75%) correct and the survey 16/24 (67%). Spearman with outcome: 0.755 for markets, 0.731 for the survey. Mean absolute error was 0.354 for markets and 0.394 for the survey (p = 0.003). The effect-size markets traded thinly: mean predicted relative effect 0.671 against an observed 0.353. Survey effect-size beliefs did better (mean 0.396; Spearman 0.614 against 0.484 for markets). Pooled over four market studies: markets 73% (76/104), surveys 66% (68/103). The authors conclude scientists somewhat overestimate reproducibility.
- **What it did not measure:** Incentive-compatible scoring against outcomes (settling at closing prices is not). Individual calibration. Learning across rounds.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **Two forecast targets, not one.** "Will it pass?" and "how big will the effect be relative to the claim?" behaved differently. For Q1 the engine should state both a probability and an expected effect ratio, and the two should be scored separately. (2) **Settling at closing prices** is exactly the failure we must avoid: forecasts scored against other forecasts, not against outcomes. Every engine odd must be scored only against the audited outcome. (3) **Information-error failure mode.** Participants were given wrong original p-values for three studies, and the authors disclose and test this. Our scout's summaries of prior literature, which feed the engine's odds, need the same check, and the auditor should spot-check them. (4) **Threshold drift.** Markets were set up at p < 0.001 but Many Labs 2 published at p < 0.0001. The pass rule must be frozen in the commit and the scorer must read it from there.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Add an "expected effect relative to the prior claim" field next to the odds in every pre-registration commit.
- Quote: "scientists on average somewhat overestimate the reproducibility of published findings".

### Predicting replicability: Analysis of survey and prediction market data from large-scale forecasting projects — Gordon, Viganola, Dreber, Johannesson & Pfeiffer, 2021, PLOS ONE 16(4):e0248780, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0248780
- **Read depth:** full (PLOS HTML: introduction, methods, results 3.1–3.5, discussion, tables and figures, read through the fetch tool). I did not see the text verbatim, so there is no quote.
- **What was built or tested:** A pooled re-analysis of forecasts for 103 findings from RPP, EERP, ML2 and SSRP, using only participants who traded.
- **How it was scored:** Fraction correct, correlation, mean absolute error, extremeness, aggregation variants (mean, median, voting, variance-weighted), and how error falls with trading. It does not report Brier scores.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** Markets got 73% (75/103) correct and surveys 66% (68/103); the difference was not significant (χ² p = 0.29). Correlation with outcome: r = .581 for markets, .564 for surveys. Mean absolute error 0.384 against 0.423 (p < .001). Both methods were more accurate at predicting non-replication than replication. Both overestimated replication (survey 0.61, market 0.63, observed 0.49). About 90% of the error reduction came within the first 69 trades. Findings with original p ≤ 0.005 replicated about 74% of the time; those with p > 0.005 about 28%.
- **What it did not measure:** Individual-forecaster skill. Feedback effects. Replication criteria and power differ across the four projects.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **Asymmetric accuracy.** Forecasters were better at calling failures than successes. Q1 should report engine calibration separately for odds below and above 0.5. (2) **The original-p-value rule (p ≤ 0.005)** is a strong, free baseline for any bet that builds on a published claim. The scout should record the source claim's p-value, and Q5 (adjacent-literature bets against AI-generated bets) should control for it. (3) **Systematic optimism (0.63 against 0.49)** is the baseline direction of human bias. If our human also overestimates, his steering value (Q3) has to be judged on discrimination, not calibration in level.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** For literature-sourced bets, log the source finding's p-value and sample size at scouting time so the p ≤ 0.005 baseline can be computed later.

## Part D — Research taste and forecasting research outcomes

### Predict science to improve science — DellaVigna, Pope & Vivalt, 2019, Science 366(6464):428–429, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaz1704
- **Read depth:** full (two-page Policy Forum, PDF mirror of the published article).
- **What was built or tested:** A proposal, not an experiment. The authors argue for collecting forecasts of research results before studies run, and announce the Social Science Prediction Platform (with BITSS). It describes a seven-step workflow: the researcher designs the study and a forecasting survey; the platform distributes it; forecasts are released to the researcher at a prespecified date; results go back to the forecasters; an optional follow-up measures belief updating. It recommends eliciting the mean effect, a 90% interval, and the sign or significance.
- **How it was scored:** Not applicable. It summarises prior studies: in an 18-treatment experiment, experts were highly accurate on average, highly cited faculty did no better than other faculty, and PhD students did best. Policy-makers were more optimistic but less certain than researchers, updated more on good news than bad, and barely responded to confidence-interval width.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** Few numbers beyond the 18-treatment example. Three claimed benefits: better interpretation of results against priors, less bias against null results (results are compared to the expert prior, not to zero), and better forecasting accuracy and experimental design. Tracking forecasts over time is the way to find "superforecasters".
- **What it did not measure:** Platform outcomes, which did not yet exist. Whether forecasts improve research selection in practice. Nothing about AI forecasters.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **Comparing results against the prior instead of the null.** For Q1 and the write-ups, every result should be reported as "engine expected X, human expected Y, observed Z". That makes a null informative and makes overclaiming visible. (2) **The elicitation format** (point estimate plus 90% interval plus sign/significance) is richer than a single probability and costs little to add to our commit. (3) **Longitudinal track records to find who forecasts well** frame Q3 directly: the human's odds and steering acts are a longitudinal forecast record and should be scored that way. (4) **Hindsight bias as the reason for ex-ante capture** justifies our rule that the human records his odds before data.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Extend the human's odds sheet to point estimate, 90% interval and sign, matching the engine's fields, so the two can be scored on the same scale.
- Quote: "set up the collection of predictions before the results are known".

### Estimating the deep replicability of scientific findings using human and artificial intelligence — Yang, Youyou & Uzzi, 2020, PNAS 117(20):10762–10768, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1909046117
- **Read depth:** full main text (author-hosted PNAS PDF from Kellogg). The SI was not read. PMC returned a browser check.
- **What was built or tested:** A text-based replication predictor. word2vec (200 dimensions) was trained on 2 million Microsoft Academic Graph abstracts. Each paper became a frequency-weighted mean word vector, with numbers and authors stripped out. An ensemble of bagged random forests and bagged logistic regression was trained on 96 RPP papers (100 rounds of threefold cross-validation). It was then tested out of sample on 317 more replicated studies from 8 psychology projects, a student replication archive, the 18-study EERP, and 122 Economics Replication Wiki studies (413 studies in total, from 80 journals).
- **How it was scored:** Accuracy at the base-rate threshold and top-k precision, compared with a "predict all fail" dummy (0.61), a "reviewer metrics" model built from reported statistics, and, on 100 overlapping papers, prediction markets and surveys.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** Narrative-only accuracy was 0.68 (the text also says 69%) against a 0.61 dummy, with top-k precision 0.74. Bag-of-words scored 0.60. The reviewer-metrics model scored 0.66 / 0.72, and text plus metrics 0.71 / 0.76. Out of sample: test set I (n = 117) 0.69 / 0.76; student projects (n = 57) 0.65 / 0.69; economics experiments 0.78; Replication Wiki 0.66. On the 10 most confident "pass" calls, the model and the markets were both 90% correct. On the 10 most confident "fail" calls, the model was 90% correct and the markets 90% (in the text) or 100% (in the figure caption). The paper's tests found no bias by author prestige, sex, journal, discipline or hedging language. Unusual n-grams were more frequent in non-replicating papers.
- **What it did not measure:** Calibrated probabilities (it reports accuracy, not Brier). Causal reasons. It is explicitly exploratory, and the data are available on request. Labels come from one-shot replications.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **A cheap text-only predictor as a Q1 baseline.** Our engine's odds should beat a model that sees only the write-up text. If they don't, the engine's "odds" may be reading its own prose style. (2) **"Narrative beats statistics"** suggests a test for Q2: does the auditor's verdict depend on the write-up's wording? Run the audit on a style-normalised version of the write-up and see whether verdicts change. (3) **Top-k precision** suits triage: when the engine ranks bets, check whether its lowest-ranked bets actually fail most often.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Keep a style-stripped copy of each write-up so the auditor can later be run blind to rhetoric, which gives a measure of how much framing drives verdicts.
- Quote: "our samples are necessarily limited by being one-shot replication tests".

### Large language models surpass human experts in predicting neuroscience results (BrainBench) — Luo, Rechardt, Sun, … Love (39 authors), online 2024, Nature Human Behaviour 9(2):305–315 (2025), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02046-9 ; arXiv 2403.03230
- **Read depth:** full (arXiv HTML v4, 28 Nov 2024, read through the fetch tool: results, discussion, methods, supplementary tables). The final ~1.6k characters (an error-reporting footer) were not read.
- **What was built or tested:** BrainBench, a forward-looking benchmark of 200 human-written test cases (plus 100 GPT-4-written cases analysed separately) from 2023 Journal of Neuroscience abstracts. In each case, the results in an abstract were minimally altered (for example, a direction reversed) while background and methods were kept. The task is to pick the real abstract. Fifteen LLMs were tested, plus BrainGPT (Mistral-7B with LoRA on about 1.3B tokens of neuroscience text from 2002–2022). 171 of 202 recruited human experts passed screening.
- **How it was scored:** The LLM picks the version with lower perplexity. Humans choose directly and give a confidence rating. The metric is accuracy, plus the confidence–accuracy relationship. Memorisation checks used the zlib-perplexity ratio and publication date against difficulty.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** Mean LLM accuracy 81.4% against 63.4% for human experts; 66.2% for the top 20% by self-rated expertise. BrainGPT added about 3%. For both LLMs (confidence = perplexity gap) and humans, higher confidence went with higher accuracy. Human–LLM item difficulty correlated only about 0.15, against about 0.75 among LLMs. Performance dropped when only the results sentence was given, which the authors read as integration across context. No memorisation signal was found.
- **What it did not measure:** Prediction of whether a real study's result is true or will replicate. The task is choosing the published version, so "matches the literature's direction" is rewarded, not "is real". Open-ended prediction (two-choice only). Closed models were not tested, since scoring needs model weights. The study was not preregistered.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **Confidence–accuracy curves as the Q1 instrument.** BrainBench shows that relative confidence carries signal for both humans and LLMs. Our Brier decomposition (resolution versus reliability) is the probabilistic version of their curve. (2) **Low human–LLM item overlap (r ≈ 0.15)** is the strongest available argument that a human's judgment could add value when combined with an AI's (Q3): the two get different items wrong. We should measure the same overlap between our human's misses and the engine's. (3) **A caution for Q5.** An LLM that is good at predicting what the literature already says will rate adjacent-literature bets as likely. That can inflate apparent calibration on literature-sourced bets compared with novel ones, so Q5 must compare Brier scores within each source, not only hit rates.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Compute the correlation between the engine's per-bet errors and the human's per-bet errors. A low correlation is the precondition for any Q3 gain.
- Quote: "LLMs were scored as choosing the abstract with the lower perplexity".

### Predicting Results of Social Science Experiments Using Large Language Models — Hewitt, Ashokkumar, Ghezae & Willer, 2024 (working paper, 8 August 2024), PDF mirror at samim.io; project page treatmenteffect.app
- **Read depth:** full main text of the 8 August 2024 working paper (abstract through discussion; SI and references not read). A later version reportedly gives different archive counts (469 effects, 119,330 participants, 15 megastudies), but I saw that only in search snippets and did not verify it. The numbers below are from the August 2024 text.
- **What was built or tested:** An archive of 70 pre-registered, nationally representative US survey experiments (50 from TESS, 2016–2022, plus 20 from a replication project), with 476 treatment effects and 105,165 participants, all re-analysed in one consistent way. GPT-4 simulated responses from demographically sampled personas, using an ensemble of prompts. The supplementary archive had 9 megastudies with 346 effects.
- **How it was scored:** Correlation between predicted and observed effects (median r over 16 random control/outcome draws, also disattenuated), direction accuracy on significant contrasts, and RMSE. The benchmarks were N = 2,659 lay forecasters and expert forecasts where the megastudies had them.
- **Headline result (numbers, from the text):** r = 0.85 (radj 0.91), with 90% correct direction on significant contrasts. Studies not public before GPT-4's training cutoff gave r = 0.90; published ones r = 0.74. Lay forecasters gave r = 0.79. Averaging human and LLM forecasts gave r = 0.88, and both were independent predictors (b = 0.35 and 0.32). The raw LLM predictions overestimated effects: RMSE 10.9pp against 8.4pp for forecasters, or 5.3pp after rescaling by 0.56. Megastudies: survey r = 0.47, field r = 0.27. Where experts forecast too, the LLM had r = 0.37 against 0.25 for experts.
- **What it did not measure:** Whether a result is real or replicates; ground truth here is the original estimate. No calibration of probabilities. Only US, text-based, survey-measured outcomes.
- **What we borrow:** (1) **"Combine human and machine" measured as two independent regression coefficients** is the cleanest available design for Q3. Regress outcomes on engine odds and human odds together; a non-zero human coefficient means he adds value. (2) **Systematic effect overestimation by the LLM** matches our founding-week overclaiming and suggests a fixed shrinkage check: the auditor compares claimed effect sizes against the engine's own pre-data expectation. (3) **The post-cutoff split** is a template for a contamination control in Q5: literature-sourced bets may be "predicted" from memory, so record whether the source result predates the engine model's training cutoff.
- **Next step it suggests for us:** Specify the Q3 analysis now: outcome regressed on engine odds plus human odds, with the human coefficient and its CI as the pre-registered statistic.
- Quote: "raw predictions derived from GPT-4 systematically overestimated actual effects".

## Cluster synthesis

This field has measured three things. First, how often published claims hold up under pre-registered re-runs: about 36% in RPP psychology, 61% in experimental economics, 62% in Nature/Science social science, and 46% in Many Labs 2. Replicated effects are about half the original size. Second, what a commitment device changes: Registered Reports, which fix the plan and promise publication before results, report support for their first hypothesis about 44% of the time, against 96% in standard papers. Third, whether forecasters can tell in advance which claims will hold. Markets get about 73% right and surveys about 66%, both systematically optimistic, and cheap statistics (original p-value, a text-only model) come close. LLMs now match or beat humans at forecasting experimental results, with errors that overlap little with human errors and with inflated effect sizes.

The gap: every forecaster studied so far predicts other people's finished work. Nobody has scored an agent that states odds on its own bets, designs the test, runs it, and then has its claims audited. None measures how much an independent audit reverses an AI's own verdicts, or whether a human steerer adds discrimination beyond the AI. The RR evidence is not yet causal, and assumes human authors and reviewers.

Our testbed borrows in-principle acceptance, outcome-neutral controls, multi-indicator verdicts and market-style scoring, and applies them to a single AI research loop. That yields pre-data odds and audit-reversal data, at the cost of far fewer bets and no ground truth on the scale of a replication project.
