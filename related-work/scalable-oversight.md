### Sandwiching (background) — Ajeya Cotra, 2021, "The case for aligning narrowly superhuman models", AI Alignment Forum / LessWrong (posted 5 March 2021), https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PZtsoaoSLpKjjbMqM/the-case-for-aligning-narrowly-superhuman-models
- Read depth: partial. The post was fetched, but the tool returned a structured summary of it, not the full text. I covered the sandwiching section, the appendix on checking whether the model is "doing the best it can", and the objections section. I did not read it verbatim, so there is no direct quote.
- What was built or tested: Nothing. It is a research-agenda proposal. Put a model between a less-capable group of humans and a more-capable group (experts) on a fuzzy task. Have experts produce a gold-standard model, then find out whether the non-experts, using only processes they can run themselves, can reach the same quality. Example tasks: coding puzzles overseen by non-coders, long-form economics or physics answers overseen by laypeople, and English-French translation overseen by monolinguals.
- How it was scored: Proposed. Experts' judgments, or an expert-trained model, are the reference. Proposed checks include augmented experts vs augmented non-experts, comparison with real outcomes in hindsight (e.g. stock picks scored a year later), and interpretability.
- Headline result: None. It is a proposal.
- What it did not measure: No data. By the author's own account it is not designed to catch deliberate deception. It concerns training a model to a target, not choosing which questions are worth asking. It has no calibration component, and no scoring of the overseers' own judgment over time.
- What we borrow: The hindsight-outcome check is the structure of our ledger. Pre-registered falsification results play the "expert" role for engine odds and human odds (Q1, Q3). The three-tier framing (weak overseer, model, reference) is the skeleton of Q3.
- Next step it suggests for us: State in the paper which component of our loop plays each sandwich role: operator = non-expert, engine = model, pre-registered outcome = expert.

### Measuring Progress on Scalable Oversight — Samuel R. Bowman, Jeeyoon Hyun, Ethan Perez, et al. (Anthropic; Surge AI), 2022, arXiv:2211.03540 (v2, 11 Nov 2022), https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03540
- Read depth: full for the main text (sections 1–6, including "Limitations of Our Results"). Appendices (participant instructions, example dialogs, the overconfident-error example) were not read.
- What was built or tested: The sandwiching paradigm is stated formally, with an inner loop (non-experts align the model) and an outer loop (experts deliver a verdict on the strategy). Two relaxations are used: a static model reached only through chat, and dataset labels in place of experts. In the proof of concept, crowdworkers (Surge AI; 5 per task, 4 kept) answer 100 MMLU questions and 100 QuALITY questions (5-minute limit) by chatting with a 52B RLHF-tuned assistant. They give each answer with a 5-point confidence rating. The correct answer is revealed after each question.
- How it was scored: Accuracy against the dataset labels, plus a 5-bin calibration error. Conditions: unassisted human, model zero-shot, model 5-shot, model best-of-20 chain-of-thought, human+model, and confidence-weighted majority vote. Published expert estimates serve as the ceiling.
- Headline result: MMLU accuracy was 57.2% for unassisted humans, 65.6% for the best model and 75.4% for human+model (78.0% with majority vote; expert estimate 90.0%). QuALITY was 48.6%, 66.9% and 76.8% (86.0% with majority vote; untimed human teams 93.5%). Model access raised confidence from 54% to 80% on MMLU and from 38% to 75% on QuALITY. On MMLU, human-model teams were worse calibrated than humans or the model alone (CE 12 and 18, vs 6 and 10). Two outlier participants (84% and 89% unassisted) were removed so the sandwich held. The authors note participants "sometimes give highly confident judgments that turn out to be wrong."
- What it did not measure: Every question has a known answer. There is no open-ended choice of what to study, and no case where nobody knows the answer. Revealing answers removes the hardest part of oversight, which is knowing when your method is good enough to trust. Calibration is measured but not examined over time. Nobody checks whether the human's contribution grows or shrinks as they learn. There is one model and one seed condition, so path dependence is not addressed. The selection effect from removing the outliers is acknowledged.
- What we borrow: (1) Sandwiching as the design for Q3. Score the engine alone, the human alone and engine+human against the same pre-registered outcomes. (2) Their pre-declared confidence-weighted combination rule as a template for our "combined" forecast, fixed before data. (3) A failure mode to test for: teaming can raise accuracy while making calibration worse, so Q3 must report Brier and calibration separately. (4) Their learning-effect check (first half vs second half) for the operator's odds.
- Next step it suggests for us: Pre-register the rule that combines engine and human odds before any Q3 scoring, and report the operator's Brier score by time period to detect learning.

### AI safety via debate — Geoffrey Irving, Paul Christiano, Dario Amodei (OpenAI), 2018, arXiv:1805.00899 (v1 2 May 2018, v2 22 Oct 2018), https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00899
- Read depth: full (all sections, through "Conclusions and future work"). Figure contents beyond captions were not read.
- What was built or tested: Mostly theory. Debate is set up as a zero-sum game: two agents make alternating statements and a judge picks the winner. The complexity analogy is that debate with polynomially many steps reaches PSPACE, while direct judging reaches NP. The one experiment is MNIST. Two MCTS agents, one honest and one liar, each reveal pixels to a fixed classifier judge that sees only 4 or 6 pixels. There is also an informal human prototype (single-pixel cat-vs-dog debate).
- How it was scored: The honest player's win rate against a pre-specified liar, compared with the judge's accuracy on random pixels. Conditions with and without precommitment (the liar must declare its lie up front).
- Headline result: With precommitment, a 6-pixel judge that is 59.4% accurate on random pixels becomes 88.9% accurate under debate, and a 4-pixel judge goes from 48.2% to 85.2%. Without precommitment the mean honest win rate was 74.4% (6 pixels) and 59.7% (4 pixels), so precommitment was crucial. The central claim: "In the debate game, it is harder to lie than to refute a lie."
- What it did not measure: No natural-language or human-judge experiment (left to future work). No real expertise gap beyond hidden pixels. No calibration. No open-ended questions. The authors themselves flag that judges must be told to reward justified admissions of ignorance, and that probabilistic arguments are hard for humans to judge.
- What we borrow: (1) Precommitment is our pre-registration. Their result is direct evidence that forcing a claim to be stated before argument weakens a liar, so it supports committing the pass rule before the run. (2) Rules for the auditor: one decisive flaw is enough to sink a result, and a justified "cannot be resolved at this budget" verdict is valid and should be logged separately from pass or fail (Q2). (3) Their suggestion to require stated confidence on each sub-claim, so that only overconfident claims can be attacked. That maps onto engine odds per claim (Q1). (4) Their convergence check, asking the judge's view after each statement, as a stop rule for multi-round audits.
- Next step it suggests for us: Add an explicit "unresolved, justified" verdict class to the audit log, and track how often it is used.

### Debate Helps Supervise Unreliable Experts — Julian Michael, Salsabila Mahdi, David Rein, Jackson Petty, Julien Dirani, Vishakh Padmakumar, Samuel R. Bowman (NYU; Cohere; Anthropic), 2023, arXiv:2311.08702 (v1, 15 Nov 2023), https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08702
- Read depth: full for the main text (sections 1–9, including Limitations, Related Work and Future Work). The 28 transcripts and the appendices on partial controls and feedback surveys were not read.
- What was built or tested: Human debates on QuALITY-hard questions with an information gap. Debaters have read the sci-fi story; the judge sees only arguments and verified quotes. Two answer options are given, one correct. The baseline is "consultancy": a single expert argues an assigned side that is wrong 50% of the time, framed as a worst-case stand-in for RLHF. There were 19 participants (mostly NYU debate-team members) and 413 sessions across human debate, human consultancy, GPT-4 debate and GPT-4 consultancy. Judges choose when to stop.
- How it was scored: Judge accuracy against the dataset label. Judges are scored with a log scoring rule and a 0.05 penalty per extra round (strictly proper). Also measured: calibration (ECE, 10% bins), efficiency in rounds and bits per round, and error analysis tagged by hand.
- Headline result: Human debate reached 84% judge accuracy against 74% for human consultancy (p = 0.04). GPT-4 debate reached 78% and GPT-4 consultancy 80%, with no significant difference. The estimated ceiling is 92.5%. Debates were 68% of the length of consultancies (2.7 rounds vs 4). Dishonest human consultants deceived judges 40% of the time. Final-judgment ECE was 0.11 in human debate, with overconfidence above 90%. Judges ended too early in 33% of mistakes. Honest debaters missed usable evidence, with "46% of errors in human debate attributable to mistakes by the honest debater."
- What it did not measure: Reading comprehension with gold labels only. The expertise gap is access to a text, not reasoning or judgment about what to research. Judges start with no prior opinion, so robustness to strong priors is untested. Experimental conditions were not perfectly controlled over the months of collection (stated by the authors). Judges got feedback after each debate, but how their skill changed over time is not reported as a result. No path dependence.
- What we borrow: (1) The consultancy-vs-debate contrast as a design for Q2. Our current loop is closer to consultancy, where the engine argues for its own result. An auditor that must argue the opposite verdict is the debate arm. (2) Two judge-error rules for the operator's audit overrides: no verdict after a single round, and no closing immediately after a swing. (3) A strictly proper score with a cost per extra round as a model for scoring the operator's budget steers (Q3). (4) Their ECE reporting format, used alongside Brier (Q1).
- Next step it suggests for us: Run a small paired comparison on past results, a single critic vs two opposed auditors, and log verdict reversals in each arm.

### Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers — Akbir Khan, John Hughes, Dan Valentine, Laura Ruis, Kshitij Sachan, Ansh Radhakrishnan, Edward Grefenstette, Samuel R. Bowman, Tim Rocktäschel, Ethan Perez, 2024, ICML 2024 (PMLR 235), arXiv:2402.06782 (v4, 25 Jul 2024), https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06782
- Read depth: full for the main text (sections 1–6, including "Discussion, Limitations & Conclusion"). Appendix A.1–A.2 (LLM-judge results, including where PGR is introduced) and the start of Appendix B (human-judge errors) were read. The rest of the appendices (implementation, prompts, transcripts) were not.
- What was built or tested: LLM debaters (GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Claude 2.1, Claude 1.3, Mixtral 8x7B) argue QuALITY-hard questions with a verified-quote tool. Protocols: debate, interactive debate, consultancy, plus a naive judge and an expert judge. Debaters are optimised for persuasiveness at inference time (best-of-N, critique-and-refinement), with no labels used. Human study: 30 recruited judges, cut to 21, with 953 test-phase judgments out of 6,476 total.
- How it was scored: Judge accuracy against labels. Persuasiveness measured without labels as win rate and Elo from a Swiss-style cross-play tournament of 20 debaters. Human calibration measured with Brier score. Selective accuracy vs coverage (dropping low-confidence answers). PGR (from Burns et al.) in the appendix.
- Headline result: Human judges reached 88% with debate, 78% with consultancy and 60% naive. LLM judges reached 76%, 54% and 48%. More persuasive debaters (higher Elo) gave higher judge accuracy. More persuasive consultants gave lower judge accuracy. Debate beat consultancy for humans (p = 0.001 static, p = 0.048 interactive). Letting judges interact did not raise accuracy. In static debate, rejecting human answers below 75% confidence kept 65% of questions at 94% accuracy. Judges were wrong in 22% of consultancies and 12% of debates. Error analysis says "LLM debaters are the bottleneck for higher judge accuracy."
- What it did not measure: The strong-weak gap is access to information only. The debaters were RLHF-tuned and so lean toward honesty, and the authors say it is unclear whether debate works on deceptive models. Single domain (stories of roughly 7,000–8,000 tokens). The judge's own value was not scored over time, beyond filtering annotators. No open-ended research direction. No path dependence.
- What we borrow: (1) Selective accuracy vs coverage as a Q1/Q3 analysis: is the engine (or the operator) right more often on the bets it gives the most extreme odds? (2) Their null result on interaction is a prior for Q3. Added questions from a human may not raise accuracy, so "value of each added question" needs its own pre-registered test rather than an assumption. (3) Persuasiveness without ground truth as a warning for the scout. A write-up optimised to convince the auditor is a consultant, and consultancy degrades as persuasiveness rises. (4) Position-swap runs to control for judge order bias, applied whenever the auditor compares two framings.
- Next step it suggests for us: Plot the ledger's hit rate against the stated-odds threshold (coverage curve) once there are 20 or more scored bets.

### On scalable oversight with weak LLMs judging strong LLMs — Zachary Kenton, Noah Y. Siegel, János Kramár, Jonah Brown-Cohen, Samuel Albanie, Jannis Bulian, Rishabh Agarwal, David Lindner, Yunhao Tang, Noah D. Goodman, Rohin Shah (Google DeepMind), 2024, arXiv:2407.04622 (v2, 12 Jul 2024), https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04622
- Read depth: full for the main text (sections 1–6, including Limitations and Future work). The appendices (per-task figures, Elo details, prompts, transcripts) were not read. Most results exist only as plots, so few exact accuracies can be quoted.
- What was built or tested: An inference-only benchmark with 9 tasks of 128 questions each, about 5 million model calls. Task types: extractive QA with an information gap (QuALITY, BoolQ, GPQA-extractive), closed QA with no gap (MMLU, GSM8KQA, PrOntoQA, TruthfulQA, GPQA) and multimodal (MMMU). Judges: Gemma7B, GPT-3.5, Gemini Pro 1.0, Gemini Pro 1.5. Gemini Pro 1.5 is the debater and consultant. Six protocols, including new "open" versions where the consultant or one debater chooses its own side.
- How it was scored: Weak-judge accuracy against labels. Permutation p-values between protocols. Elo of debaters. In the open protocols, judge accuracy split by whether the strong model chose correctly. Ablations on turns, best-of-N, few-shot, judge chain-of-thought, turn order and position.
- Headline result: Debate beat assigned-role consultancy on every task type. Debate did not clearly beat the judge answering directly. On closed tasks it beat direct QA only for the GPT-3.5 judge (p = 0.037); there was no significant difference for Pro 1.0 (p = 0.1); and it was worse when judge and debater were the same model. In open consultancy the weak judge is equally convinced whether or not the consultant chose correctly, which "runs the risk of amplifying the consultant's incorrect behavior." Open debate lessens this. Stronger debaters raised judge accuracy only modestly. Judge chain-of-thought tended to hurt. Judges from the Gemini family rated GPT-3.5 lower than expected, which the authors suggest may be self-preference bias.
- What it did not measure: No training (inference only), and no human judges. The models are RLHF-tuned toward honesty. The tasks are QA with gold labels, not open-ended research choices. No calibration of judges. No path dependence. No scoring of a human's added value.
- What we borrow: (1) "Open consultancy" describes our engine exactly: it picks its own bets and argues for them. The paper's finding that a weak judge then follows the strong model's errors is the main risk Q2 must measure. Open debate, where the auditor is assigned the opposing side, is the mitigation to test. (2) The self-preference caveat: our auditor is the same model family as the engine, so an audit pass is weaker evidence than an independent-family pass. Q2 should include a cross-family auditor arm if budget allows. (3) A direct-answer baseline. The auditor's verdict should be compared with the auditor answering the question cold, without seeing the engine's argument.
- Next step it suggests for us: For each audited result, also log the auditor's cold verdict before it reads the engine's write-up. The difference between the two verdicts measures how much the write-up persuaded it.

### Weak-to-Strong Generalization — Collin Burns, Pavel Izmailov, Jan Hendrik Kirchner, Bowen Baker, Leo Gao, Leopold Aschenbrenner, Yining Chen, Adrien Ecoffet, Manas Joglekar, Jan Leike, Ilya Sutskever, Jeff Wu (OpenAI), 2023, arXiv:2312.09390 (v1, 14 Dec 2023), https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.09390
- Read depth: full for the main text (sections 1–7, including the Limitations paragraph in section 3 and the Disanalogies discussion in section 6). Appendices (training details, easy-to-hard generalization, other weak supervisors) were not read.
- What was built or tested: Weak model supervisors (small GPT-4-family models fine-tuned on ground truth) label data, and strong pretrained models are fine-tuned on those labels. Three settings: 22 NLP classification tasks, chess puzzles, and ChatGPT reward modelling. Fixes tested: an auxiliary confidence loss, bootstrapping through intermediate sizes, and generative fine-tuning.
- How it was scored: Performance gap recovered, PGR = (weak-to-strong − weak) / (strong ceiling − weak). A PGR of 0 means the student only matches its supervisor; 1 means it reaches the ground-truth ceiling. Student-supervisor agreement is used to measure imitation of errors. GPT-4 and about half of the NLP tasks were held out while methods were developed.
- Headline result: PGR was almost always positive. NLP recovered more than 20% even with the weakest supervisors, and often more than 50% for the largest students. Reward modelling recovered only about 10% and almost never more than 20%. The confidence loss raised median NLP PGR from about 25% to nearly 80% (weakest supervisor, largest student). Students overfit to weak labels within one epoch. "Cheating" early stopping on ground truth gave about 5 points of PGR on reward modelling and 15 on NLP. The authors' summary: "we are still far from recovering the full capabilities of strong models."
- What it did not measure: There are no humans in the loop. The weak supervisor is a small model, and the authors list imitation salience and pretraining leakage as disanalogies. Labels exist for every task. There is no stated-odds calibration of the supervisor, and no open-ended direction-setting.
- What we borrow: (1) A PGR-style metric for Q3: (Brier(engine) − Brier(engine+human)) / (Brier(engine) − Brier(best available reference)). This is the fraction of the engine's forecasting gap the human closes, which is comparable across programs. (2) Student-supervisor agreement as an anchoring check. Measure how often the operator's odds sit within a few points of the engine's. That is a reason to keep recording his odds before he sees the engine's. (3) Their warning against "cheating" with ground truth, applied to the auditor. Tune it only on results that are not used to score it.
- Next step it suggests for us: Add a "human gap recovered" column to the ledger summary, defined and committed before the first Q3 analysis.

### AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion — Ryan Greenblatt, Buck Shlegeris, Kshitij Sachan, Fabien Roger (Redwood Research), 2023 (v5 23 Jul 2024; shortened version in ICML 2024), arXiv:2312.06942, https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06942
- Read depth: full for the main text (sections 1–6, including "Difficulties and Limitations of AI Control"). Table 7 values were read from the extracted table. Appendices (sampling method, suspicion thresholds, prompts) were not read.
- What was built or tested: The "APPS backdooring" testbed. Solve 5,000 programming problems (simulated from 1,500) with an untrusted strong model U (GPT-4), a trusted weak model T (GPT-3.5) and a 2% budget of trusted high-quality auditing H (implemented with GPT-4). Never submit a backdoored solution. A red team replaces U with attack policies. Blue-team protocols include upfront auditing, trusted monitoring, defer-to-trusted, untrusted monitoring (with synthetic backdoors to validate the monitor, plus redaction against collusion) and trusted editing.
- How it was scored: Safety is the probability that no backdoor is submitted across the whole scenario, against the red team's best attack. It is worst-case: "it's a safety failure if even one backdoor is submitted." Usefulness is correctness rescaled so GPT-3.5 = 0 and GPT-4 = 1 (raw 22% and 54%).
- Headline result: Upfront auditing gave 15% safety. Trusted monitoring gave 62% safety at 97% usefulness, and with 10% deferral 83% safety at 93% usefulness. Naive untrusted monitoring fell to 0% once the monitor lied. With synthetic-backdoor validation and redaction, untrusted monitoring reached 79% safety at 96% usefulness. Trusted editing at a 20% threshold reached 92% safety at 94% usefulness. The authors flag that no train/validation split was used (an overfitting risk). They also flag that auditing was nearly perfect here, whereas weak auditing is the harder future case.
- What it did not measure: The task is a single, uniform one with a known definition of failure (a backdoor plus an exploit). There is no open-ended research, and no question of whether a result is true or merely looks true. There is no calibration and no human operator (H is simulated with GPT-4). No path dependence.
- What we borrow: (1) Our auditor is an "untrusted monitor": the same model family as the engine. The paper's main lesson is to validate such a monitor on planted flaws before trusting its passes. For Q2, seed the audit stream with synthetic results containing known artefacts (a tie-breaking bug, a pooling error like the two already caught) and measure the auditor's recall and false-alarm rate. (2) Redaction against collusion. The auditor sees data, code and the claim, but not the engine's persuasive narrative. (3) The safety/usefulness frame for the operator's audit-override budget, where human attention is the scarce "H". (4) Defer-to-trusted as a routing rule. Results the auditor flags above a threshold go to the operator.
- Next step it suggests for us: Build a set of 5–10 planted-artefact results, mix them blind into the auditor's queue, and report the catch rate with the first audit log.

## Cluster synthesis

Scalable oversight research measures one thing well: whether a weaker judge can reach the right answer with help from a stronger, possibly unreliable system. The main results come from multiple-choice or reading-comprehension questions with gold labels. Sandwiching showed that human-model teams beat either alone. Debate beats single-advocate "consultancy" for human judges, and the advantage grows with debater skill on QuALITY. With LLM judges across many tasks, the debate advantage shrinks, and the evidence is weakest against simply answering directly. Weak-to-strong work supplies a clean metric (PGR). AI-control work supplies an adversarial way to evaluate monitors: validate an untrusted monitor on planted flaws.

What the field has not measured is oversight of research direction, where nobody knows the answer at the time and outcomes arrive later from experiments. It rarely scores calibration of stated odds before data. It does not track whether a particular human overseer adds value over time as they learn. It does not test whether starting conditions change the agenda. Most setups also let the strong model play only an assigned side, while a research agent chooses its own bets, which Kenton et al. show is the riskier "open" case.

Our testbed applies sandwiching-style comparisons (engine, human, combined) and adversarial auditing to open-ended bets resolved by pre-registered tests. It is far smaller and less controlled than these studies. With one operator, few bets and one model family, its numbers will be noisy: it is a case study piloting these measures, not a benchmark.
